30.1 Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you are the Chief Actuary for a rapidly growing general insurance company in Mumbai, writing policies for everything from individual motor vehicles to massive petrochemical refineries in Gujarat. Unlike life insurance, where the event (death) is certain to happen eventually and the payout is typically a fixed sum, general insurance deals with events that may never happen, but if they do, the financial impact can range from a minor dent in a bumper to billions of rupees in catastrophic damage.

Your challenge is two-fold: First, you must predict how often accidents will occur (frequency). Second, you must predict how much each accident will cost (severity). A minor fender-bender might cost ₹15,000, while a severe crash could result in third-party liability claims exceeding ₹50,000,000. For a portfolio of thousands of policies, you need to understand the distribution of the total aggregate claims. If a freak storm hits a coastal industrial zone, thousands of claims might pour in simultaneously. How much capital must the company hold to ensure it can pay all these claims without going bankrupt?

This chapter introduces the mathematical and statistical machinery used to answer these questions. We move away from deterministic cash flows and delve into the highly stochastic world of non-life insurance, building the framework to price these risks, set reserves, and structure reinsurance treaties.

30.2 General Insurance Products

General insurance (often called Property and Casualty, or P&C, in North America, and non-life insurance elsewhere) encompasses any insurance that is not life insurance. These products typically have a short duration (usually one year) and cover a wide array of risks.

30.2.1 Motor Insurance

Motor insurance is typically the largest portfolio for any general insurer.

  • Third-Party (TP) Liability: Covers bodily injury or property damage caused to third parties. In many jurisdictions, including India, this is mandatory by law. The liability can be theoretically unlimited, especially for bodily injury leading to death or permanent disability.
  • Own Damage (OD): Covers damage to the insured's own vehicle due to accidents, theft, natural disasters, etc. This is capped at the Insured Declared Value (IDV) of the vehicle.
  • Commercial Vehicle: A separate class covering trucks, buses, and taxis. The risk profile is significantly different due to higher mileage, heavier loads, and commercial usage.

30.2.2 Property Insurance

Property insurance covers physical assets against loss or damage.

  • Fire and Special Perils: The foundational property policy, covering fire, lightning, explosion, and often extended to cover natural perils like floods, earthquakes (STFI - Storm, Tempest, Flood, Inundation), and riots.
  • Erection All Risks (EAR) & Contractor's All Risks (CAR): Covers projects during the construction or erection phase.
  • Industrial All Risk (IAR): A comprehensive package policy for large industrial setups covering physical damage and often business interruption.

30.2.3 Marine Insurance

One of the oldest forms of insurance, marine insurance covers goods in transit.

  • Marine Cargo: Covers goods being transported by sea, air, rail, or road.
  • Marine Hull: Covers the vessel itself (ships, tankers, etc.) and its machinery.

30.2.4 Liability Insurance

Liability insurance protects against legal obligations to pay damages to third parties.

  • Professional Indemnity (PI): Covers professionals (doctors, architects, consultants) against negligence claims.
  • Public/Product Liability: Covers businesses against injury or damage claims arising from their operations or products.
  • Directors & Officers (D&O): Protects corporate directors and officers from personal losses if they are sued as a result of serving on a board.

30.2.5 Specialized Lines

  • Engineering: Covers machinery breakdown, electronic equipment, etc.
  • Crop/Agriculture: Protects farmers against yield losses due to natural perils (e.g., PMFBY in India).
  • Cyber Insurance: A rapidly growing line covering first-party and third-party risks associated with data breaches, ransomware, and IT infrastructure failures.

30.3 Frequency-Severity Model

The cornerstone of non-life actuarial mathematics is the collective risk model, which separates the aggregate claims into two independent components: the number of claims (frequency) and the size of each claim (severity).

Definition 30.1: Collective Risk Model
Let \( N \) be a random variable representing the total number of claims in a given period (frequency). Let \( X_1, X_2, \dots \) be random variables representing the amount of each individual claim (severities). The aggregate loss \( S \) is defined as: \[ S = \sum_{i=1}^{N} X_i \] with the convention that if \( N=0 \), then \( S=0 \).

Standard Assumptions:

  1. The severities \( X_1, X_2, \dots \) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables. Let \( X \) denote a generic random variable with this distribution.
  2. The frequency \( N \) is independent of the severities \( X_i \).

30.3.1 Moments of Aggregate Loss

Using the laws of total expectation and total variance, we can derive the moments of \( S \).

Theorem 30.1: Moments of Aggregate Claims
Under the assumptions of the collective risk model, the expected value and variance of the aggregate loss \( S \) are given by: \[ \E[S] = \E[N] \cdot \E[X] \] \[ \Var(S) = \E[N] \cdot \Var(X) + \Var(N) \cdot (\E[X])^2 \]
Proof
By the law of total expectation: \[ \E[S] = \E[\E[S|N]] = \E\left[\E\left[\sum_{i=1}^N X_i \middle| N\right]\right] \] Given \( N \), the sum is of \( N \) i.i.d. variables, so \( \E\left[\sum_{i=1}^N X_i \middle| N\right] = N \E[X] \). \[ \E[S] = \E[N \E[X]] = \E[N]\E[X] \] By the law of total variance: \[ \Var(S) = \E[\Var(S|N)] + \Var(\E[S|N]) \] Given \( N \), because \( X_i \) are independent, \( \Var(S|N) = N \Var(X) \). \[ \Var(S) = \E[N \Var(X)] + \Var(N \E[X]) \] \[ \Var(S) = \E[N]\Var(X) + (\E[X])^2 \Var(N) \]
Example 30.1: Compute aggregate claim distribution moments for Poisson(100)-Exponential(5000) model

Problem: A motor third-party insurance portfolio experiences a number of claims \( N \) following a Poisson distribution with mean 100 per year. The severity of each claim \( X \) follows an Exponential distribution with a mean of ₹5,000. Calculate the expected aggregate loss and the standard deviation of the aggregate loss.

Solution:

For the frequency \( N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda = 100) \):

  • \( \E[N] = 100 \)
  • \( \Var(N) = 100 \)

For the severity \( X \sim \text{Exponential}(\theta = 5000) \):

  • \( \E[X] = \theta = 5000 \)
  • \( \Var(X) = \theta^2 = 25,000,000 \)

Using Theorem 30.1, the expected aggregate loss is:

\[ \E[S] = \E[N]\E[X] = 100 \times 5000 = \text{₹}500,000 \]

The variance of the aggregate loss is:

\[ \Var(S) = \E[N]\Var(X) + \Var(N)(\E[X])^2 \] \[ \Var(S) = 100(25,000,000) + 100(5000)^2 = 2,500,000,000 + 2,500,000,000 = 5,000,000,000 \]

The standard deviation is:

\[ \sigma_S = \sqrt{5,000,000,000} \approx \text{₹}70,710.68 \]

30.3.2 Distributions for Frequency and Severity

Typical distributions used for the claim count \( N \):

  • Poisson Distribution: The standard assumption, where \( \Var(N) = \E[N] \).
  • Negative Binomial Distribution: Used when there is overdispersion, i.e., \( \Var(N) > \E[N] \). This often arises in a mixed Poisson model where the Poisson parameter itself is a random variable (e.g., Gamma distributed), representing heterogeneity in the portfolio.
  • Binomial Distribution: Used when the maximum number of claims is bounded, resulting in underdispersion \( \Var(N) < \E[N] \).

Typical distributions used for the claim severity \( X \):

  • Exponential: Light-tailed, mathematically tractable.
  • Gamma / Weibull: Flexible distributions for moderate tails.
  • Lognormal: Very common in property and motor lines for right-skewed data.
  • Pareto / Burr: Heavy-tailed distributions, crucial for liability and catastrophic risks.

30.4 Compound Poisson Process

If we view the claims arriving over time, we extend the static collective risk model to a dynamic stochastic process.

Definition 30.2: Compound Poisson Process
The aggregate claim process \( \{S(t)\}_{t \ge 0} \) is a compound Poisson process if: \[ S(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N(t)} X_i \] where: 1. \( \{N(t)\}_{t \ge 0} \) is a homogeneous Poisson process with rate \( \lambda > 0 \). 2. \( \{X_i\}_{i=1}^{\infty} \) are i.i.d. positive random variables representing claim severities. 3. The processes \( \{N(t)\} \) and \( \{X_i\} \) are independent.

At any fixed time \( t \), the number of claims \( N(t) \) follows a Poisson distribution with parameter \( \lambda t \). Therefore, \( S(t) \) has a compound Poisson distribution. The Moment Generating Function (MGF) provides a powerful way to analyze \( S(t) \).

Theorem 30.2: MGF of Compound Poisson Distribution
If \( S = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i \) where \( N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \) and \( X_i \) has MGF \( M_X(r) \), then the MGF of \( S \) is: \[ M_S(r) = \exp\left\{ \lambda (M_X(r) - 1) \right\} \]
Proof
By definition, \( M_S(r) = \E[e^{rS}] \). Using the law of total expectation conditioning on \( N \): \[ M_S(r) = \E[\E[e^{rS}|N]] = \E\left[\E\left[\exp\left(r\sum_{i=1}^N X_i\right) \middle| N\right]\right] \] Since the \( X_i \)'s are i.i.d.: \[ \E\left[\prod_{i=1}^N e^{r X_i} \middle| N\right] = (M_X(r))^N \] Now we take the expectation with respect to \( N \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda) \): \[ M_S(r) = \E[(M_X(r))^N] = P_N(M_X(r)) \] where \( P_N(z) \) is the probability generating function (PGF) of \( N \). For a Poisson distribution, \( P_N(z) = e^{\lambda(z-1)} \). Substituting \( z = M_X(r) \): \[ M_S(r) = \exp\left\{ \lambda(M_X(r) - 1) \right\} \]

Computing the exact distribution of \( S \) analytically is often impossible unless the severity distribution has a very specific form (e.g., if \( X \sim \text{Exponential} \), \( S \) follows a Compound Poisson-Exponential distribution, where the non-zero part is related to a Bessel function). In practice, we rely on approximations (like the Normal or translated Gamma approximation) or exact recursive algorithms.

30.5 Panjer's Recursion

When the severity distribution is defined on a discrete grid (or discretized from a continuous distribution), the exact distribution of the aggregate loss \( S \) can be computed using a highly efficient recursive algorithm known as Panjer's Recursion.

Assume that the severity random variable \( X \) can only take values on a grid \( 0, 1, 2, \dots, m \) with probabilities \( f_X(k) = P(X = k) \). (The unit could be e.g., ₹10,000).

The frequency distribution \( N \) must belong to the (a,b,0) class of distributions. This class includes the Poisson, Binomial, and Negative Binomial distributions, which satisfy the recursive relation:

\[ P(N = n) = \left( a + \frac{b}{n} \right) P(N = n-1), \quad \text{for } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]
  • Poisson(\( \lambda \)): \( a = 0, b = \lambda \)
  • Negative Binomial(\( r, p \)): \( a = 1-p, b = (r-1)(1-p) \)
  • Binomial(\( m, q \)): \( a = \frac{-q}{1-q}, b = \frac{(m+1)q}{1-q} \)
Theorem 30.3: Panjer's Recursion
Let \( f_S(x) = P(S = x) \). If \( N \) is in the (a,b,0) class and \( X \) is discrete on \( \{0, 1, 2, \dots\} \), then \( f_S(x) \) can be computed recursively as: \[ f_S(0) = \begin{cases} \exp(\lambda(f_X(0) - 1)) & \text{if } N \sim \text{Poisson} \\ (1 - a f_X(0))^{-(a+b)/a} & \text{if } N \sim \text{Negative Binomial} \\ (1 - a f_X(0))^{-(a+b)/a} & \text{if } N \sim \text{Binomial} \end{cases} \] For \( x = 1, 2, 3, \dots \): \[ f_S(x) = \frac{1}{1 - a f_X(0)} \sum_{j=1}^{x} \left( a + \frac{b \cdot j}{x} \right) f_X(j) f_S(x-j) \]
Example 30.2: Panjer's recursion for compound Poisson with discrete severity

Problem: The number of claims \( N \) follows a Poisson distribution with \( \lambda = 2 \). The claim severity \( X \) can take values ₹10,000 or ₹20,000 with probabilities \( P(X=1) = 0.6 \) and \( P(X=2) = 0.4 \) (where 1 unit = ₹10,000). Note that \( f_X(0) = 0 \). Calculate the probability that aggregate claims \( S \) equal exactly 0, 1, 2, and 3 units.

Solution:

For Poisson(\( \lambda=2 \)), the (a,b,0) parameters are \( a = 0 \) and \( b = \lambda = 2 \).

The severity probabilities are \( f_X(1) = 0.6, f_X(2) = 0.4 \), and \( f_X(x) = 0 \) for \( x > 2 \).

Step 1: Calculate \( f_S(0) \)

\[ f_S(0) = P(N=0) = e^{-\lambda} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135335 \]

Step 2: Apply the recursion for \( x=1 \)

\[ f_S(1) = \frac{1}{1 - 0} \sum_{j=1}^{1} \left( 0 + \frac{2j}{1} \right) f_X(j) f_S(1-j) = (2 \times 1) f_X(1) f_S(0) \] \[ f_S(1) = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.135335 = 1.2 \times 0.135335 \approx 0.162402 \]

Step 3: Apply the recursion for \( x=2 \)

\[ f_S(2) = \sum_{j=1}^{2} \left( \frac{2j}{2} \right) f_X(j) f_S(2-j) = \sum_{j=1}^{2} j \cdot f_X(j) f_S(2-j) \] \[ f_S(2) = 1 \cdot f_X(1) f_S(1) + 2 \cdot f_X(2) f_S(0) \] \[ f_S(2) = (0.6 \times 0.162402) + (2 \times 0.4 \times 0.135335) = 0.097441 + 0.108268 \approx 0.205709 \]

Step 4: Apply the recursion for \( x=3 \)

\[ f_S(3) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} \left( \frac{2j}{3} \right) f_X(j) f_S(3-j) \] \[ f_S(3) = \frac{2}{3}f_X(1)f_S(2) + \frac{4}{3}f_X(2)f_S(1) + \frac{6}{3}f_X(3)f_S(0) \] Since \( f_X(3) = 0 \): \[ f_S(3) = \left(\frac{2}{3} \times 0.6 \times 0.205709\right) + \left(\frac{4}{3} \times 0.4 \times 0.162402\right) \] \[ f_S(3) = 0.082284 + 0.086614 \approx 0.168898 \]

Thus, \( P(S = \text{₹}30,000) \approx 16.89\% \).

30.6 Coverage Modifications

Insurance contracts rarely cover the ground-up loss perfectly. Insurers implement mechanisms to reduce small frequent claims, cap maximum exposure, and align the insured's incentives with risk mitigation.

30.6.1 Deductibles

A deductible shifts the initial burden of a loss to the policyholder.

  • Ordinary Deductible (\( d \)): The insurer pays only the amount of loss exceeding \( d \).
    Let \( X \) be the ground-up loss. The amount paid per loss event, \( Y_L \), is: \[ Y_L = (X - d)_+ = \max(0, X - d) \] The expected cost per loss event is \( \E[Y_L] = \E[(X-d)_+] \). This includes zeros when claims are below the deductible. Note that this is different from the expected cost per payment, \( \E[Y_P] = \E[X - d | X > d] \).
  • Franchise Deductible: If the loss is less than or equal to \( d \), the insurer pays nothing. If the loss exceeds \( d \), the insurer pays the entire loss \( X \). \[ Y = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } X \le d \\ X & \text{if } X > d \end{cases} \]

30.6.2 Policy Limits

A policy limit caps the maximum amount the insurer will pay for a single event.

Let \( u \) be the policy limit. The amount paid by the insurer is \( Y = \min(X, u) = X \wedge u \).

The expected payment is the limited expected value, denoted \( \E[X \wedge u] \):

\[ \E[X \wedge u] = \int_{0}^{u} x f_X(x) \dd x + u [1 - F_X(u)] = \int_{0}^{u} (1 - F_X(x)) \dd x \]

30.6.3 Combined Modifications and Coinsurance

If a policy has a deductible \( d \), a limit \( u \) applied to the loss, and a coinsurance factor \( \alpha \) (where the insurer pays \( \alpha \) fraction of the payable claim), the payment is:

\[ Y = \alpha \cdot (\min(X, u) - d)_+ = \alpha \left[ (X \wedge u) - (X \wedge d) \right] \]

The expected payment per loss is:

\[ \E[Y_L] = \alpha \left( \E[X \wedge u] - \E[X \wedge d] \right) \]
Definition 30.3: Loss Elimination Ratio (LER)
The Loss Elimination Ratio is the fraction of expected total losses that are eliminated (not paid by the insurer) due to the deductible \( d \). \[ \text{LER} = \frac{\E[X \wedge d]}{\E[X]} \] It follows that the expected cost per loss with an ordinary deductible \( d \) is \( \E[Y_L] = \E[X] - \E[X \wedge d] = \E[X](1 - \text{LER}) \).
Example 30.3: Impact of a deductible on expected claims and loss elimination ratio

Problem: Ground-up losses for a property risk follow a Pareto distribution with shape parameter \( \alpha = 3 \) and scale parameter \( \theta = \text{₹}10,000 \). The insurer is considering introducing an ordinary deductible of \( d = \text{₹}2,000 \). Calculate the expected ground-up loss, the expected payout per loss under the new deductible, and the Loss Elimination Ratio.

Solution:

For a Pareto(\( \alpha, \theta \)) distribution, the CDF is \( F(x) = 1 - \left(\frac{\theta}{x+\theta}\right)^\alpha \) and the limited expected value is:

\[ \E[X \wedge d] = \frac{\theta}{\alpha-1} \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{\theta}{d+\theta}\right)^{\alpha-1} \right] \]

First, calculate the expected ground-up loss (\( d \to \infty \)):

\[ \E[X] = \frac{\theta}{\alpha - 1} = \frac{10,000}{3 - 1} = \text{₹}5,000 \]

Next, calculate the limited expected value at the deductible \( d = 2,000 \):

\[ \E[X \wedge 2000] = \frac{10,000}{2} \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{10,000}{2,000+10,000}\right)^{2} \right] = 5000 \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{10}{12}\right)^2 \right] \] \[ \E[X \wedge 2000] = 5000 \left[ 1 - \frac{100}{144} \right] = 5000 \left[ \frac{44}{144} \right] = 5000 \times 0.30555 = \text{₹}1,527.78 \]

The expected payout per loss is:

\[ \E[Y_L] = \E[X] - \E[X \wedge d] = 5000 - 1527.78 = \text{₹}3,472.22 \]

The Loss Elimination Ratio is:

\[ \text{LER} = \frac{\E[X \wedge d]}{\E[X]} = \frac{1527.78}{5000} = 30.56\% \]

By implementing a ₹2,000 deductible, the insurer eliminates 30.56% of the expected losses.

30.7 Claim Amount Distributions and Fitting

To use the models described above, we must fit probability distributions to historical claim data.

30.7.1 Common Parametric Families

  • Lognormal Distribution: \( X \sim \text{LN}(\mu, \sigma^2) \). The log of the claim is normally distributed. Very common for property claims. \[ f_X(x) = \frac{1}{x \sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(\ln x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]
  • Pareto Distribution: A power-law probability distribution useful for heavy-tailed phenomena like liability claims. \[ f_X(x) = \frac{\alpha \theta^\alpha}{(x+\theta)^{\alpha+1}}, \quad x>0 \]
  • Burr Distribution: A generalization of the Pareto providing a more flexible shape.
  • Weibull Distribution: Can have a heavy or light tail depending on the shape parameter. Useful in engineering and marine claims.

30.7.2 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

The standard method for parameter estimation. For a sample of \( n \) i.i.d. observed claims \( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n \), the likelihood function is \( L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f_X(x_i | \theta) \). We maximize the log-likelihood \( \ell(\theta) = \ln L(\theta) \).

Warning: Data Censoring and Truncation
Insurance data is rarely clean ground-up losses.
  • Truncation from below: If a deductible exists, claims below the deductible are simply not recorded. You must condition the likelihood on \( X > d \): \( f(x|X>d) = \frac{f(x)}{1-F(d)} \).
  • Censoring from above: If a policy limit \( u \) exists, claims exceeding \( u \) are recorded exactly as \( u \). For these claims, the contribution to the likelihood is the probability mass \( 1 - F(u) \), not the density \( f(u) \).
Ignoring truncation and censoring will result in severely biased parameter estimates.
Example 30.4: Fit Pareto to claim data using MLE

Problem: You observe a sample of 3 large liability claims: \( x_1 = 20,000 \), \( x_2 = 40,000 \), \( x_3 = 100,000 \). Assume these follow a single-parameter Pareto distribution where the scale parameter \( \theta \) is known to be \( 10,000 \) (so the support is \( x > 10,000 \), this is a shifted/Type I Pareto variation where \( f(x) = \alpha \theta^\alpha x^{-(\alpha+1)} \) for \( x > \theta \)). Find the MLE for \( \alpha \).

Solution:

The density is \( f(x) = \alpha \theta^\alpha x^{-(\alpha+1)} \) for \( x > \theta \). Here \( \theta = 10,000 \).

The likelihood function for \( n \) observations is:

\[ L(\alpha) = \prod_{i=1}^n \alpha \theta^\alpha x_i^{-(\alpha+1)} = \alpha^n \theta^{n\alpha} \prod_{i=1}^n x_i^{-(\alpha+1)} \]

The log-likelihood is:

\[ \ell(\alpha) = n \ln \alpha + n\alpha \ln \theta - (\alpha+1) \sum_{i=1}^n \ln x_i \]

Take the derivative with respect to \( \alpha \) and set to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \alpha} = \frac{n}{\alpha} + n \ln \theta - \sum_{i=1}^n \ln x_i = 0 \] \[ \frac{n}{\hat{\alpha}} = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln x_i - n \ln \theta = \sum_{i=1}^n (\ln x_i - \ln \theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ln\left(\frac{x_i}{\theta}\right) \] \[ \hat{\alpha} = \frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^n \ln(x_i / \theta)} \]

Plugging in our data (\( \theta = 10000 \)):

  • \( x_1 = 20000 \implies \ln(2) \approx 0.693 \)
  • \( x_2 = 40000 \implies \ln(4) \approx 1.386 \)
  • \( x_3 = 100000 \implies \ln(10) \approx 2.303 \)
\[ \sum \ln(x_i / \theta) = 0.693 + 1.386 + 2.303 = 4.382 \] \[ \hat{\alpha} = \frac{3}{4.382} \approx 0.684 \]

The estimated shape parameter is \( 0.684 \). Because \( \hat{\alpha} \le 1 \), this fitted distribution has an infinite mean, indicating an extremely heavy tail, which is common in un-capped liability data.

30.8 Large Claims and Heavy Tails

In life insurance, a claim cannot exceed the sum assured. In general insurance, particularly liability and natural catastrophe lines, a single event can devastate the portfolio. This behavior is captured by "heavy-tailed" distributions.

Definition 30.4: Mean Excess Function
The mean excess function (or mean residual life function), \( e(d) \), is the expected value of the claim amount exceeding a threshold \( d \), given that the claim is larger than \( d \). \[ e(d) = \E[X - d | X > d] = \frac{\int_d^\infty (1 - F_X(x)) \dd x}{1 - F_X(d)} \]

The behavior of \( e(d) \) as \( d \) increases is a fundamental diagnostic tool:

  • Constant \( e(d) \): Indicates an Exponential distribution (memoryless property). Light-tailed.
  • Decreasing \( e(d) \): Indicates a distribution with a tail lighter than exponential (e.g., Weibull with shape > 1, Uniform).
  • Increasing \( e(d) \): Indicates a heavy-tailed distribution (e.g., Pareto, Lognormal). The larger the claim gets, the larger the expected remaining claim size becomes.
Example 30.5: Mean excess plot to determine appropriate tail model

Problem: For a Pareto distribution with \( F(x) = 1 - \left(\frac{\theta}{x+\theta}\right)^\alpha \), derive the mean excess function \( e(d) \) and prove that it is linearly increasing in \( d \).

Solution:

First, the survival function is \( S(x) = 1 - F(x) = \left(\frac{\theta}{x+\theta}\right)^\alpha \).

The numerator of \( e(d) \) is the integral of the survival function from \( d \) to \( \infty \):

\[ \int_d^\infty \left(\frac{\theta}{x+\theta}\right)^\alpha \dd x = \theta^\alpha \left[ \frac{(x+\theta)^{-\alpha+1}}{-\alpha+1} \right]_d^\infty \] Assuming \( \alpha > 1 \) (so the integral converges): \[ \int_d^\infty S(x) \dd x = \frac{\theta^\alpha (d+\theta)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1} \]

The denominator is \( S(d) = \left(\frac{\theta}{d+\theta}\right)^\alpha = \theta^\alpha (d+\theta)^{-\alpha} \).

Dividing the numerator by the denominator:

\[ e(d) = \frac{\frac{\theta^\alpha (d+\theta)^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1}}{\theta^\alpha (d+\theta)^{-\alpha}} = \frac{d + \theta}{\alpha - 1} \]

Conclusion: \( e(d) = \frac{1}{\alpha-1} d + \frac{\theta}{\alpha-1} \). This is a straight line with a positive slope (since \( \alpha > 1 \)). A linearly increasing mean excess function is the signature of a Pareto tail.

30.9 Diagrams & Empirical Analysis

In practice, actuaries construct an empirical mean excess plot to choose distributions before formal fitting.

  • Figure 30.1 (Empirical Mean Excess Plot): A scatter plot of threshold \( d \) on the x-axis versus the sample mean of \( (x_i - d) \) for all \( x_i > d \). If the plot curves upwards, a lognormal or Weibull (shape < 1) is indicated. If it forms a straight line upwards, a Pareto distribution is heavily suggested. If it oscillates horizontally, an Exponential is appropriate.
  • Figure 30.2 (Aggregate Loss Histogram): A right-skewed bell-like curve resulting from Panjer's recursion or Monte Carlo simulation, showing the exact aggregate distribution \( S \). The 99th or 99.5th percentile of this curve determines the insurer's economic capital requirement.

30.10 Python Implementation: Panjer's Recursion

The following Python code implements Panjer's recursion for the exact aggregate loss distribution. It uses arbitrary precision or careful scaling in practice, but here is the textbook educational version.

import numpy as np

def panjer_recursion(a, b, f_X, p0_N, max_s):
    """
    Compute aggregate loss distribution using Panjer's recursion.
    
    Parameters:
    a, b  : Parameters of the (a,b,0) class frequency distribution
    f_X   : Array of severity probabilities, f_X[k] = P(X = k*unit)
            Must start with f_X[0] (which is typically 0).
    p0_N  : P(N=0) for the frequency distribution
    max_s : Maximum aggregate loss to compute
    
    Returns:
    f_S   : Array of aggregate loss probabilities up to max_s
    """
    f_S = np.zeros(max_s + 1)
    
    # Initialization step
    if f_X[0] == 0:
        f_S[0] = p0_N
    else:
        # Complex initialization if f_X(0) > 0 (handled per theorem 30.3)
        if a == 0: # Poisson
            lam = b
            f_S[0] = np.exp(lam * (f_X[0] - 1))
        else:
            f_S[0] = (1 - a * f_X[0]) ** (-(a + b) / a)
            
    # Recursion step
    for s in range(1, max_s + 1):
        sum_term = 0
        limit = min(s, len(f_X) - 1)
        for j in range(1, limit + 1):
            term = (a + b * j / s) * f_X[j] * f_S[s - j]
            sum_term += term
            
        f_S[s] = sum_term / (1 - a * f_X[0])
        
    return f_S

# Example 30.2 reproduction
# Poisson(2), f_X = [0, 0.6, 0.4]
a = 0
lam = 2
b = lam
p0_N = np.exp(-lam)
f_X = np.array([0.0, 0.6, 0.4])

f_S = panjer_recursion(a, b, f_X, p0_N, max_s=5)

print("Aggregate Loss Probabilities:")
for s, prob in enumerate(f_S):
    print(f"P(S = {s} units) = {prob:.6f}")
    
# Output:
# P(S = 0 units) = 0.135335
# P(S = 1 units) = 0.162402
# P(S = 2 units) = 0.205709

30.11 The Indian General Insurance Market

Industry Application: Evolution and Structure of the Indian GI Market

The Indian General Insurance market is a fascinating blend of state intervention, rapid privatization, and unique systemic risk pools. Fully nationalized in 1972, the sector was reopened to private players in 2000 through the IRDA Act.

Key Structural Elements

  • Public vs. Private: The market includes four dominant public sector insurers (New India, United India, Oriental, National) and dozens of private players. While public insurers historically dominated property lines due to public sector bank tie-ups, private insurers drive innovation in motor and health.
  • GIC Re: The General Insurance Corporation of India is the sole domestic reinsurer. By regulation, every direct insurer in India must cede a mandatory percentage (currently 4%, historically higher) of every policy written to GIC Re, known as the "obligatory cession." This retains premium within the country.
  • Motor Third-Party (TP) Pricing: Historically, Motor TP premiums were strictly tariff-controlled by the regulator (IRDAI) to keep them affordable. Because court-awarded liabilities are uncapped, this led to massive underwriting losses. To socialize this loss, the Indian Motor Third Party Insurance Pool (IMTPIP) was created, later replaced by the Declined Risk Pool, requiring all insurers to share the burden of commercial vehicle TP losses based on their market share.
  • Terrorism and Nuclear Pools: Following global catastrophic events, international reinsurers withdrew terrorism cover. India established the Indian Terrorism Risk Insurance Pool (TRIP), managed by GIC Re, where all non-life companies contribute to provide capacity for terrorism risks locally. A similar pool exists for Nuclear Liability.
  • Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY): One of the largest crop insurance schemes globally. It operates on an actuarial premium basis, where farmers pay a heavily subsidized flat rate (e.g., 2% for Kharif crops), and the state/central governments pay the balance actuarial premium. It requires complex weather and yield indices modeling.

30.12 Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Key Formulas

  • Collective Risk Model Moments:
    \( \E[S] = \E[N]\E[X] \)
    \( \Var(S) = \E[N]\Var(X) + \Var(N)(\E[X])^2 \)
  • Compound Poisson MGF:
    \( M_S(r) = \exp\{\lambda(M_X(r) - 1)\} \)
  • Panjer Recursion:
    \( f_S(x) = \frac{1}{1 - a f_X(0)} \sum_{j=1}^{x} \left( a + \frac{b \cdot j}{x} \right) f_X(j) f_S(x-j) \)
  • Limited Expected Value:
    \( \E[X \wedge u] = \int_0^u (1 - F_X(x)) \dd x \)
  • Loss Elimination Ratio (LER):
    \( \text{LER} = \frac{\E[X \wedge d]}{\E[X]} \)
  • Mean Excess Function:
    \( e(d) = \frac{\int_d^\infty (1 - F_X(x)) \dd x}{1 - F_X(d)} \)

Solutions to Selected Exercises

Solution to 30.1:

\( \E[N] = 50 \), \( \Var(N) = 50 \) (Poisson).

\( \E[X] = 100,000 \), \( \sigma_X = 50,000 \implies \Var(X) = 2,500,000,000 \).

\( \E[S] = 50 \times 100,000 = \text{₹}5,000,000 \).

\( \Var(S) = 50(2,500,000,000) + 50(100,000)^2 = 1.25 \times 10^{11} + 5 \times 10^{11} = 6.25 \times 10^{11} \).

\( \sigma_S = \sqrt{6.25 \times 10^{11}} \approx \text{₹}790,569 \).

Solution to 30.4:

For Exponential(\( \theta = 10,000 \)), \( F(x) = 1 - e^{-x/10000} \).

\( \E[X \wedge d] = \int_0^d (1 - F(x)) \dd x = \int_0^d e^{-x/\theta} \dd x = \theta(1 - e^{-d/\theta}) \).

At \( d = 2000 \), \( \E[X \wedge 2000] = 10000(1 - e^{-0.2}) \approx 10000(1 - 0.8187) = 1812.69 \).

LER = \( 1812.69 / 10000 = 18.13\% \).

Solution to 30.5:

Uniform on \( [0, 20000] \implies f(x) = 1/20000 \), \( F(x) = x/20000 \).

Limit \( u = 15000 \), deductible \( d = 5000 \).

\( \E[Y_L] = \E[X \wedge 15000] - \E[X \wedge 5000] \).

\( \E[X \wedge w] = \int_0^w (1 - x/20000) \dd x = [x - \frac{x^2}{40000}]_0^w = w - \frac{w^2}{40000} \).

\( \E[X \wedge 15000] = 15000 - \frac{225,000,000}{40000} = 15000 - 5625 = 9375 \).

\( \E[X \wedge 5000] = 5000 - \frac{25,000,000}{40000} = 5000 - 625 = 4375 \).

\( \E[Y_L] = 9375 - 4375 = \text{₹}5,000 \).

Solution to 30.7:

For Exponential(\( \theta \)), \( F(x) = 1 - e^{-x/\theta} \).

Numerator: \( \int_d^\infty e^{-x/\theta} \dd x = \theta e^{-d/\theta} \).

Denominator: \( 1 - F(d) = e^{-d/\theta} \).

\( e(d) = \frac{\theta e^{-d/\theta}}{e^{-d/\theta}} = \theta \). This proves the memoryless property; regardless of the deductible, the expected remaining claim size is always \( \theta \).

Solution to 30.8:

Given \( \E[N] = 4 \), \( \Var(N) = 12 \), \( \E[X] = 50 \), \( \Var(X) = 100 \).

\( \Var(S) = \E[N]\Var(X) + \Var(N)(\E[X])^2 \)

\( \Var(S) = 4(100) + 12(50^2) = 400 + 12(2500) = 400 + 30000 = 30,400 \).

Solution to 30.12:

Using the MGF property from Theorem 30.2:

\( M_{S_1}(r) = \exp\{\lambda_1 (M_{X_1}(r) - 1)\} \) and \( M_{S_2}(r) = \exp\{\lambda_2 (M_{X_2}(r) - 1)\} \).

Since \( S_1 \) and \( S_2 \) are independent, \( M_S(r) = M_{S_1}(r)M_{S_2}(r) \).

\( M_S(r) = \exp\{\lambda_1 M_{X_1}(r) - \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 M_{X_2}(r) - \lambda_2\} \).

Let \( \lambda = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \). The expression can be rewritten as:

\( M_S(r) = \exp\left\{ \lambda \left( \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda}M_{X_1}(r) + \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda}M_{X_2}(r) - 1 \right) \right\} \).

This is the MGF of a compound Poisson process with rate \( \lambda = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \) and a mixture severity distribution \( F_X(x) = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda}F_1(x) + \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda}F_2(x) \).

Chapter 27: Premiums and Reserves

Learning Objectives
  • Understand the Loss-at-Issue Random Variable and apply the equivalence principle to determine net premiums.
  • Incorporate expense loadings to price practical insurance products with gross premiums.
  • Calculate and analyze prospective and retrospective policy reserves, recognizing their equivalence under certain conditions.
  • Model the continuous evaluation of reserves via Thiele's Differential Equation and calculate asset shares.
  • Perform recursive reserve calculations and identify sources of surplus.
Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter builds heavily on Chapter 25 (Survival Models) and Chapter 26 (Life Insurance & Annuities). You must be comfortable with actuarial present values ($A_x$, $\ddot{a}_x$) and mortality tables before proceeding.

1. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you run a life insurance company. A 30-year-old client approaches you to buy a \$100,000 whole life policy. How much should you charge them every year? If you charge too little, the company will go bankrupt when claims arise. If you charge too much, the client will go to a competitor. Furthermore, since premiums are collected early (while the client is young and healthy) and claims are paid late, you must hold a portion of the premiums as a reserve. Determining the correct premium and managing the associated reserve over time is the core of actuarial pricing and valuation.

2. Formal Theory

2.1 Loss-at-Issue Random Variable

The fundamental building block for pricing life insurance is the loss-at-issue random variable, denoted $L_0$. It represents the present value of future benefits minus the present value of future premium payments, evaluated at the time the policy is issued.

Loss-at-Issue Random Variable ($L_0$)

For a policy issued to a life aged $x$, let $T_x$ be the future lifetime. For a continuous whole life policy with unit benefit and continuous premium rate $\bar{P}$:

\[ L_0 = v^{T_x} - \bar{P} \bar{a}_{\overline{T_x}|} \]

For a discrete whole life policy with unit benefit and annual premium $P$, paying at the end of the year of death, $L_0 = v^{K_x+1} - P \ddot{a}_{\overline{K_x+1}|}$, where $K_x$ is the curtate future lifetime.

2.2 Net Premiums

The equivalence principle states that the expected loss at issue must be zero. This yields the net premium, which covers exactly the expected benefit costs.

Equivalence Principle
\[ \E[L_0] = 0 \implies \E[\text{PV(Benefits)}] = \E[\text{PV(Premiums)}] \]

For a fully discrete whole life policy paying \$1: $A_x = P \ddot{a}_x \implies P = \frac{A_x}{\ddot{a}_x}$.

Alternatively, under the percentile premium principle, we set $P$ such that $\P(L_0 > 0) \le \alpha$ for some small probability $\alpha$.

2.3 Gross Premiums

In reality, insurers incur expenses (acquisition, maintenance, claims handling) and require a profit margin. The gross premium $G$ incorporates these loadings. Expenses are often modeled as:

  • Acquisition costs (e.g., commissions), occurring at issue ($t=0$).
  • Maintenance costs, occurring each year.
  • Claim handling expenses, occurring at death.
\[ G \cdot \ddot{a}_x = A_x + \text{APV(Expenses)} + \text{Profit Margin} \]

2.4 Net Premium Reserves

At time $t$, the expected present value of future benefits usually exceeds the expected present value of future net premiums. The difference is the reserve, $_tV$.

Prospective and Retrospective Reserves

Prospective Method: Looking forward, $_tV = \text{APV}(\text{Future Benefits}) - \text{APV}(\text{Future Premiums})$.

\[ _tV = A_{x+t} - P \ddot{a}_{x+t} \]

Retrospective Method: Looking backward, $_tV = \text{Accumulated}(\text{Past Premiums}) - \text{Accumulated}(\text{Past Benefits})$.

Under the equivalence principle, if actual experience matches assumptions exactly, prospective and retrospective reserves are strictly equal.

2.5 Gross Premium Reserves and Zillmerization

If we use gross premiums to calculate reserves, we get the Gross Premium Reserve (GPR). Since high acquisition costs occur in the first year, standard prospective reserves can be negative at $t=1$. Modified reserve methods like Zillmerization or Full Preliminary Term (FPT) alter the assumed premium stream to defer these acquisition costs, removing negative initial reserves.

2.6 Thiele's Differential Equation

For continuous policies, the reserve evolves smoothly over time, governed by Thiele's ODE.

Thiele's Differential Equation
\[ \dv{_tV}{t} = \delta \cdot {_tV} + P_t - e_t - (b_t - {_tV}) \mu_{x+t} \]

This states that the rate of change of the reserve is the interest earned ($\delta {_tV}$), plus premiums received ($P_t$), minus expenses paid ($e_t$), minus the expected net amount at risk $(b_t - {_tV})$ paid to those dying at time $t$.

2.7 Recursion for Reserves

For discrete policies, reserves at time $t$ and $t+1$ are linked via a recursion.

\[ (_tV + P_t)(1+i) = q_{x+t} b_{t+1} + p_{x+t} \cdot {_{t+1}V} \]

This allows insurers to do reserve roll-forwards and identify sources of surplus.

2.8 Reserve Analysis and Asset Shares

The asset share is the actual retrospective accumulation of funds per surviving policyholder, based on actual mortality, interest, and expenses rather than pricing assumptions. Comparing the asset share to the reserve tells the insurer if the policy is currently profitable and when it breaks even.

3. Worked Examples

Example 27.1: Net Premium for a 20-Year Endowment

Problem: Calculate the annual net premium for a INR 1,000,000 20-year endowment policy for a life aged 40. Given: $A_{40:\overline{20}|} = 0.38$, $\ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20}|} = 12.4$.

Solution: The net premium $P$ must satisfy $P \cdot \ddot{a}_{40:\overline{20}|} = 1,000,000 \cdot A_{40:\overline{20}|}$.

\[ P = \frac{1,000,000 \times 0.38}{12.4} = \frac{380,000}{12.4} = \text{INR } 30,645.16 \]
Example 27.2: Gross Premium with Expenses

Problem: Find the gross premium $G$ for a whole life policy of \$50,000 on (30). Given: $A_{30} = 0.15$, $\ddot{a}_{30} = 18.0$. Expenses are 50% of the first premium, 5% of renewal premiums, and \$100 per year (including the first year).

Solution: The actuarial present value of benefits is $50,000(0.15) = 7,500$.

APV of Expenses = $0.50G + 0.05G(\ddot{a}_{30} - 1) + 100\ddot{a}_{30} = 0.50G + 0.05G(17) + 1800 = 1.35G + 1800$.

Equating APV(Premiums) = APV(Benefits) + APV(Expenses):

\[ 18.0 G = 7,500 + 1.35 G + 1,800 \implies 16.65 G = 9,300 \implies G = \$558.56 \]
Example 27.3: Reserve Calculations (Prospective)

Problem: Calculate the prospective reserve at $t=10$ for a \$100,000 whole life policy issued to (35). The net premium is \$1,200. Given $A_{45} = 0.25$, $\ddot{a}_{45} = 16.0$.

Solution:

\[ _{10}V = 100,000 \cdot A_{45} - P \cdot \ddot{a}_{45} = 25,000 - 1,200(16) = 25,000 - 19,200 = \$5,800 \]
Example 27.4: Reserve Recursion Check

Problem: Given $_3V = 200$, $P = 50$, $i = 0.05$, $q_{x+3} = 0.01$, and benefit $b = 10,000$, find $_4V$.

Solution: Use the recursion $(_3V + P)(1+i) = q_{x+3} b + p_{x+3} {_4V}$.

\[ (200 + 50)(1.05) = 0.01(10000) + 0.99 \cdot {_4V} \] \[ 262.5 = 100 + 0.99 \cdot {_4V} \implies {_4V} = \frac{162.5}{0.99} = 164.14 \]
Example 27.5: Thiele's ODE (Constant Force)

Problem: Evaluate the reserve slope at $t=5$ for a continuous term policy. Given $_5V = 1000$, $P=200$, $b=50,000$, $\mu=0.004$, $\delta=0.05$, expenses $e=0$.

Solution:

\[ \dv{_5V}{t} = 0.05(1000) + 200 - 0 - (50000 - 1000)(0.004) \] \[ \dv{_5V}{t} = 50 + 200 - (49000)(0.004) = 250 - 196 = 54 \]

The reserve is growing at a rate of 54 per year.

4. Python Code

The following script uses Euler's method to numerically integrate Thiele's differential equation and produce a reserve profile.


import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def thiele_reserve(t_max, delta, mu_func, P, b, steps=1000):
    dt = t_max / steps
    V = np.zeros(steps + 1)
    t_vals = np.linspace(0, t_max, steps + 1)
    
    # Forward Euler method
    for i in range(steps):
        t = t_vals[i]
        mu = mu_func(t)
        # Thiele's ODE
        dV = (delta * V[i] + P - (b - V[i]) * mu) * dt
        V[i+1] = V[i] + dV
        
    return t_vals, V

# Policy Parameters
delta = 0.04
b = 100000
P = 1500
def mu(t): return 0.005 * np.exp(0.04 * t) # Gompertz law

t_vals, V_vals = thiele_reserve(30, delta, mu, P, b)
print(f"Reserve at t=15: ${V_vals[500]:.2f}")
print(f"Reserve at t=30: ${V_vals[-1]:.2f}")

6. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Common Mistakes
  • Confusing Initial and Terminal Reserves: Remember that the discrete recursion formula relates the initial reserve $(_tV + P)$ at the start of year $t+1$ to the terminal reserve $_{t+1}V$ at the end of the year.
  • Assuming Asset Share = Reserve: The asset share represents actual historical accumulation using real-world experience, while the prospective reserve relies on conservative valuation assumptions. They are not the same!
  • Ignoring Expense Timing: Acquisition expenses occur at $t=0$, while maintenance expenses usually occur at the beginning of each year. A common error is applying an annuity factor $\ddot{a}_x$ to acquisition expenses.

7. Industry Application

Industry Application: Source of Earnings (SoE) Analysis

In practice, life insurers perform a "Source of Earnings" (SoE) analysis to explain profit deviations. The recursive reserve formula is decomposed into distinct variances:

  • Mortality Gain: Expected Death Benefits - Actual Death Benefits
  • Interest Gain: Actual Investment Income - Expected Interest on Reserve
  • Expense Gain: Expected Expenses - Actual Expenses

By splitting profit into these drivers, management can identify whether their pricing, underwriting, or investments are underperforming.

8. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Net Premium (Equivalence): $\E[L_0] = 0 \implies P = \text{APV(FB)} / \ddot{a}_x$
  • Prospective Reserve: $_tV = \text{APV(FB)}_t - \text{APV(FP)}_t$
  • Retrospective Reserve: $_tV = \text{Accum(P)} - \text{Accum(FB)}$
  • Discrete Recursion: $(_tV + P_t)(1+i) = q_{x+t} b_{t+1} + p_{x+t} {_{t+1}V}$
  • Thiele's ODE: $\dv{_tV}{t} = \delta {_tV} + P_t - (b_t - {_tV}) \mu_{x+t}$

Solutions to Selected Exercises

Solution 1: $P = 1000 \frac{A_{40}}{\ddot{a}_{40}} = 1000 \frac{0.16132}{14.825} = 10.88$.

Solution 2: $(400 + 60)(1.06) = 0.02(10000) + 0.98 \cdot {_6V} \implies 487.6 = 200 + 0.98 \cdot {_6V} \implies {_6V} = 293.47$.

Solution 5: Since $\E[L_0]=0$, $\text{APV(Benefits)} = \text{APV(Premiums)}$. Splitting both into $[0, t]$ and $(t, \infty)$, we get: $\text{Past Benefits} + v^t \cdot _tp_x \text{Future Benefits} = \text{Past Premiums} + v^t \cdot _tp_x \text{Future Premiums}$. Rearranging and dividing by the discount and survival factors gives the retrospective formula equal to the prospective formula.

Solution 6: $G \cdot 7.5 = 100000(0.05) + [0.40G + 200 + 0.05G(7.5 - 1)] \implies 7.5G = 5000 + 0.40G + 200 + 0.325G \implies 6.775G = 5200 \implies G = 767.53$.

Solution 8: Asset Share $= \frac{(P - e)(1+i) - q_x \cdot b}{p_x} = \frac{(500 - 200)(1.07) - 0.005(50000)}{0.995} = \frac{321 - 250}{0.995} = 71.36$.

Solution 12: Since $P = A_x / \ddot{a}_x$ and $\ddot{a}_{\overline{K+1}|} = \frac{1 - v^{K+1}}{d}$, we have $L_0 = v^{K+1} (1 + \frac{P}{d}) - \frac{P}{d}$. Thus $\Var(L_0) = (1 + \frac{P}{d})^2 \Var(v^{K+1}) = (1 + \frac{P}{d})^2 (^2A_x - (A_x)^2)$.

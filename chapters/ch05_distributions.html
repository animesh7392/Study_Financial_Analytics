Chapter 5: Probability Distributions

Learning Objectives

  • Categorize and apply fundamental discrete and continuous probability distributions used in finance, insurance, and quantitative analysis.
  • Understand the generative mechanisms, parameterizations, and mathematical properties (PMF/PDF, CDF, MGF, moments) of core distributions.
  • Master the \((a,b,0)\) and \((a,b,1)\) recursive classes and mixture distributions for complex risk modeling.
  • Fit theoretical distributions to empirical data using the Method of Moments (MoM) and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE).
  • Assess goodness-of-fit using visual diagnostics and formal statistical tests (Chi-square, KS, Anderson-Darling).

Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter builds directly on the foundational concepts established in Chapter 4: Probability Theory. The distributions introduced here will serve as the essential toolkit for statistical inference in Chapter 6: Mathematical Statistics and for risk modeling in subsequent chapters on Actuarial Science and Quantitative Finance.

5.1 Intuition & Motivation

Consider the role of a Chief Risk Officer (CRO) at a major insurer or a quant at a hedge fund. The world presents them with empirical data: a series of daily stock returns, a history of hurricane claims, or the time-to-default of corporate bonds. Raw data is messy, noisy, and challenging to extrapolate. To make predictions, price derivatives, or reserve capital for extreme losses, we must map this empirical reality onto theoretical probability distributions.

Probability distributions are idealized mathematical models of randomness. They act as "data generating engines." If we believe stock prices evolve multiplicatively, we might reach for the Lognormal distribution. If we are modeling the number of car accidents in a year, the Poisson or Negative Binomial distributions are natural candidates. By carefully selecting and calibrating (fitting) a distribution to data, we transition from observing historical artifacts to making forward-looking, probabilistic statements about risk.

5.2 Discrete Distributions

Discrete distributions model random variables that take on a countable set of values (e.g., \(0, 1, 2, \dots\)).

Bernoulli Distribution

A random variable \(X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)\) represents a single trial with success probability \(p\) and failure probability \(q = 1-p\).

  • PMF: \(\P(X=x) = p^x (1-p)^{1-x}\) for \(x \in \{0,1\}\)
  • Mean: \(p\)
  • Variance: \(p(1-p)\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = 1-p + pe^t\)
Binomial Distribution

A random variable \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)\) represents the number of successes in \(n\) independent Bernoulli trials.

  • PMF: \(\P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\) for \(k = 0, 1, \dots, n\)
  • Mean: \(np\)
  • Variance: \(np(1-p)\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = (1-p + pe^t)^n\)
Poisson Distribution

A random variable \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) models the number of events occurring in a fixed interval, where events occur with a known constant mean rate \(\lambda\) and independently of the time since the last event. It is the limit of the Binomial distribution as \(n \to \infty\), \(p \to 0\) with \(np = \lambda\).

  • PMF: \(\P(X=k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\lambda\)
  • Variance: \(\lambda\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \exp(\lambda(e^t - 1))\)
Geometric Distribution

A random variable \(X \sim \text{Geometric}(p)\) models the number of failures before the first success in a sequence of independent Bernoulli trials.

  • PMF: \(\P(X=k) = (1-p)^k p\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\frac{1-p}{p}\)
  • Variance: \(\frac{1-p}{p^2}\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \frac{p}{1 - (1-p)e^t}\)

Note: The geometric distribution is the only discrete distribution with the memoryless property: \(\P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = \P(X > t)\).

Negative Binomial Distribution

A random variable \(X \sim \text{NB}(r, p)\) models the number of failures before the \(r\)-th success. It can be viewed as a sum of \(r\) independent Geometric random variables.

  • PMF: \(\P(X=k) = \binom{k+r-1}{k} p^r (1-p)^k\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\)
  • Mean: \(\frac{r(1-p)}{p}\)
  • Variance: \(\frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}\)
  • MGF: \(M_X(t) = \left( \frac{p}{1 - (1-p)e^t} \right)^r\)

Because its variance is strictly greater than its mean, the Negative Binomial is extensively used in insurance to model overdispersed claim counts, often conceptualized as a Gamma-mixed Poisson distribution.

Hypergeometric Distribution

Models the number of successes in a sequence of \(n\) draws from a finite population without replacement. If the population is of size \(N\) containing \(K\) successes, then \(X \sim \text{Hypergeometric}(N, K, n)\) has PMF \(\P(X=k) = \frac{\binom{K}{k}\binom{N-K}{n-k}}{\binom{N}{n}}\).

5.3 Continuous Distributions

Continuous distributions model random variables defined on an interval of the real line.

Uniform Distribution

\(X \sim \text{Uniform}(a, b)\)

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}\) for \(x \in [a, b]\)
  • CDF: \(F(x) = \frac{x-a}{b-a}\)
  • Mean: \(\frac{a+b}{2}\)
  • Variance: \(\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}\)

Crucial for simulation (via the probability integral transform).

Exponential Distribution

\(X \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)\) models waiting times between Poisson events.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) for \(x > 0\)
  • CDF: \(F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}\)
  • Mean: \(1/\lambda\)
  • Variance: \(1/\lambda^2\)

The only continuous distribution with the memoryless property.

Gamma Distribution

\(X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) where \(\alpha\) is the shape and \(\beta\) is the rate (sometimes parameterized by scale \(\theta = 1/\beta\)). It models the waiting time until the \(\alpha\)-th Poisson event.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}\) for \(x > 0\)
  • Mean: \(\alpha / \beta\)
  • Variance: \(\alpha / \beta^2\)
Normal (Gaussian) Distribution

\(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\). The cornerstone of statistics due to the Central Limit Theorem.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\)
  • CDF: Denoted by \(\Phi(z)\) for the standard normal \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\)
  • Mean: \(\mu\)
  • Variance: \(\sigma^2\)

Follows the 68-95-99.7 empirical rule.

Lognormal Distribution

If \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), then \(Y = e^X \sim \text{Lognormal}(\mu, \sigma^2)\).

  • PDF: \(f(y) = \frac{1}{y\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{(\ln y - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\) for \(y > 0\)
  • Mean: \(\exp(\mu + \sigma^2/2)\)
  • Variance: \([\exp(\sigma^2) - 1] \exp(2\mu + \sigma^2)\)

Widely used for stock prices and asset values, as it ensures positive values and arises from multiplicative processes (geometric Brownian motion).

Beta Distribution

\(X \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) is defined on \([0,1]\). Often used as a prior distribution for probabilities or proportions.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}\)
  • Mean: \(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\)
Weibull Distribution

\(X \sim \text{Weibull}(k, \lambda)\) with shape \(k\) and scale \(\lambda\). Used in survival analysis and reliability.

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{k}{\lambda} \left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k-1} e^{-(x/\lambda)^k}\) for \(x \ge 0\)
  • Hazard Rate: \(h(x) = \frac{k}{\lambda} \left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k-1}\). Decreasing for \(k < 1\), constant (Exponential) for \(k=1\), increasing for \(k > 1\).
Pareto Distribution

Models heavy-tailed phenomena (the "80/20 rule"). If \(X \sim \text{Pareto}(\alpha, x_m)\), where \(x_m\) is the minimum possible value and \(\alpha\) is the shape parameter (tail index).

  • PDF: \(f(x) = \frac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}}\) for \(x \ge x_m\)
  • CDF: \(F(x) = 1 - \left(\frac{x_m}{x}\right)^\alpha\)
  • Mean: \(\frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1}\) (only exists if \(\alpha > 1\))

Distributions Related to the Normal

Student-t Distribution: With \(\nu\) degrees of freedom. Heavy tails for small \(\nu\), approaches Normal as \(\nu \to \infty\). Used in small-sample inference.
Chi-square (\(\chi^2\)) Distribution: Sum of \(\nu\) squared independent standard normals. Used in goodness-of-fit and variance testing.
F-distribution: Ratio of two scaled \(\chi^2\) variables. Used in ANOVA and testing equality of variances.

Extreme Value Distributions

GEV (Generalized Extreme Value): Parameters \(\mu, \sigma, \xi\). Used for modeling block maxima (e.g., maximum daily loss in a year).
Generalized Pareto (GPD): Parameters \(\sigma, \xi\). Used in Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) methods to model excesses over a high threshold.

5.4 Special Topics in Distribution Theory

5.4.1 Mixture Distributions

A mixture distribution arises when a population is composed of distinct subpopulations. For instance, an insurance portfolio might contain "good risks" and "bad risks". Let \(f_1(x)\) and \(f_2(x)\) be the PDFs of the subpopulations, and \(w\) be the proportion of the first subpopulation. The mixture PDF is:

\[ f_M(x) = w f_1(x) + (1-w) f_2(x) \]

The \(k\)-th moment of a mixture is the weighted sum of the \(k\)-th moments of the components. Mixture models are often fitted using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm.

5.4.2 The \((a, b, 0)\) Class of Discrete Distributions

A profound result by Harry Panjer shows that the three fundamental counting distributions—Poisson, Binomial, and Negative Binomial—are the only discrete distributions (supported on \(\N_0\)) that satisfy the recursive relationship:

\[ \frac{p_k}{p_{k-1}} = a + \frac{b}{k} \quad \text{for } k = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]
Theorem 5.1: Panjer's (a,b,0) Classification

A counting distribution \(\{p_k\}_{k=0}^{\infty}\) with \(p_0 > 0\) belongs to the \((a,b,0)\) class if and only if it is one of: Poisson, Binomial, or Negative Binomial. These are the only distributions satisfying the recursion \(\frac{p_k}{p_{k-1}} = a + \frac{b}{k}\) for \(k = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) with constants \(a, b\).

Reference: Panjer, H.H. (1981). "Recursive Evaluation of a Family of Compound Distributions." ASTIN Bulletin, 12(1), 22–26.

Where \(p_k = \P(X=k)\). The sign of \(a\) determines the distribution:

  • \(a = 0\): Poisson (\(b = \lambda\))
  • \(a < 0\): Binomial (\(a = \frac{-p}{1-p}\))
  • \(a > 0\): Negative Binomial (\(a = 1-p\))

5.4.3 The \((a, b, 1)\) Class

Sometimes empirical data has too many zeros (e.g., probability of zero claims is very high) or zero is impossible (truncated). The \((a,b,1)\) class modifies the recursion to start at \(k=2\):

\[ \frac{p_k}{p_{k-1}} = a + \frac{b}{k} \quad \text{for } k = 2, 3, 4, \dots \]

This class includes Zero-Truncated and Zero-Modified versions of the \((a,b,0)\) distributions, plus the Logarithmic distribution (which only exists for \(a > 0, b = -a\)).

5.5 Distribution Relationships

Probability distributions are deeply interconnected. A conceptual relationship chart would illustrate the following pathways:

  • Summation Limits: Bernoulli \(\xrightarrow{\text{Sum}}\) Binomial \(\xrightarrow{n \to \infty, p \to 0}\) Poisson.
  • Central Limit Theorem: Sums of almost any independent distributions approach the Normal distribution.
  • Time/Count Duality: If times between events are Exponential, the count of events is Poisson. If times are Gamma(k, \(\lambda\)), the count is related to Poisson.
  • Transformations: Normal \(\xrightarrow{\exp}\) Lognormal. Normal \(\xrightarrow{\text{Square}}\) Chi-Square \(\xrightarrow{\text{Ratio}}\) F-Distribution.
  • Special Cases: Exponential is a special case of Gamma (\(\alpha=1\)) and Weibull (\(k=1\)).

5.6 Fitting Methods & Goodness of Fit

Method of Moments (MoM)

Equate sample moments to theoretical moments and solve for parameters. If a distribution has \(k\) parameters, we use the first \(k\) moments.

\[ \E[X] = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i, \quad \E[X^2] = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^2 \]

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Find the parameters \(\theta\) that maximize the probability (likelihood) of observing the given sample \(\mathbf{x}\).

\[ \hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_{\theta} \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid \mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_{\theta} \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta) \]

Usually, we maximize the log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta) = \sum \ln f(x_i \mid \theta)\).

Goodness of Fit Diagnostics

  • Q-Q Plots: Plot empirical quantiles vs theoretical quantiles. A straight line indicates a good fit.
  • Chi-Square Test: Bins data and compares observed counts to expected counts.
  • Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) Test: Based on the maximum absolute distance between the empirical CDF and theoretical CDF.
  • Anderson-Darling (AD) Test: Similar to KS but gives more weight to the tails, crucial for financial/actuarial data.

5.7 Worked Examples

Example 5.1: Fitting a Poisson Distribution

Problem: An insurer observes the following number of claims per policyholder over one year for a sample of 1000 policies:

0 claims: 810, 1 claim: 165, 2 claims: 22, 3 claims: 3, 4+ claims: 0.

Fit a Poisson distribution using MLE and calculate the expected frequencies.

Solution:

1. Calculate sample mean (MLE for Poisson \(\lambda\)):
\(\hat{\lambda} = \frac{0(810) + 1(165) + 2(22) + 3(3)}{1000} = \frac{218}{1000} = 0.218\)

2. Calculate expected probabilities \(\P(X=k) = \frac{e^{-0.218}(0.218)^k}{k!}\):
\(p_0 = e^{-0.218} \approx 0.8041\)
\(p_1 = 0.8041 \times 0.218 \approx 0.1753\)
\(p_2 = 0.1753 \times \frac{0.218}{2} \approx 0.0191\)
\(p_3 = 0.0191 \times \frac{0.218}{3} \approx 0.0014\)
\(p_{4+} = 1 - (p_0+p_1+p_2+p_3) \approx 0.0001\)

3. Expected frequencies (multiply by 1000):
0: 804.1, 1: 175.3, 2: 19.1, 3: 1.4, 4+: 0.1.

The fit looks visually reasonable, though a formal Chi-square test would confirm it.

Example 5.2: Lognormal Model for VaR

Problem: A stock currently trades at $100. Its price in one year, \(S_1\), is modeled as Lognormal. The continuously compounded return has mean \(\mu = 0.08\) and volatility \(\sigma = 0.20\). Find the probability that the stock drops below $90, and find the 5% Value at Risk (VaR) relative to the initial price.

Solution:

1. We know \(\ln(S_1/S_0) \sim \mathcal{N}(0.08, 0.20^2)\). Therefore \(\ln S_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\ln(100) + 0.08, 0.20^2) = \mathcal{N}(4.685, 0.04)\).

2. Probability \(S_1 < 90\):
\(\P(S_1 < 90) = \P(\ln S_1 < \ln 90) = \P(\ln S_1 < 4.4998)\)
Convert to standard normal: \(Z = \frac{4.4998 - 4.685}{0.20} = -0.926\)
\(\Phi(-0.926) \approx 17.7\%\)

3. 5% VaR: We need the 5th percentile of \(S_1\). The 5th percentile of \(Z\) is \(-1.645\).
\(\ln S_{5\%} = 4.685 + (-1.645)(0.20) = 4.356\)
\(S_{5\%} = e^{4.356} = \$77.94\)
VaR = Initial Value - 5th Percentile = $100 - $77.94 = $22.06.

Example 5.3: Pareto Limited Expected Value

Problem: Losses follow a Pareto distribution with \(\alpha = 3\) and \(x_m = 1000\). An insurance policy has a deductible of 0 and a policy limit of \(L = 5000\). Calculate the expected loss to the insurer.

Solution:

We need the Limited Expected Value (LEV), denoted \(\E[X \wedge L]\). For a Pareto distribution, the formula is:

\[ \E[X \wedge L] = \frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha - 1} \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{x_m}{L}\right)^{\alpha-1} \right] \]

\(\E[X \wedge 5000] = \frac{3(1000)}{2} \left[ 1 - \left(\frac{1000}{5000}\right)^2 \right]\)
\(= 1500 \left[ 1 - (0.2)^2 \right] = 1500 [1 - 0.04] = 1500(0.96) = 1440\).

Without the limit, the expected loss would be 1500. The limit reduces the expected payout by 60.

Example 5.4: Verifying the (a,b,0) Class

Problem: Prove that the Binomial\((n,p)\) distribution belongs to the \((a,b,0)\) class and find \(a\) and \(b\).

Solution:

The PMF is \(p_k = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}\).

Calculate the ratio \(p_k / p_{k-1}\):

\[ \frac{p_k}{p_{k-1}} = \frac{\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}}{\frac{n!}{(k-1)!(n-k+1)!} p^{k-1} (1-p)^{n-k+1}} \] \[ = \frac{(k-1)!}{k!} \frac{(n-k+1)!}{(n-k)!} \frac{p}{1-p} \] \[ = \frac{n-k+1}{k} \frac{p}{1-p} \] \[ = \left( \frac{n+1}{k} - 1 \right) \frac{p}{1-p} \] \[ = \frac{-p}{1-p} + \frac{(n+1)p/(1-p)}{k} \]

This matches \(a + b/k\), where \(a = \frac{-p}{1-p}\) and \(b = \frac{(n+1)p}{1-p}\).

Example 5.5: Moments of a Mixture

Problem: A hedge fund's daily returns are modeled as a mixture of two normal distributions. 90% of days are "calm" (\(\mu_1=0.001, \sigma_1=0.01\)) and 10% are "volatile" (\(\mu_2=-0.005, \sigma_2=0.05\)). Find the mean and variance of the daily return \(X\).

Solution:

Let \(w=0.9\). Mean \(\E[X] = w\mu_1 + (1-w)\mu_2 = 0.9(0.001) + 0.1(-0.005) = 0.0009 - 0.0005 = 0.0004\).

To find variance, first find \(\E[X^2]\) for the mixture:
\(\E[X_1^2] = \sigma_1^2 + \mu_1^2 = 0.0001 + 0.000001 = 0.000101\)
\(\E[X_2^2] = \sigma_2^2 + \mu_2^2 = 0.0025 + 0.000025 = 0.002525\)
\(\E[X^2] = 0.9(0.000101) + 0.1(0.002525) = 0.0000909 + 0.0002525 = 0.0003434\)

\(\Var(X) = \E[X^2] - (\E[X])^2 = 0.0003434 - (0.0004)^2 = 0.0003434 - 0.00000016 = 0.00034324\).

Standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.00034324} \approx 0.0185\).

5.8 Python Code: Distribution Fitting and AIC

In practice, quants fit multiple distributions to empirical data and compare them using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Lower AIC indicates a better model.


import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as stats
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Generate some synthetic "heavy-tailed" loss data
np.random.seed(42)
true_data = stats.lognorm.rvs(s=0.8, scale=np.exp(5), size=1000)

# Define distributions to fit
dist_names = ['expon', 'lognorm', 'weibull_min']

for dist_name in dist_names:
    dist = getattr(stats, dist_name)
    # Fit the distribution to the data via MLE
    params = dist.fit(true_data)
    
    # Calculate Log-Likelihood
    ll = np.sum(dist.logpdf(true_data, *params))
    k = len(params) # Number of parameters
    
    # Calculate AIC
    aic = 2 * k - 2 * ll
    
    print(f"Distribution: {dist_name.ljust(12)} | AIC: {aic:.2f} | Params: {params}")

# Expected Output structure:
# Distribution: expon        | AIC: 16738.54 | Params: (...)
# Distribution: lognorm      | AIC: 13915.22 | Params: (...)
# Distribution: weibull_min  | AIC: 14522.10 | Params: (...)
# Result: Lognormal has the lowest AIC, making it the best fit.
Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Assuming Normality: Financial returns and insurance losses are almost never normally distributed. They exhibit fat tails and skewness. Assuming normality severely underestimates extreme risks (tail risk).
  • Confusing Gamma and Exponential: Exponential models the time to the first event. Gamma models the time to the \(\alpha\)-th event.
  • Misunderstanding Memorylessness: Only the Exponential (continuous) and Geometric (discrete) distributions are memoryless. A Weibull distribution with \(k>1\) implies that items "age" and become more likely to fail.
Industry Application: Actuarial Reserving & Catastrophe Modeling

Property & Casualty (P&C) actuaries constantly model the Aggregate Loss \(S = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i\). The count \(N\) is modeled using a discrete distribution (often Negative Binomial to capture volatility in storm frequency). The severity \(X_i\) is modeled using continuous, heavy-tailed distributions like Lognormal, Gamma, or Pareto. By combining these via Monte Carlo simulation or Panjer recursion, insurers calculate the capital reserves needed to survive a 1-in-200-year catastrophe, fulfilling regulatory frameworks like Solvency II.

Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Poisson: \(\E[X] = \lambda\), \(\Var(X) = \lambda\)
  • Negative Binomial: \(\E[X] = \frac{r(1-p)}{p}\), \(\Var(X) = \frac{r(1-p)}{p^2}\)
  • Exponential: \(\E[X] = 1/\lambda\), \(\Var(X) = 1/\lambda^2\)
  • Gamma: \(\E[X] = \alpha/\beta\), \(\Var(X) = \alpha/\beta^2\)
  • Lognormal: \(\E[X] = \exp(\mu + \sigma^2/2)\)
  • Panjer \((a,b,0)\) recursion: \(\frac{p_k}{p_{k-1}} = a + \frac{b}{k}\)

Solutions to Selected Exercises

Solution to 1: \(\P(X=2) = \frac{e^{-4}4^2}{2!} = \frac{16e^{-4}}{2} = 8e^{-4} \approx 0.1465\).
Solution to 2: The median \(m\) satisfies \(1 - e^{-0.2m} = 0.5 \implies e^{-0.2m} = 0.5 \implies m = \frac{-\ln(0.5)}{0.2} \approx 3.466\).
Solution to 3: \(X \sim \text{Binomial}(10, 0.05)\). \(\P(X=2) = \binom{10}{2} (0.05)^2 (0.95)^8 = 45(0.0025)(0.6634) \approx 0.0746\).
Solution to 6: \(\P(X > 1000) = 1 - F(1000) = \left(\frac{500}{1000}\right)^4 = (0.5)^4 = 0.0625\).
Solution to 8: \(S_1 \sim \text{Lognormal}(\ln(50) + 0.05, 0.3^2)\). \(\E[S_1] = \exp(\ln(50) + 0.05 + 0.3^2/2) = 50 \exp(0.05 + 0.045) = 50 \exp(0.095) \approx 54.98\).
Solution to 9: Mean \(=\alpha/\beta = 10\). Var \(=\alpha/\beta^2 = 20\). Divide mean by variance: \( (\alpha/\beta) / (\alpha/\beta^2) = \beta = 10/20 = 0.5 \). Then \(\alpha = 10\beta = 5\).
Solution to 11: \(\mathcal{L} = \prod \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\). Log-likelihood: \(\ell = -\frac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\ln(\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\). Setting \(\pdv{\ell}{\mu} = 0\) gives \(\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i\). Setting \(\pdv{\ell}{(\sigma^2)} = 0\) gives \(\widehat{\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{n}\sum(x_i-\hat{\mu})^2\).
Solution to 12: \(\P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = \frac{\P(X > s+t \text{ and } X > s)}{\P(X > s)} = \frac{\P(X > s+t)}{\P(X > s)} = \frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}} = e^{-\lambda t} = \P(X > t)\).

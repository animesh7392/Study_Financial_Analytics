Chapter 6: Statistics and Inference

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand and evaluate point estimators using properties such as unbiasedness, consistency, and efficiency.
  • Derive and apply Maximum Likelihood Estimators (MLE) and the Method of Moments (MoM) for various distributions.
  • Construct and interpret confidence intervals and perform hypothesis tests using both classical and Bayesian methodologies.
  • Apply generalized linear models (GLMs), regression techniques, and regularization to insurance and financial data.
  • Utilize non-parametric and computational methods like the Bootstrap, EM Algorithm, and cross-validation for complex estimation and model selection.

2. Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter extensively builds upon the concepts from Chapter 4 (Probability Theory) and Chapter 5 (Statistical Distributions). You must be comfortable with expectation, variance, conditional probability, normal distributions, and the exponential family. The tools developed here will be crucial for Chapter 7 (Stochastic Processes) and subsequent chapters on actuarial modeling and risk management.

3. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you are the Chief Actuary of a burgeoning auto insurance company in India. You have observed a sample of $n = 1,000$ claim severities over the past year. Your task is to set the premium for the next year. If you assume the claims follow a Pareto distribution, what is the best guess for the parameters? How confident are you in this guess? What if the true distribution is Lognormal?

Statistical inference provides the mathematical machinery to move from finite, observed data to conclusions about the underlying, unobserved population. We want to estimate parameters, quantify our uncertainty (confidence intervals), test assumptions (hypothesis testing), and predict future observations (regression). In actuarial science, precision in inference is the difference between profitability and insolvency.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Point Estimation

A point estimator $\hat{\theta}$ is a statistic (a function of the data $X_1, \dots, X_n$) that guesses the true parameter $\theta$.

Definition 6.1 (Properties of Estimators)
  • Bias: $\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}) = \E[\hat{\theta}] - \theta$. An estimator is unbiased if $\E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta$.
  • Consistency: $\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{p} \theta$ as $n \to \infty$. (Convergence in probability).
  • Mean Squared Error (MSE): $\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \E[(\hat{\theta} - \theta)^2] = \text{Bias}(\hat{\theta})^2 + \Var(\hat{\theta})$.
  • Sufficiency: A statistic $T(X)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if the conditional distribution of the data given $T(X)$ does not depend on $\theta$.
Theorem 6.1 (Cramér-Rao Lower Bound)
For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$, under mild regularity conditions: \[ \Var(\hat{\theta}) \ge \frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)} \] where $\mathcal{I}(\theta) = -n \E\left[ \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \theta^2} \log f(X; \theta) \right]$ is the Fisher Information.

4.2 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

The likelihood function $L(\theta; \mathbf{x}) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i; \theta)$ represents the probability of observing the data given $\theta$. The MLE $\hat{\theta}_{MLE}$ maximizes this function, or equivalently, the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta; \mathbf{x}) = \log L(\theta; \mathbf{x})$.

The score function is $S(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell(\theta)}{\partial \theta}$. Setting $S(\theta) = 0$ gives the MLE.

Asymptotic Normality of MLE
As $n \to \infty$, $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{MLE} - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \mathcal{I}(\theta)^{-1})$. This provides large-sample confidence intervals.

4.3 Method of Moments (MoM)

MoM equates the population moments to the sample moments: $\E[X^k] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^k$. Solving the resulting system of equations yields the MoM estimators. While simpler than MLE, MoM estimators are generally less efficient.

4.4 Interval Estimation

A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval (CI) is a random interval $[L(X), U(X)]$ such that $\P(L(X) \le \theta \le U(X)) = 1-\alpha$.

For large samples using MLE asymptotics, a $95\%$ CI is approximately $\hat{\theta} \pm 1.96 \sqrt{\widehat{\Var}(\hat{\theta})}$.

4.5 Hypothesis Testing

We test a null hypothesis $H_0$ against an alternative $H_1$.

  • Type I Error ($\alpha$): Rejecting $H_0$ when it is true (Significance level).
  • Type II Error ($\beta$): Failing to reject $H_0$ when $H_1$ is true.
  • Power ($1-\beta$): Probability of correctly rejecting $H_0$.
  • p-value: The probability of observing a test statistic as extreme as the one computed, assuming $H_0$ is true.
Neyman-Pearson Lemma
The most powerful test of size $\alpha$ for testing a simple $H_0: \theta = \theta_0$ versus a simple $H_1: \theta = \theta_1$ is given by the likelihood ratio test: reject $H_0$ if $\frac{L(\theta_1; x)}{L(\theta_0; x)} > k$.

4.6 Goodness of Fit Tests

Tests like the Pearson Chi-Square ($\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$), Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test, and Anderson-Darling test are used to check if data follows a hypothesized distribution.

4.7 Bayesian Inference

Bayes' Theorem updates our prior belief $p(\theta)$ using the data likelihood $p(x|\theta)$ to obtain the posterior $p(\theta|x)$:

\[ p(\theta | x) = \frac{p(x | \theta) p(\theta)}{\int p(x | \theta) p(\theta) \dd\theta} \propto p(x | \theta) p(\theta) \]

A conjugate prior is one where the posterior belongs to the same distribution family as the prior (e.g., Gamma prior with Poisson likelihood yields a Gamma posterior). The posterior mean is often a credibility-weighted average of the prior mean and sample mean.

4.8 Bootstrap Methods

When analytical standard errors are untractable, we use the bootstrap. The Non-parametric Bootstrap resamples with replacement from the original data. We compute the statistic on $B$ bootstrap samples (e.g., $B=10,000$) to estimate its sampling distribution and confidence intervals (percentile or BCa).

4.9 EM Algorithm

The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm is an iterative method to find MLEs in models with latent variables or incomplete data (like finite mixture models).

  • E-step: Calculate expected value of log-likelihood with respect to latent variables given current parameters.
  • M-step: Maximize the expected log-likelihood to update parameters.

4.10 Regression and GLMs

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS): For $Y = X\beta + \epsilon$, the estimator is $\hat{\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^TY$.

Generalized Linear Models (GLMs): Extend linear regression to exponential family distributions using a link function $g(\mu_i) = X_i \beta$.

  • Poisson Regression (Log link): $Y_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_i)$, $\log(\mu_i) = x_i^T\beta$.
  • Logistic Regression (Logit link): $Y_i \sim \text{Binomial}(1, p_i)$, $\log(\frac{p_i}{1-p_i}) = x_i^T\beta$.

4.11 Model Selection

Used to penalize complexity and avoid overfitting.

  • $\text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ell(\hat{\theta})$
  • $\text{BIC} = k\ln(n) - 2\ell(\hat{\theta})$
  • Cross-Validation: Splits data into $K$ folds to evaluate out-of-sample prediction error.

5. Worked Examples

Example 6.1: MLE for Exponential Distribution

Problem: Suppose insurance claim inter-arrival times $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are i.i.d. $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$. Derive the MLE for $\lambda$ and a $95\%$ asymptotic CI. A sample of $n=100$ claims has $\bar{x} = 2.5$ days. Calculate the estimate and CI.

Solution:

  1. The PDF is $f(x_i; \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}$.
  2. Likelihood: $L(\lambda) = \prod_{i=1}^n \lambda e^{-\lambda x_i} = \lambda^n e^{-\lambda \sum x_i}$.
  3. Log-likelihood: $\ell(\lambda) = n \log(\lambda) - \lambda \sum x_i$.
  4. Score function: $\frac{d\ell}{d\lambda} = \frac{n}{\lambda} - \sum x_i = 0 \implies \hat{\lambda} = \frac{n}{\sum x_i} = \frac{1}{\bar{x}}$.
  5. Fisher Information: $I(\lambda) = -E\left[ -\frac{n}{\lambda^2} \right] = \frac{n}{\lambda^2}$.
  6. Asymptotic Variance: $\Var(\hat{\lambda}) \approx \frac{1}{I(\lambda)} = \frac{\lambda^2}{n}$.
  7. Numerical Estimate: $\hat{\lambda} = 1/2.5 = 0.40$ claims/day.
  8. Std Error: $SE = \hat{\lambda}/\sqrt{n} = 0.40 / 10 = 0.04$.
  9. 95% CI: $0.40 \pm 1.96(0.04) \implies [0.3216, 0.4784]$.
Example 6.2: Bayesian Inference with Gamma-Poisson

Problem: A policyholder's annual claim count $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$. The prior for $\lambda$ is $\text{Gamma}(\alpha=2, \beta=1)$ (mean = 2). We observe 3 years of data: 4, 3, 5 claims. Find the posterior distribution and posterior mean.

Solution:

Prior: $p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta\lambda} = \lambda^1 e^{-\lambda}$.

Likelihood: $p(X|\lambda) \propto \prod \lambda^{x_i} e^{-\lambda} = \lambda^{\sum x_i} e^{-n\lambda} = \lambda^{12} e^{-3\lambda}$.

Posterior: $p(\lambda|X) \propto p(X|\lambda)p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{12+1} e^{-(3+1)\lambda} = \lambda^{13} e^{-4\lambda}$.

This is a $\text{Gamma}(\alpha' = 14, \beta' = 4)$ distribution.

Posterior Mean: $\frac{\alpha'}{\beta'} = \frac{14}{4} = 3.5$.

Notice the credibility formulation: $\E[\lambda|X] = Z \bar{X} + (1-Z)\mu_{prior}$, where $Z = \frac{n}{n+\beta} = \frac{3}{4}$. So, $\frac{3}{4}(4) + \frac{1}{4}(2) = 3 + 0.5 = 3.5$.

Example 6.3: GLM Poisson Regression

Problem: Fit a Poisson regression to claim counts using age as a predictor. $\log(\E[Y_i]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Age}_i$. Assume estimates are $\hat{\beta}_0 = -2.0, \hat{\beta}_1 = 0.05$. What is the expected claim count for a 30-year-old?

Solution:

$\log(\mu_{30}) = -2.0 + 0.05(30) = -2.0 + 1.5 = -0.5$.

$\mu_{30} = e^{-0.5} \approx 0.606$ claims.

Example 6.4: Bootstrap Confidence Interval

Problem: We have a sample of 5 claim severities in INR: 5k, 12k, 18k, 45k, 120k. The median is 18k. Explain how to find a 95% bootstrap CI for the median.

Solution:

  1. Sample 5 items with replacement from the data. E.g., {5k, 18k, 18k, 120k, 120k}. Median = 18k.
  2. Repeat this process $B=10,000$ times to obtain $B$ bootstrap medians.
  3. Sort the $B$ medians. The 95% percentile CI is the interval from the 2.5th percentile to the 97.5th percentile of these sorted values.
Example 6.5: Model Selection using AIC

Problem: We fit Exponential (k=1), Gamma (k=2), and Lognormal (k=2) models to loss data. The log-likelihoods are -500, -480, and -475 respectively. Which is best according to AIC?

Solution:

$\text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ell$. Lower is better.

  • Exponential: $2(1) - 2(-500) = 2 + 1000 = 1002$.
  • Gamma: $2(2) - 2(-480) = 4 + 960 = 964$.
  • Lognormal: $2(2) - 2(-475) = 4 + 950 = 954$.

The Lognormal model minimizes the AIC and is therefore preferred.

6. Python Code

Here we reproduce the GLM Poisson regression example (similar to Example 6.3) using Python's `statsmodels`.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm

# Simulate some insurance data
np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 1000
age = np.random.uniform(18, 70, n_samples)
# True relationship: log(lambda) = -2 + 0.03 * age
true_lambda = np.exp(-2 + 0.03 * age)
claims = np.random.poisson(true_lambda)

# Prepare data for statsmodels
X = sm.add_constant(age)  # Adds the intercept term beta_0
model = sm.GLM(claims, X, family=sm.families.Poisson())
results = model.fit()

print(results.summary())

# Predict for a 30-year-old
new_data = sm.add_constant(np.array([30]), has_constant='add')
pred = results.predict(new_data)
print(f"Expected claims for 30-year-old: {pred[0]:.4f}")

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Confidence Intervals: Saying "there is a 95% probability that the true parameter lies in this interval." Incorrect! The parameter is fixed; the interval is random. It means 95% of intervals generated this way will contain the true parameter.
  • p-values: Interpreting the p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true. A p-value is $\P(Data | H_0)$, not $\P(H_0 | Data)$.
  • Correlation vs Causation in Regression: A significant $\beta$ coefficient only implies statistical association, not that changing $X$ will cause $Y$ to change.
9. Industry Application Box: GLMs in Ratemaking

In property & casualty (P&C) insurance, actuaries calculate base premiums and adjust them using rating factors (e.g., age, car type, zip code). The industry standard for determining these adjustments is the Generalized Linear Model (GLM).

Typically, frequency (how often claims happen) is modeled with a Poisson GLM (log link), and severity (how large claims are) is modeled with a Gamma GLM (log link). By using the log link, the resulting premium formula becomes multiplicative: $\text{Premium} = \text{Base Rate} \times \text{Factor}_1 \times \text{Factor}_2$. This multiplicative structure is intuitive for underwriters and easily deployable in IT systems.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • MSE: $\text{MSE}(\hat{\theta}) = \text{Bias}^2 + \Var(\hat{\theta})$
  • Score Function: $S(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell(\theta)}{\partial \theta}$
  • Fisher Information: $\mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\E[S'(\theta)]$
  • Asymptotic CI for MLE: $\hat{\theta} \pm Z_{1-\alpha/2} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{I}(\hat{\theta})}}$
  • Bayes' Theorem: $\text{Posterior} \propto \text{Likelihood} \times \text{Prior}$
  • AIC: $2k - 2\ell(\hat{\theta})$

Solutions to Selected Exercises

Solution to 1: No. $\E[\hat{\theta}^2] = \Var(\hat{\theta}) + (\E[\hat{\theta}])^2 = \Var(\hat{\theta}) + \theta^2$. As long as $\Var(\hat{\theta}) > 0$, $\E[\hat{\theta}^2] > \theta^2$, making it biased.
Solution to 2: $\E[X] = \lambda$. Equating to sample mean gives $\hat{\lambda}_{MoM} = \bar{X}$.
Solution to 5: $\text{AIC} = 2k - 2\ell = 2(3) - 2(-120) = 6 + 240 = 246$.
Solution to 6: MLE is $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2$. Since $\E[\hat{\sigma}^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, it is biased. (The unbiased estimator divides by $n-1$).
Solution to 7: $\ell(p) = \sum x_i \log p + (n-\sum x_i) \log(1-p)$. $S(p) = \frac{\sum x_i}{p} - \frac{n-\sum x_i}{1-p}$. Fisher info $\mathcal{I}(p) = \frac{n}{p(1-p)}$. CRLB is $\frac{p(1-p)}{n}$.
Solution to 12: $\hat{\beta} = (X^TX)^{-1}X^TY = (X^TX)^{-1}X^T(X\beta + \epsilon) = \beta + (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\epsilon$. Taking expectation conditionally on $X$, $\E[\hat{\beta}|X] = \beta + (X^TX)^{-1}X^T\E[\epsilon|X] = \beta + 0 = \beta$.
Solution to 13: Ridge uses $\sum \beta_j^2 \le t$, resulting in a circular constraint region. LASSO uses $\sum |\beta_j| \le t$, a diamond-shaped region. The elliptical contours of the sum of squared errors are more likely to hit the corners of the LASSO diamond (where some $\beta_j=0$) than the smooth edges of the Ridge circle.

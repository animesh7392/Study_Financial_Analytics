Chapter 28: Multiple Life and Multiple Decrement Models

Learning Objectives
  • Understand and calculate survival probabilities, annuity values, and insurance premiums for joint-life and last-survivor statuses.
  • Evaluate reversionary annuities and contingent insurances involving two or more lives.
  • Incorporate dependence between lives using common shock models and copulas.
  • Construct and utilize multiple decrement tables, linking associated single decrement rates to multiple decrement rates.
  • Model complex insurance products, such as disability income insurance, using continuous-time multi-state Markov models and the Kolmogorov forward equations.
  • Extend the traditional single-life framework to multi-state frameworks using Thiele's differential equations.
Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter builds heavily on survival models (Chapter 24), life insurances and annuities (Chapters 25 and 26), and net premiums and reserves (Chapter 27). We expand our state space from simple "alive/dead" frameworks to multi-dimensional state spaces that can track multiple individuals (e.g., married couples) or multiple causes of transition (e.g., death, disability, withdrawal). The principles laid out here are foundational for pension mathematics covered in Chapter 29.

28.1 Intuition & Motivation

Consider a pension plan that promises to pay $50,000 annually to a retired employee as long as they live, and then, upon their death, to pay $25,000 annually to their surviving spouse for the remainder of the spouse's life. How does the pension fund price this benefit? Traditional single-life mortality models are insufficient because the payouts depend on the survival of both individuals. We need a framework to handle multiple lives.

Now, consider a different scenario: an employer provides life insurance, but an employee can also terminate employment (withdraw) or become disabled. An employee only receives the death benefit if they die while employed. The rate at which employees die is influenced by the rate at which they withdraw or become disabled. This scenario requires a multiple decrement model, where a single state (active employment) is subject to several competing risks.

Finally, what if disabled employees can recover and return to work? The flow between states is no longer unidirectional. This requires a generalized multi-state Markov model, which forms the theoretical bedrock of modern actuarial and quantitative finance approaches to life contingencies, disability insurance, and long-term care.

28.2 Joint Life Status

Let \((x)\) and \((y)\) denote two individuals aged \(x\) and \(y\), respectively. Their future lifetimes are random variables denoted by \(T(x)\) and \(T(y)\). The joint-life status, denoted by \(xy\), is a status that survives as long as both \((x)\) and \((y)\) are alive. In other words, the joint-life status fails upon the first death.

Definition 28.1: Joint-Life Future Lifetime

The future lifetime of the joint-life status \(xy\), denoted \(T(xy)\), is the time until the first death among \((x)\) and \((y)\): \[ T(xy) = \min(T(x), T(y)) \]

The probability that the joint-life status survives \(t\) years is denoted by \({}_t p_{xy}\). Assuming that the future lifetimes \(T(x)\) and \(T(y)\) are independent, the joint survival probability is simply the product of the individual survival probabilities:

\[ {}_t p_{xy} = \P(T(xy) > t) = \P(\min(T(x), T(y)) > t) = \P(T(x) > t \text{ and } T(y) > t) \] Under the assumption of independence: \[ {}_t p_{xy} = \P(T(x) > t) \cdot \P(T(y) > t) = {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y \]

Similarly, the probability that the joint-life status fails within \(t\) years is:

\[ {}_t q_{xy} = 1 - {}_t p_{xy} = 1 - {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y \] Note that \({}_t q_{xy}\) is not equal to \({}_t q_x \cdot {}_t q_y\).

Theorem 28.1: Force of Mortality for Joint-Life Status

Under the assumption of independent future lifetimes, the force of mortality for the joint-life status, \(\mu_{x+t, y+t}\) (or simply \(\mu_{xy}(t)\)), is the sum of the individual forces of mortality:

\[ \mu_{xy}(t) = \mu_{x+t} + \mu_{y+t} \]

Proof: By definition, the force of mortality is the negative derivative of the log-survival function: \[ \mu_{xy}(t) = -\dv{}{t} \ln {}_t p_{xy} \] Using the independence assumption \({}_t p_{xy} = {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y\): \[ \mu_{xy}(t) = -\dv{}{t} \ln ({}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y) = -\dv{}{t} (\ln {}_t p_x + \ln {}_t p_y) \] \[ \mu_{xy}(t) = -\dv{}{t} \ln {}_t p_x - \dv{}{t} \ln {}_t p_y = \mu_{x+t} + \mu_{y+t} \]

Joint-Life Insurance and Annuities

Because the joint-life status operates mathematically just like a single life with a modified survival function, we can price insurances and annuities on the joint-life status using the same formulas from Chapters 25 and 26, substituting \(xy\) for \(x\).

For a continuous whole life insurance paying 1 upon the failure of the joint-life status (the first death):

\[ \bar{A}_{xy} = \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_{xy} \cdot \mu_{xy}(t) \dd t \]

For a continuous life annuity paying 1 per year while the joint-life status survives (both are alive):

\[ \bar{a}_{xy} = \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_{xy} \dd t \]

The standard relationships hold, for example:

\[ \bar{A}_{xy} = 1 - \delta \bar{a}_{xy} \] \[ \ddot{a}_{xy} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty v^k \cdot {}_k p_{xy} \]
Example 28.1: Joint Life Annuity for a Married Couple

Consider a married couple, (65) and (62). You are given that their future lifetimes are independent, and mortality follows a constant force of mortality with \(\mu_{65+t} = 0.02\) and \(\mu_{62+t} = 0.015\) for all \(t \ge 0\). The continuous interest rate is \(\delta = 0.04\).

Calculate the present value of a continuous joint-life annuity of INR 100,000 per year, \(\bar{a}_{65:62}\).

Solution:

The force of mortality for the joint-life status is:

\[ \mu_{xy}(t) = \mu_{65} + \mu_{62} = 0.02 + 0.015 = 0.035 \]

Because the joint force of mortality is constant, the joint-life status follows an exponential distribution with parameter \(\mu = 0.035\). For a constant force of mortality and constant force of interest, the continuous annuity value is:

\[ \bar{a}_{xy} = \frac{1}{\mu_{xy} + \delta} = \frac{1}{0.035 + 0.04} = \frac{1}{0.075} = 13.3333 \]

The present value of the annuity is:

\[ PV = 100,000 \times 13.3333 = \text{INR } 1,333,333.33 \]

28.3 Last Survivor Status

The last-survivor status, denoted by \(\overline{xy}\), is a status that survives as long as at least one of \((x)\) or \((y)\) is alive. It fails upon the second death.

Definition 28.2: Last-Survivor Future Lifetime

The future lifetime of the last-survivor status \(\overline{xy}\), denoted \(T(\overline{xy})\), is the time until the last death among \((x)\) and \((y)\): \[ T(\overline{xy}) = \max(T(x), T(y)) \]

Using basic probability principles, for any two random variables \(A\) and \(B\), \(\max(A, B) + \min(A, B) = A + B\). Thus:

\[ T(\overline{xy}) + T(xy) = T(x) + T(y) \]

The probability that the last-survivor status survives \(t\) years (i.e., at least one is alive) can be derived using the inclusion-exclusion principle:

\[ \P(\max(T(x), T(y)) > t) = \P(\{T(x) > t\} \cup \{T(y) > t\}) \] \[ = \P(T(x) > t) + \P(T(y) > t) - \P(T(x) > t \cap T(y) > t) \]

Therefore, we have the fundamental survival relationship:

\[ {}_t p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_t p_x + {}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy} \]

This implies that any expected present value function that is linear in the survival probabilities will satisfy the same relationship. For example, for continuous annuities:

\[ \bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_{\overline{xy}} \dd t = \int_0^\infty v^t ({}_t p_x + {}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy}) \dd t \] \[ \bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \bar{a}_x + \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy} \]

Similarly, for insurances:

\[ A_{\overline{xy}} = A_x + A_y - A_{xy} \]
Common Mistake

Do not attempt to calculate \(\mu_{\overline{xy}}(t)\) as \(\mu_x(t) + \mu_y(t) - \mu_{xy}(t)\). The inclusion-exclusion relationship holds for probabilities, expectations, and present values, but not for the force of mortality. The force of mortality for the last-survivor status is complex and depends on whether one or both lives are still surviving at time \(t\).

Example 28.2: Last Survivor Insurance Premium

An insurance company offers a continuous whole life insurance policy to a married couple, (50) and (55), paying USD 500,000 upon the second death. You are given:

  • \(\bar{A}_{50} = 0.30\)
  • \(\bar{A}_{55} = 0.38\)
  • \(\bar{A}_{50:55} = 0.45\)

Calculate the net single premium (NSP) for this policy.

Solution:

The policy pays on the failure of the last-survivor status \(\overline{50:55}\). We need to find \(\bar{A}_{\overline{50:55}}\).

Using the fundamental relationship:

\[ \bar{A}_{\overline{50:55}} = \bar{A}_{50} + \bar{A}_{55} - \bar{A}_{50:55} \] \[ \bar{A}_{\overline{50:55}} = 0.30 + 0.38 - 0.45 = 0.23 \]

The Net Single Premium is:

\[ NSP = 500,000 \times \bar{A}_{\overline{50:55}} = 500,000 \times 0.23 = \text{USD } 115,000 \]

28.4 Reversionary Annuities

A reversionary annuity is an annuity that begins paying upon the failure of one status (e.g., the death of \((x)\)) and continues paying as long as a second status (e.g., \((y)\)) survives. If the second status fails before the first, the annuity never makes any payments.

The most common application is a widow(er)'s pension: a pension is payable to a spouse \((y)\) after the death of the primary earner \((x)\).

The present value of a continuous reversionary annuity payable to \((y)\) after the death of \((x)\) is denoted \(\bar{a}_{x|y}\).

Consider the payment at time \(t\). A payment of 1 is made if and only if \((y)\) is alive at time \(t\) AND \((x)\) is dead at time \(t\). The probability of this joint event (assuming independence) is:

\[ \P(T(y) > t \text{ and } T(x) \le t) = {}_t p_y \cdot (1 - {}_t p_x) = {}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy} \]

Taking the present value of all such future payments:

\[ \bar{a}_{x|y} = \int_0^\infty v^t ({}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy}) \dd t = \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_y \dd t - \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_{xy} \dd t \] \[ \bar{a}_{x|y} = \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy} \]

The intuition is clear: a reversionary annuity pays when \((y)\) is alive, except when \((x)\) is also alive. So it is the value of a life annuity on \((y)\), minus the value of the annuity during the period when both are alive.

Example 28.3: Reversionary Annuity for Pension Provision

A retiree aged 65 is selecting a pension option. They can choose a joint-and-survivor pension that pays INR 1,200,000 annually while both the retiree (65) and their spouse (60) are alive, and drops to 60% of that amount (INR 720,000) when the retiree dies, payable for the life of the surviving spouse. (If the spouse dies first, the retiree continues to receive INR 1,200,000).

You are given: \(\ddot{a}_{65} = 12.0\), \(\ddot{a}_{60} = 14.5\), \(\ddot{a}_{65:60} = 10.5\).

Calculate the expected present value (EPV) of this pension.

Solution:

The pension consists of two parts:

  1. An annuity of INR 1,200,000 payable as long as the retiree (65) is alive. EPV = \(1,200,000 \times \ddot{a}_{65}\).
  2. A reversionary annuity of INR 720,000 payable to the spouse (60) after the death of the retiree (65). EPV = \(720,000 \times \ddot{a}_{65|60}\).

First, calculate the reversionary annuity value:

\[ \ddot{a}_{65|60} = \ddot{a}_{60} - \ddot{a}_{65:60} = 14.5 - 10.5 = 4.0 \]

Now, calculate the total EPV:

\[ EPV = (1,200,000 \times 12.0) + (720,000 \times 4.0) \] \[ EPV = 14,400,000 + 2,880,000 = \text{INR } 17,280,000 \]

28.5 Contingent Insurance

A contingent insurance is an insurance policy whose payment depends not only on the failure of a status but also on the order of deaths.

For example, a policy that pays 1 upon the death of \((x)\), provided that \((y)\) is still alive at the time of \((x)\)'s death. This is denoted by \(A^{1}_{xy}\), where the "1" above the \(x\) indicates that \((x)\) must die first.

The expected present value of a continuous contingent insurance is:

\[ \bar{A}^{1}_{xy} = \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_t p_{xy} \cdot \mu_{x+t} \dd t \]

Notice the difference between this and \(\bar{A}_{xy}\). The joint insurance \(\bar{A}_{xy}\) pays on the first death, regardless of who it is, so the relevant mortality rate is \(\mu_{xy}(t) = \mu_{x+t} + \mu_{y+t}\). The contingent insurance \(\bar{A}^{1}_{xy}\) only pays if \((x)\) dies first, so the relevant mortality rate is just \(\mu_{x+t}\).

Since the first death must be either \((x)\) or \((y)\), we have the relationship:

\[ \bar{A}_{xy} = \bar{A}^{1}_{xy} + \bar{A}^{1}_{yx} \]

Similarly, an insurance payable on the death of \((x)\), provided that \((y)\) has already died, is denoted by \(\bar{A}_{xy}^{2}\) (meaning \((x)\) is the second death). Since \((x)\) must die either first or second, we have:

\[ \bar{A}_x = \bar{A}^{1}_{xy} + \bar{A}_{xy}^{2} \]

28.6 Common Shock Model (Dependence)

Until now, we have assumed that \(T(x)\) and \(T(y)\) are independent. In reality, paired lives (like married couples) often exhibit positive dependence. They share living environments, dietary habits, and emotional states (the "broken heart syndrome"). Most importantly, they are subject to common shocks—events like accidents or natural disasters that could kill both simultaneously.

A classic way to model this is the Marshall-Olkin Bivariate Exponential Model. Assume there are three independent sources of decrement, represented by independent exponential random variables:

  • \(T_1 \sim \text{Exp}(\mu_1)\): Shock affecting only \((x)\)
  • \(T_2 \sim \text{Exp}(\mu_2)\): Shock affecting only \((y)\)
  • \(T_3 \sim \text{Exp}(\mu_{12})\): Common shock affecting both \((x)\) and \((y)\) simultaneously

The time of death for \((x)\) is \(T(x) = \min(T_1, T_3)\), and for \((y)\) is \(T(y) = \min(T_2, T_3)\).

Under this model, the joint survival function is:

\[ {}_t p_{xy} = \P(T(x) > t, T(y) > t) = \P(T_1 > t, T_2 > t, T_3 > t) \] \[ {}_t p_{xy} = e^{-\mu_1 t} \cdot e^{-\mu_2 t} \cdot e^{-\mu_{12} t} = e^{-(\mu_1 + \mu_2 + \mu_{12})t} \]

Note that the marginal survival functions are \({}_t p_x = e^{-(\mu_1 + \mu_{12})t}\) and \({}_t p_y = e^{-(\mu_2 + \mu_{12})t}\). Thus:

\[ {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y = e^{-(\mu_1 + \mu_2 + 2\mu_{12})t} \neq {}_t p_{xy} \]

This demonstrates the dependence. Copulas (such as the Gaussian or Frank copula) provide a more generalized framework to stitch together arbitrary marginal distributions with a specified dependence structure, extensively used in modern actuarial pricing.

Industry Application: Joint-Life Annuity Pricing

Life insurers pricing joint-and-survivor annuities must account for mortality dependence. If a husband and wife have highly correlated survival times, the probability that at least one survives to extreme old age is lower than if they were independent. Pricing with an independence assumption overestimates the value of a last-survivor annuity \(\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}}\) and underestimates the joint-life annuity \(\bar{a}_{xy}\). Actuaries often apply a "correlation load" or use Copula models (like the Frank Copula) calibrated to joint mortality data to adjust the premiums.

28.7 Multiple Decrement Models

In a multiple decrement model, a life can leave an initial state (usually "active") due to several competing causes. For example, an active employee can leave via:

  1. Death (\(d\))
  2. Withdrawal/Resignation (\(w\))
  3. Retirement (\(r\))
  4. Disability (\(i\))

Let the total number of decrements be \(m\). We use a superscript \((j)\) to denote a specific decrement \(j\), and \((\tau)\) to denote the total decrement from all causes.

The probability that a life aged \(x\) will leave the active state within \(t\) years due to decrement \(j\) is denoted by \({}_t q_x^{(j)}\). The probability that the life will leave due to any cause is:

\[ {}_t q_x^{(\tau)} = \sum_{j=1}^m {}_t q_x^{(j)} \]

The probability of remaining in the active state after \(t\) years is:

\[ {}_t p_x^{(\tau)} = 1 - {}_t q_x^{(\tau)} \]

Associated Single Decrement Tables (ASDT)

To construct a multiple decrement table, actuaries usually start with tables derived for each decrement in isolation. The probability that \((x)\) leaves due to decrement \(j\) within \(t\) years, assuming decrement \(j\) is the only possible cause of decrement in the universe, is called the Associated Single Decrement (ASD) rate, denoted \({}_t q_x^{\prime (j)}\).

The fundamental relationship between the multiple decrement survival probability and the ASD survival probabilities, assuming the competing risks act independently, is:

\[ {}_t p_x^{(\tau)} = \prod_{j=1}^m {}_t p_x^{\prime (j)} \]

where \({}_t p_x^{\prime (j)} = 1 - {}_t q_x^{\prime (j)}\).

Uniform Distribution of Decrements (UDD)

To link the single decrement rates \({}_1 q_x^{\prime (j)}\) to the multiple decrement probabilities \({}_1 q_x^{(j)}\) over a one-year interval, we need an assumption about how the decrements are distributed throughout the year. The most common assumption is that each decrement is uniformly distributed in the multiple decrement model (UDD in MDT).

Theorem 28.2: UDD in Multiple Decrement Model

If each decrement \(j\) is uniformly distributed over the year of age \([x, x+1]\) in the multiple decrement environment, then the relationship between the single and multiple decrement rates is given by the exact formula:

\[ p_x^{\prime (j)} = (p_x^{(\tau)})^{\frac{q_x^{(j)}}{q_x^{(\tau)}}} \]

Or equivalently:

\[ q_x^{(j)} = \frac{\ln p_x^{\prime (j)}}{\ln p_x^{(\tau)}} \cdot q_x^{(\tau)} \]
Example 28.4: Multiple Decrement Table Construction

For an active employee aged 40, you are given the following associated single decrement probabilities for death (\(d\)) and withdrawal (\(w\)):

  • \(q_{40}^{\prime (d)} = 0.02\)
  • \(q_{40}^{\prime (w)} = 0.15\)

Assuming UDD in the multiple decrement model, calculate the multiple decrement probabilities \(q_{40}^{(d)}\) and \(q_{40}^{(w)}\).

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate the total survival probability \(p_{40}^{(\tau)}\).

\[ p_{40}^{(\tau)} = p_{40}^{\prime (d)} \cdot p_{40}^{\prime (w)} = (1 - 0.02) \cdot (1 - 0.15) = 0.98 \times 0.85 = 0.833 \]

Therefore, the total multiple decrement rate is:

\[ q_{40}^{(\tau)} = 1 - p_{40}^{(\tau)} = 1 - 0.833 = 0.167 \]

Step 2: Use the UDD approximation to find the individual multiple decrement probabilities.

\[ q_{40}^{(d)} = \frac{\ln p_{40}^{\prime (d)}}{\ln p_{40}^{(\tau)}} \cdot q_{40}^{(\tau)} = \frac{\ln(0.98)}{\ln(0.833)} \times 0.167 = \frac{-0.02020}{-0.18272} \times 0.167 = 0.01847 \] \[ q_{40}^{(w)} = \frac{\ln p_{40}^{\prime (w)}}{\ln p_{40}^{(\tau)}} \cdot q_{40}^{(\tau)} = \frac{\ln(0.85)}{\ln(0.833)} \times 0.167 = \frac{-0.16252}{-0.18272} \times 0.167 = 0.14853 \]

Check: \(q_{40}^{(d)} + q_{40}^{(w)} = 0.01847 + 0.14853 = 0.167 = q_{40}^{(\tau)}\). Matches exactly.

Notice that \(q_{40}^{(d)} < q_{40}^{\prime (d)}\). This is because some people who would have died during the year end up withdrawing first, removing them from the exposure to death. This is the essence of competing risks.

28.8 The Disability-Death Model (Three-State Model)

The standard multiple decrement model assumes that once a life leaves the "active" state, it never returns. This is true for death or retirement, but not for disability. Disabled individuals can recover and return to the active state. To handle bidirectional transitions, we need a multi-state Markov model.

Consider a 3-state model:

  • State 0: Active (Healthy)
  • State 1: Disabled (Sick)
  • State 2: Dead

We define transition intensities (forces of transition) between states \(i\) and \(j\) at age \(x+t\) as \(\mu_x^{ij}(t)\).

  • \(\mu_x^{01}\): Rate of becoming disabled
  • \(\mu_x^{10}\): Rate of recovery
  • \(\mu_x^{02}\): Mortality rate of active lives
  • \(\mu_x^{12}\): Mortality rate of disabled lives

The probability that an individual in state \(i\) at age \(x\) is in state \(j\) at age \(x+t\) is denoted \({}_t p_x^{ij}\).

Kolmogorov Forward Equations

The transition probabilities are governed by a system of differential equations known as the Kolmogorov Forward Equations. For our 3-state model, considering the change in the probability of being in State 0 over a small interval \(\dd t\):

\[ \dv{}{t} {}_t p_x^{00} = -{}_t p_x^{00}(\mu_{x+t}^{01} + \mu_{x+t}^{02}) + {}_t p_x^{01}\mu_{x+t}^{10} \]

This intuitively says: the rate of change of the probability of being Active is equal to the probability of being Active right now times the rate of leaving (negative), plus the probability of being Disabled right now times the rate of recovering back to Active.

Example 28.5: Three-State Disability Model Premium

Consider a 5-year continuous disability income policy for a healthy life aged 50. The policy pays a continuous benefit of USD 50,000 per year while the insured is in the Disabled state (State 1). Premiums of \(P\) per year are paid continuously while the insured is in the Active state (State 0). The force of interest is \(\delta = 0.05\).

Using numerical methods on the Kolmogorov equations, the following expected present values (EPVs) of 1 per year continuous annuities are calculated:

  • \(\bar{a}_{50}^{00}:\overline{5|}\) (EPV of 1 per year while in State 0) = 4.25
  • \(\bar{a}_{50}^{01}:\overline{5|}\) (EPV of 1 per year while in State 1) = 0.35

Calculate the annual net premium \(P\).

Solution:

By the equivalence principle, the EPV of premiums must equal the EPV of benefits.

\[ \text{EPV(Premiums)} = P \times \bar{a}_{50}^{00}:\overline{5|} = 4.25P \] \[ \text{EPV(Benefits)} = 50,000 \times \bar{a}_{50}^{01}:\overline{5|} = 50,000 \times 0.35 = 17,500 \] \[ 4.25P = 17,500 \] \[ P = \frac{17,500}{4.25} = \text{USD } 4,117.65 \]

28.9 Thiele's Differential Equation for Multi-State Models

Thiele's differential equation (introduced in Chapter 27 for single states) generalizes beautifully to multi-state models, providing a continuous-time framework for reserving.

Let \({}_t V^{(i)}\) be the reserve at time \(t\) given the insured is currently in state \(i\). For a state \(i\), let \(B_t^{(i)}\) be the continuous benefit payment rate, \(P_t^{(i)}\) be the continuous premium payment rate, and \(S_t^{(ij)}\) be the lump sum payable immediately upon transition from state \(i\) to state \(j\).

Theorem 28.3: Generalized Thiele's Differential Equation

The evolution of the reserve in state \(i\) follows:

\[ \dv{}{t} {}_t V^{(i)} = \delta {}_t V^{(i)} + P_t^{(i)} - B_t^{(i)} - \sum_{j \neq i} \mu_{x+t}^{ij} \left( S_t^{(ij)} + {}_t V^{(j)} - {}_t V^{(i)} \right) \]

The term \((S_t^{(ij)} + {}_t V^{(j)} - {}_t V^{(i)})\) is the Net Amount at Risk (NAR) for a transition from state \(i\) to \(j\). It represents the immediate cash outlay plus the change in required reserve upon transition.

28.10 Python Code: Joint Life Annuity Calculator

The following Python code defines a class to calculate joint life and last survivor annuity values, assuming a constant force of mortality for simplicity.


import numpy as np

class MultipleLifeModel:
    def __init__(self, mu_x, mu_y, delta):
        self.mu_x = mu_x
        self.mu_y = mu_y
        self.delta = delta
        
    def a_x(self):
        """Continuous annuity on (x)"""
        return 1 / (self.mu_x + self.delta)
        
    def a_y(self):
        """Continuous annuity on (y)"""
        return 1 / (self.mu_y + self.delta)
        
    def a_xy(self):
        """Continuous joint-life annuity"""
        mu_xy = self.mu_x + self.mu_y
        return 1 / (mu_xy + self.delta)
        
    def a_last_survivor(self):
        """Continuous last-survivor annuity"""
        return self.a_x() + self.a_y() - self.a_xy()
        
    def a_reversionary(self):
        """Continuous reversionary annuity to (y) after (x) dies"""
        return self.a_y() - self.a_xy()

# Example 28.1 Implementation
mu_65 = 0.02
mu_62 = 0.015
delta = 0.04

model = MultipleLifeModel(mu_65, mu_62, delta)

print(f"Joint Life Annuity a_{{65:62}}: {model.a_xy():.4f}")
print(f"Last Survivor Annuity: {model.a_last_survivor():.4f}")
print(f"Reversionary Annuity (65|62): {model.a_reversionary():.4f}")

# Output:
# Joint Life Annuity a_{65:62}: 13.3333
# Last Survivor Annuity: 18.0606
# Reversionary Annuity (65|62): 4.8485

28.11 Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Chapter 28 Cheat-Sheet
  • Joint Life Survival: \({}_t p_{xy} = {}_t p_x \cdot {}_t p_y\) (Assuming independence)
  • Joint Life Force of Mortality: \(\mu_{xy}(t) = \mu_{x+t} + \mu_{y+t}\)
  • Last Survivor Survival: \({}_t p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_t p_x + {}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy}\)
  • Last Survivor Annuity: \(\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \bar{a}_x + \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy}\)
  • Reversionary Annuity (to \(y\) after \(x\)): \(\bar{a}_{x|y} = \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy}\)
  • Multiple Decrement to Single (UDD): \(p_x^{\prime (j)} = (p_x^{(\tau)})^{\frac{q_x^{(j)}}{q_x^{(\tau)}}}\)
  • Generalized Thiele's Eq: \(\dv{}{t} {}_t V^{(i)} = \delta {}_t V^{(i)} + P_t^{(i)} - B_t^{(i)} - \sum_{j \neq i} \mu_{x+t}^{ij} ( S_t^{(ij)} + {}_t V^{(j)} - {}_t V^{(i)} )\)

28.12 Exercises

Basic Concepts

1. For two independent lives (x) and (y), you are given: \({}_{10} p_x = 0.8\), \({}_{10} p_y = 0.9\). Calculate the probability that the joint-life status survives 10 years, and the probability that the last-survivor status survives 10 years.

Solution:
Joint-life survival: \({}_{10} p_{xy} = {}_{10} p_x \cdot {}_{10} p_y = 0.8 \times 0.9 = 0.72\).
Last-survivor survival: \({}_{10} p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_{10} p_x + {}_{10} p_y - {}_{10} p_{xy} = 0.8 + 0.9 - 0.72 = 0.98\).

2. Given \(\mu_{x+t} = 0.03\) and \(\mu_{y+t} = 0.05\) for all \(t\), find the expected future lifetime of the joint status \(xy\), \(\mathring{e}_{xy}\).

3. Prove that \(\bar{A}_{\overline{xy}} = \bar{A}_x + \bar{A}_y - \bar{A}_{xy}\).

Solution:
We know \({}_t p_{\overline{xy}} = {}_t p_x + {}_t p_y - {}_t p_{xy}\).
Multiply by \(v^t\) and integrate from 0 to \(\infty\):
\(\int_0^\infty v^t {}_t p_{\overline{xy}} dt = \int_0^\infty v^t {}_t p_x dt + \int_0^\infty v^t {}_t p_y dt - \int_0^\infty v^t {}_t p_{xy} dt\)
This gives \(\bar{a}_{\overline{xy}} = \bar{a}_x + \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy}\).
Using \(\bar{A} = 1 - \delta \bar{a}\):
\(\bar{A}_{\overline{xy}} = 1 - \delta(\bar{a}_x + \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy})\)
\(= 1 - \delta \bar{a}_x - \delta \bar{a}_y + \delta \bar{a}_{xy}\)
\(= \bar{A}_x + (1 - \delta \bar{a}_y) - (1 - \delta \bar{a}_{xy})\)
\(= \bar{A}_x + \bar{A}_y - \bar{A}_{xy}\).

4. In a multiple decrement model with two decrements (death = d, withdrawal = w), you are given \(q_x^{(d)} = 0.05\) and \(q_x^{(w)} = 0.10\). Calculate the probability of surviving both decrements over the year, \(p_x^{(\tau)}\).

Intermediate Problems

5. You are given: \(\bar{A}_x = 0.2\), \(\bar{A}_y = 0.3\), and \(\bar{A}_{xy} = 0.4\). Calculate the expected present value of a reversionary annuity of 1 per year, payable continuously to (y) after the death of (x). Assume \(\delta = 0.05\).

Solution:
First, find the annuity values: \(\bar{a}_k = (1 - \bar{A}_k) / \delta\).
\(\bar{a}_y = (1 - 0.3) / 0.05 = 14\).
\(\bar{a}_{xy} = (1 - 0.4) / 0.05 = 12\).
The reversionary annuity is \(\bar{a}_{x|y} = \bar{a}_y - \bar{a}_{xy} = 14 - 12 = 2\).

6. Assume independent lifetimes for (50) and (60). \(\mu_{50+t} = 0.01\) and \(\mu_{60+t} = 0.02\) for all \(t\). Calculate the probability that (50) dies before (60), denoted \({}_{\infty} q_{50:60}^1\).

Solution:
The probability is the integral of the joint survival probability times the force of mortality for (50):
\(\int_0^\infty {}_t p_{50:60} \cdot \mu_{50+t} dt\)
\(= \int_0^\infty e^{-0.01t} \cdot e^{-0.02t} \cdot (0.01) dt = \int_0^\infty 0.01 e^{-0.03t} dt\)
\(= \frac{0.01}{0.03} = \frac{1}{3}\).

7. Given ASD rates \(q_x^{\prime (1)} = 0.1\) and \(q_x^{\prime (2)} = 0.2\). Assume UDD in the multiple decrement model. Calculate \(q_x^{(1)}\).

Solution:
\(p_x^{(\tau)} = p_x^{\prime (1)} \cdot p_x^{\prime (2)} = (0.9)(0.8) = 0.72\).
\(q_x^{(\tau)} = 1 - 0.72 = 0.28\).
Using UDD relation: \(q_x^{(1)} = \frac{\ln(p_x^{\prime (1)})}{\ln(p_x^{(\tau)})} \cdot q_x^{(\tau)}\)
\(q_x^{(1)} = \frac{\ln(0.9)}{\ln(0.72)} \cdot 0.28 = \frac{-0.10536}{-0.32850} \cdot 0.28 = 0.0898\).

8. Under the Marshall-Olkin model, \(\mu_1 = 0.01\), \(\mu_2 = 0.015\), \(\mu_{12} = 0.005\). Calculate the probability that the last-survivor status survives 10 years.

Advanced Applications

9. Write the Kolmogorov Forward Equation for \(\dv{}{t} {}_t p_x^{01}\) in a 3-state disability model (0=Active, 1=Disabled, 2=Dead), given the transition intensities \(\mu^{01}, \mu^{10}, \mu^{02}, \mu^{12}\).

Solution:
\(\dv{}{t} {}_t p_x^{01} = {}_t p_x^{00} \cdot \mu_{x+t}^{01} - {}_t p_x^{01} (\mu_{x+t}^{10} + \mu_{x+t}^{12})\).

10. Derive Thiele's differential equation for the reserve of an Active life \({}_t V^{(0)}\) in the 3-state disability model. Assume premiums \(P\) are paid continuously while Active, disability benefits \(B\) are paid continuously while Disabled, and a death benefit \(S\) is paid upon death from either state.

11. Two lives (x) and (y) are subject to a common shock model. An insurance policy pays 100,000 at the moment of the second death. Assuming \(\delta = 0.05\), \(\mu_1 = 0.01\), \(\mu_2 = 0.02\), and \(\mu_{12} = 0.02\). Calculate the net single premium for this policy.

Solution:
Marginal rates: \(\mu_x = 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.03\), \(\mu_y = 0.02 + 0.02 = 0.04\).
Joint rate: \(\mu_{xy} = \mu_1 + \mu_2 + \mu_{12} = 0.01 + 0.02 + 0.02 = 0.05\).
\(\bar{A}_x = \frac{\mu_x}{\mu_x + \delta} = \frac{0.03}{0.08} = 0.375\).
\(\bar{A}_y = \frac{\mu_y}{\mu_y + \delta} = \frac{0.04}{0.09} = 0.4444\).
\(\bar{A}_{xy} = \frac{\mu_{xy}}{\mu_{xy} + \delta} = \frac{0.05}{0.10} = 0.500\).
\(\bar{A}_{\overline{xy}} = 0.375 + 0.4444 - 0.500 = 0.3194\).
Premium = \(100,000 \times 0.3194 = 31,944\).

12. Prove that if the forces of decrement \(\mu_x^{(j)}(t)\) are constant between integer ages, then UDD in the multiple decrement table implies constant forces of decrement, and vice versa.

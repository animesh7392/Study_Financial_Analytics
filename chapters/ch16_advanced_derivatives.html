Chapter 16: Advanced Derivative Pricing

1. Learning Objectives
  • Understand and implement Monte Carlo simulation for derivative pricing, including variance reduction techniques.
  • Apply Finite-Difference (PDE) methods (Explicit, Implicit, and Crank-Nicolson) for solving the Black-Scholes PDE.
  • Price American options using the Longstaff-Schwartz algorithm (LSM) and free-boundary PDE formulations.
  • Analyze and price exotic options such as Barriers, Asians, Lookbacks, Digitals, and Basket options.
  • Extend the constant volatility framework to Local Volatility (Dupire), Stochastic Volatility (Heston, SABR), and Jump-Diffusion models (Merton).
  • Understand variance swaps, VIX construction, and techniques for robust model calibration.
2. Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter builds directly on the foundational concepts introduced in earlier chapters. Specifically:

  • Chapter 8 (Stochastic Calculus): Ito's Lemma, Geometric Brownian Motion, and risk-neutral measures.
  • Chapter 9 (Numerical Methods): Basic root-finding, optimization, and matrix algebra.
  • Chapter 15 (Options): Black-Scholes-Merton framework, vanilla options, and the Greeks.

We now move beyond vanilla European options under constant volatility. This chapter will introduce advanced numerical methods (Monte Carlo and PDEs) necessary when analytical solutions are unavailable (e.g., American and path-dependent exotics), and advanced models (Local Vol, Heston, Jumps) necessary to capture the volatility smile.

3. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you are a structurer at a global investment bank. A corporate client in India wants to hedge their currency risk (USD/INR) but finds traditional vanilla options too expensive. They ask you for a tailored product that only provides protection if the exchange rate breaches a specific level (a barrier option), or perhaps based on the average exchange rate over the next year (an Asian option). The Black-Scholes formula, beautiful as it is, cannot price these directly.

Furthermore, the Black-Scholes model assumes volatility is constant over time and across strikes. A quick look at the market reveals the "volatility smile": out-of-the-money puts often trade at significantly higher implied volatilities than at-the-money options. To accurately price and hedge complex derivatives, we must model this market reality using Local Volatility, Stochastic Volatility, or Jump models. Advanced derivative pricing is the art and science of relaxing standard assumptions to accurately reflect market dynamics and handle complex, path-dependent payoffs.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Monte Carlo Pricing

Definition 16.1: Risk-Neutral Monte Carlo Pricing

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the price of any derivative with payoff \(h(S_T)\) at maturity \(T\) is:

\[ V_0 = e^{-rT} \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[ h(S_T) ] \]

The Monte Carlo estimator approximates this expectation by averaging over \(N\) simulated paths: \(\hat{V}_0 = e^{-rT} \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} h(S_T^{(i)})\), with standard error \(\text{SE} = \hat{\sigma}_{\text{payoff}} / \sqrt{N}\).

Theorem 16.1: Dupire's Local Volatility Formula

Given a smooth surface of European call prices \(C(K,T)\) as a function of strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\), the unique local volatility function that is consistent with all these prices satisfies:

\[ \sigma_{\text{loc}}^2(K,T) = \frac{2\left[\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} + (r-q)K\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} + qC\right]}{K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}} \]

Reference: Dupire, B. (1994). "Pricing with a Smile." Risk, 7(1), 18–20.

For a stock following Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM):

\[ dS_t = (r - q) S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t \]

By simulating paths of \(S_t\), calculating the payoff for each path, and taking the average, we obtain the MC estimate. The exact solution for GBM over a time step \(\Delta t\) is:

\[ S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t \exp\left( (r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)\Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z \right) \]

where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

Variance Reduction: MC convergence is slow (\(O(1/\sqrt{N})\)). We use variance reduction to speed it up:
1. Antithetic Variates: For every random draw \(Z\), we also use \(-Z\). This induces negative correlation, reducing the variance of the average.
2. Control Variates: We use a similar option with a known analytical price (e.g., Black-Scholes European call) to correct the simulated price of the complex option.

4.2 Finite-Difference PDE Methods

The Black-Scholes PDE is a parabolic partial differential equation:

\[ \pdv{V}{t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + (r-q)S\pdv{V}{S} - rV = 0 \]

We discretize the time to maturity \(T\) into steps of \(\Delta t\) and the asset price \(S\) into a grid with spacing \(\Delta S\). Let \(V_{i,j}\) be the option price at time \(j\Delta t\) and asset price \(i\Delta S\).

  • Explicit Method: Replaces derivatives with forward differences in time and central differences in space. It is easy to implement but only conditionally stable (requires \(\Delta t \le (\Delta S)^2 / (2\sigma^2 S^2)\)).
  • Implicit Method: Uses backward differences in time. Unconditionally stable. Requires solving a tridiagonal system of linear equations at each time step.
  • Crank-Nicolson: The average of the Explicit and Implicit schemes. It is unconditionally stable and achieves second-order accuracy in both time and space.

4.3 American Options

American options can be exercised at any time before expiration. This introduces a "free boundary" problem in PDE formulations, where at each node we take \(\max(\text{Continuation Value}, \text{Exercise Value})\).

In Monte Carlo, we use the Longstaff-Schwartz Method (LSM). The core idea is to estimate the continuation value (the expected payoff of keeping the option alive) by regressing the discounted future realized payoffs against basis functions (like polynomials) of the current asset price. If the immediate exercise value is greater than the estimated continuation value, the path is marked for early exercise.

4.4 Exotic Options

  • Barrier Options: Path-dependent options that activate (knock-in) or terminate (knock-out) if the underlying breaches a barrier \(H\). Pricing relies on the reflection principle of Brownian motion for continuous monitoring, yielding closed-form formulas for standard barriers.
  • Asian Options: Payoff depends on the average price over a period. Geometric averages have a closed-form solution (as the product of lognormals is lognormal), but arithmetic averages require numerical integration or Monte Carlo, often using the geometric Asian as a control variate.
  • Lookback Options: Payoff depends on the maximum or minimum price reached during the life of the option. Highly sensitive to volatility.
  • Digital/Binary Options: Pay a fixed amount if the asset is above (or below) the strike. The Cash-or-Nothing call price is simply \(e^{-rT} N(d_2)\), reflecting the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money.

4.5 Local Volatility (Dupire)

To match the market implied volatility surface exactly, Bruno Dupire showed that there exists a unique state-dependent volatility function \(\sigma(K, T)\) given by the Dupire Equation:

\[ \sigma_{loc}^2(K,T) = \frac{2\left(\pdv{C}{T} + (r-q)K\pdv{C}{K} + qC\right)}{K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}} \]

where \(C(K,T)\) is the market price of European calls.

4.6 Stochastic Volatility & Jumps

Heston Model: Assumes volatility itself follows a mean-reverting stochastic process:

\[ dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sqrt{v_t} S_t dW_{1,t} \] \[ dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)dt + \xi \sqrt{v_t} dW_{2,t} \]

where \(dW_1\) and \(dW_2\) have correlation \(\rho\). Pricing is done via Fourier inversion of the characteristic function.

Merton Jump-Diffusion: Asset prices occasionally experience discontinuous jumps. The path is a combination of standard GBM and a Poisson jump process. The price of a European option is an infinite sum of Black-Scholes prices with modified parameters.

4.7 Variance Swaps & Model Calibration

A variance swap pays the difference between realized variance and a fixed variance strike \(K_{var}\). Strikingly, it can be perfectly replicated using a static portfolio of out-of-the-money options (a "log contract"), which is the theoretical foundation for the VIX index.

Model calibration involves finding parameters (e.g., Heston's \(\kappa, \theta, \rho, \xi, v_0\)) that minimize the difference between model prices and market prices. Typically, practitioners minimize the sum of squared errors (SSE) of implied volatilities, often weighted by Vega so that more sensitive options have a higher impact on the fit.

5. Worked Examples

Example 16.1: Monte Carlo pricing with Antithetic and Control Variates

Problem: Price a European Call with \(S_0=100\), \(K=100\), \(r=0.05\), \(\sigma=0.2\), \(T=1.0\). We use a 1-step MC with 1 pair of antithetic standard normal draws \(Z_1 = 1.5, Z_2 = -1.5\). (Assume zero dividend \(q=0\)). Then apply a control variate.

Solution:

Step 1: Antithetic Variates

Terminal prices:

\[ S_T(Z) = S_0 \exp((r - 0.5\sigma^2)T + \sigma \sqrt{T} Z) \] \[ S_T(1.5) = 100 \exp(0.03 + 0.2 \times 1.5) = 100 \exp(0.33) \approx 139.097 \] \[ S_T(-1.5) = 100 \exp(0.03 - 0.3) = 100 \exp(-0.27) \approx 76.338 \]

Payoffs:

\[ C(1.5) = \max(139.097 - 100, 0) = 39.097 \] \[ C(-1.5) = \max(76.338 - 100, 0) = 0 \]

Average Payoff = \( (39.097 + 0)/2 = 19.5485 \).

Discounted Price (MC Estimate) = \( 19.5485 \times e^{-0.05} \approx 18.59 \).

Step 2: Control Variates

Suppose our actual BS Call Price is \( C_{BS} = 10.45 \). If we simulated the same call and got \( C_{sim} = 18.59 \), and we are pricing an Asian Call that simulated to \( A_{sim} = 12.00 \). The control variate estimate for the Asian call is:

\[ A_{adj} = A_{sim} - c(C_{sim} - C_{BS}) = 12.00 - 1.0(18.59 - 10.45) = 3.86 \]

(Assuming \(c \approx 1\), covariance is high between Asian and European).

Example 16.2: Down-and-Out Barrier Call

Problem: Price a Down-and-Out Call (DOC). \(S_0=100\), \(K=100\), Barrier \(H=90\), \(r=0.05\), \(q=0\), \(\sigma=0.2\), \(T=1.0\).

Solution:

The standard formula for a DOC (when \(H \le K\)) using the reflection principle is:

\[ C_{DOC} = C_{BS}(S_0) - \left(\frac{H}{S_0}\right)^{2\lambda} C_{BS}\left(\frac{H^2}{S_0}\right) \]

where \(\lambda = \frac{r-q+\sigma^2/2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.05 + 0.02}{0.04} = 1.75\).

\(C_{BS}(S_0=100) = 10.45\).

\(H^2/S_0 = 8100/100 = 81\). We calculate \(C_{BS}\) for an asset price of 81 with strike 100.

\(d_1 = \frac{\ln(81/100) + (0.05+0.02) \times 1}{0.2 \times 1} = \frac{-0.2107 + 0.07}{0.2} = -0.7036\).

\(d_2 = -0.7036 - 0.2 = -0.9036\).

\(N(d_1) = 0.2408\), \(N(d_2) = 0.1831\).

\(C_{BS}(81) = 81(0.2408) - 100 e^{-0.05}(0.1831) = 19.50 - 17.41 = 2.09\).

Price of DOC = \( 10.45 - (0.9)^{3.5} \times 2.09 = 10.45 - 0.692 \times 2.09 = 10.45 - 1.44 = 9.01 \).

Example 16.3: Longstaff-Schwartz Algorithm (LSM)

Problem: Value an American Put on a non-dividend paying stock. Strike \(K=1.1\), \(r=0.06\). \(\Delta t = 1\), \(T=3\). Simulated paths:

  • Path 1: S(1)=1.08, S(2)=1.07, S(3)=1.05 (In Money at T=3, Payoff=0.05)
  • Path 2: S(1)=1.15, S(2)=1.12, S(3)=1.18 (Out of Money)
  • Path 3: S(1)=1.02, S(2)=1.04, S(3)=1.09 (In Money at T=3, Payoff=0.01)

Solution: We work backwards from \(t=2\).

At \(t=2\), ITM paths are Path 1 (S=1.07) and Path 3 (S=1.04).

Exercise values: Path 1 = 1.1 - 1.07 = 0.03. Path 3 = 1.1 - 1.04 = 0.06.

Discounted future payoffs from \(t=3\): Path 1 = \(0.05 \times e^{-0.06} = 0.047\). Path 3 = \(0.01 \times e^{-0.06} = 0.0094\).

Regress discounted future payoffs \(Y\) on \(S\) and \(S^2\). (Since we only have 2 points, it's a perfect fit, but in practice, you use least squares on many paths).

Let's say the regression gives Continuation Value (CV) for Path 1 = 0.04, and Path 3 = 0.02.

For Path 1: Exercise (0.03) < CV (0.04). Do not exercise.

For Path 3: Exercise (0.06) > CV (0.02). Exercise! The payoff is now 0.06 at \(t=2\), overriding the \(t=3\) payoff.

Repeat for \(t=1\). The final value is the average of discounted cash flows along the optimal exercise boundary.

Example 16.4: Heston Model Implied Volatility

Problem: Given a Heston model output where a European Call with \(K=90\) is priced at \(\$14.00\) and \(K=110\) is priced at \(\$2.50\). \(S=100, T=1, r=0\). What does this imply about the smile?

Solution: By solving the standard Black-Scholes formula for \(\sigma\) using a root-finding method (like Newton-Raphson), we find the implied volatilities.

For \(K=90, C=14.00 \implies\) Invert BS formula \(\implies \sigma_{imp} \approx 25.5\%\).

For \(K=110, C=2.50 \implies\) Invert BS formula \(\implies \sigma_{imp} \approx 18.2\%\).

The higher implied volatility for the downside strike (\(K=90\)) compared to the upside strike (\(K=110\)) indicates a negative skew. In the Heston model, this is generated by setting the correlation parameter \(\rho < 0\).

Example 16.5: Geometric Asian Option

Problem: Price a continuously monitored geometric Asian call. \(S_0=100, K=100, r=0.05, q=0, \sigma=0.3, T=1.0\).

Solution: The geometric average of lognormal random variables is lognormal. We adjust the volatility and dividend yield.

Adjusted volatility: \(\sigma_A = \sigma / \sqrt{3} = 0.3 / \sqrt{3} = 0.1732\).

Adjusted dividend yield: \(q_A = \frac{1}{2}\left(r + q + \frac{\sigma^2}{6}\right) = \frac{1}{2}(0.05 + 0 + 0.015) = 0.0325\).

We use the standard BS formula with \(S_0=100, K=100, r=0.05, q=0.0325, \sigma=0.1732, T=1\).

\(d_1 = \frac{\ln(1) + (0.05 - 0.0325 + 0.1732^2/2)}{0.1732} = \frac{0.0175 + 0.015}{0.1732} = 0.1876\).

\(d_2 = 0.1876 - 0.1732 = 0.0144\).

\(N(d_1) = 0.5744, N(d_2) = 0.5057\).

\(C = 100 e^{-0.0325}(0.5744) - 100 e^{-0.05}(0.5057) = 55.60 - 48.10 = 7.50\).

6. Python Code

Below is an implementation of the Longstaff-Schwartz Algorithm to price an American Put Option using Monte Carlo simulation.


import numpy as np

def lsm_american_put(S0, K, T, r, sigma, N_sims, N_steps):
    """
    Price an American Put option using the Longstaff-Schwartz method.
    """
    np.random.seed(42)
    dt = T / N_steps
    discount = np.exp(-r * dt)
    
    # 1. Simulate paths (GBM)
    S = np.zeros((N_steps + 1, N_sims))
    S[0] = S0
    for t in range(1, N_steps + 1):
        Z = np.random.standard_normal(N_sims)
        S[t] = S[t-1] * np.exp((r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * Z)
        
    # 2. Initialization at maturity
    payoffs = np.maximum(K - S[-1], 0)
    
    # 3. Backward Induction
    for t in range(N_steps - 1, 0, -1):
        itm = S[t] < K # Only regress In-The-Money paths
        if np.sum(itm) > 0:
            X = S[t][itm]
            Y = payoffs[itm] * discount
            
            # Regress Y on X and X^2 (basis functions)
            poly = np.polyfit(X, Y, 2)
            continuation_value = np.polyval(poly, X)
            
            exercise_value = K - X
            
            # Determine early exercise
            exercise_now = exercise_value > continuation_value
            
            # Update payoffs
            payoffs[itm] = np.where(exercise_now, exercise_value, Y)
            payoffs[~itm] = payoffs[~itm] * discount
            
    # Discount back to T=0
    price = np.mean(payoffs * discount)
    return price

# Example execution
S0, K, T, r, sigma = 100.0, 100.0, 1.0, 0.05, 0.20
eur_put = 5.57 # From Black-Scholes formula
am_put = lsm_american_put(S0, K, T, r, sigma, 50000, 50)
print(f"European Put Price (BS): ${eur_put:.2f}")
print(f"American Put Price (LSM): ${am_put:.2f}")
print(f"Early Exercise Premium: ${am_put - eur_put:.2f}")

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Simulating Physical instead of Risk-Neutral paths: Using the real-world drift \(\mu\) instead of the risk-free rate \(r-q\) in Monte Carlo simulations is a fatal error. Derivative pricing requires the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\).
  • Overfitting in Calibration: Calibrating a highly parameterized model (like a jump-diffusion model with local volatility) perfectly to today's prices might lead to unstable hedging ratios tomorrow. Parsimony is often better.
  • Implicit assumption of continuous monitoring in barriers: Standard formulas assume continuous barrier monitoring. In reality, contracts specify discrete monitoring (e.g., daily close). This requires a shift in the barrier level (Broadie-Glasserman-Kou correction).
  • Misapplying Control Variates: Choosing a control variate that is weakly correlated with the target derivative increases variance rather than reducing it. Always verify the correlation.
9. Industry Application Box: Hedging Exotics

At a large derivatives desk, an exotic options trader sells a massive size of Down-and-In Puts to a hedge fund. How is this risk managed?

Using the Black-Scholes model directly is dangerous. If the trader merely hedges the Delta, they face "barrier risk". As the asset price approaches the barrier, the Gamma (rate of change of Delta) explodes. The trader would be forced to sell massive amounts of stock exactly when liquidity is likely poor.

To mitigate this, quantitative researchers build Local Volatility and Stochastic Volatility models to capture the joint probability of large downward moves and volatility spikes. Furthermore, they use static replication—trading a portfolio of standard European vanilla puts at different strikes—to mimic the barrier payoff, minimizing the need for continuous, unstable dynamic hedging at the barrier.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Monte Carlo Path: \( S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t e^{(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z} \)

Explicit PDE Stability: \(\Delta t \le \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2\sigma^2}\)

Dupire Local Vol: \( \sigma_{loc}^2(K,T) = \frac{2(\pdv{C}{T} + (r-q)K\pdv{C}{K} + qC)}{K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}} \)

Heston Variance Process: \( dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t)dt + \xi \sqrt{v_t} dW_{2,t} \)

Merton Jump-Diffusion Price: \( C_{Merton} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\lambda' T} (\lambda' T)^n}{n!} C_{BS}(S_0, K, T, r_n, \sigma_n) \)

where \(r_n = r - \lambda k + \frac{n \ln(1+k)}{T}\) and \(\sigma_n^2 = \sigma^2 + \frac{n \delta^2}{T}\).

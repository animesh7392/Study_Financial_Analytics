1. Intuition & Motivation

Consider the traditional auto insurance pricing model: a 25-year-old driver living in an urban area is charged a high premium based on broad demographic factors. However, this driver might only use their vehicle for a short, low-speed commute twice a week. Traditional models fail to capture this idiosyncratic risk profile. Enter Insurance Data Analytics. By harnessing telematics data from smartphones or on-board diagnostic (OBD) devices, insurers can measure actual driving behavior—speed, acceleration, braking, and time of day—and price the risk dynamically. This shift from proxy variables to behavioral variables exemplifies the revolution sweeping the insurance industry.

Beyond auto insurance, unstructured data is transforming every line of business. Claims departments use Natural Language Processing (NLP) to read adjuster notes, computer vision to assess vehicle damage from photos, and network analysis to uncover sophisticated fraud rings. The modern actuary and data scientist must navigate this landscape, not only to price risk more accurately but also to enhance customer retention through Customer Lifetime Value (CLV) modeling and develop innovative products like parametric insurance and digital twins.

2. Formal Theory

2.1 Telematics and Usage-Based Insurance (UBI)

Usage-Based Insurance (UBI) tailors premiums to individual usage patterns. There are two primary paradigms: Pay-As-You-Drive (PAYD), which focuses on mileage, and Pay-How-You-Drive (PHYD), which incorporates driving behavior.

Telematics Driving Score

A composite index \( S \in [0, 100] \) derived from kinematic vehicle data designed to reflect the risk of collision. The score typically incorporates hard braking, rapid acceleration, speeding, and cornering events.

Let \( \mathbf{X}_i(t) \) denote the telematics feature vector for driver \( i \) at time \( t \). The frequency of risky events (e.g., hard braking instances per 100 miles) is modeled as a Poisson process. The telematics score \( S_i \) is often constructed using a logistic regression or gradient boosting model predicting claim probability \( p_i \):

\[ \text{logit}(p_i) = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^k \beta_j g_j(\mathbf{X}_{ij}) \]

where \( g_j(\cdot) \) are transformation functions (e.g., binning or splines) applied to the raw telematics features. The score is then mapped to a relativity multiplier \( M(S_i) \) applied to the base premium.

2.2 Geospatial Analytics

Geospatial analysis incorporates spatial dependencies into risk modeling. The proximity to perils such as coastlines, fault lines, or flood zones significantly alters the risk profile.

Let \( (lat_i, lon_i) \) be the coordinates of property \( i \). We can define a spatial weight matrix \( \mathbf{W} \) where \( w_{ij} \) represents the inverse distance or contiguity between locations \( i \) and \( j \). A spatial autoregressive (SAR) model extends standard GLMs:

\[ \mathbf{y} = \rho \mathbf{W} \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \]

where \( \rho \) captures the spatial correlation (e.g., the tendency for properties in the same neighborhood to experience similar burglary rates).

2.3 Text Mining for Claims

Claims data often includes unstructured text from First Notice of Loss (FNOL), adjuster notes, and medical reports. Natural Language Processing (NLP) extracts features from this text. Techniques like TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) or word embeddings (Word2Vec, BERT) map text into numerical vectors.

For a document \( d \) and term \( t \):

\[ \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{count}(t, d)}{\sum_{k} \text{count}(k, d)} \times \log\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}_t}\right) \]

where \( N \) is the total number of documents and \( \text{df}_t \) is the number of documents containing term \( t \). These vectors are fed into classifiers for claims triage (e.g., fast-tracking simple claims or flagging potential fraud).

2.4 Image Analytics

Computer vision leverages Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to analyze imagery. For auto claims, an image \( \mathbf{I} \) is processed through layers of convolutions to detect features (e.g., dents, broken glass) and estimate repair costs. In property insurance, satellite and drone imagery assess roof conditions or crop health (using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index, NDVI).

2.5 Fraud Detection Advanced

While traditional fraud detection uses rules and supervised learning on individual claims, organized fraud involves networks of actors (colluding doctors, lawyers, auto shops, and claimants). Graph theory is employed here.

Fraud Network Graph

A graph \( G = (V, E) \) where vertices \( V \) represent entities (individuals, vehicles, service providers) and edges \( E \) represent relationships (e.g., same address, same accident). Fraud rings appear as dense subgraphs or cliques.

Metrics like Degree Centrality, Betweenness Centrality, and the clustering coefficient identify key nodes orchestrating the fraud.

2.6 Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)

Customer Lifetime Value is the present value of all future cash flows attributed to the customer relationship. It requires predicting retention probabilities over time.

Using survival analysis, let \( S(t) \) be the probability a customer remains with the insurer at time \( t \). The CLV is given by:

\[ \text{CLV} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \frac{S(t) \cdot (P_t - C_t - E_t)}{(1 + d)^t} \]

where \( P_t \) is premium, \( C_t \) is expected claims, \( E_t \) is expenses, and \( d \) is the discount rate. A Cox Proportional Hazards model or a discrete-time logistic model is typically used to estimate \( S(t) \).

2.7 Pricing Innovation

Parametric insurance triggers payouts based on the occurrence of an objective, measurable event (e.g., earthquake magnitude, cumulative rainfall) rather than actual loss assessment. This eliminates claims adjustment expenses and moral hazard.

Parametric Pricing Formula

If the payout \( L(X) \) depends on index \( X \), the pure premium is \( \pi = \E[L(X)] \). For a rainfall index \( X \) with trigger \( x_0 \), tick size \( \Delta \), and limit \( M \):

\[ L(X) = \min\left( M, \max(0, \Delta \cdot (x_0 - X)) \right) \]

The premium is calculated by integrating this payoff function against the probability density function \( f(x) \) of the index \( X \).

2.8 Ethical AI in Insurance

As algorithms govern pricing and underwriting, algorithmic fairness is paramount. A model exhibits Disparate Impact if its outcomes disproportionately affect protected classes (e.g., race, gender) even if those attributes are not explicitly used as inputs (due to proxy variables like zip code).

Regulatory frameworks (like the EU AI Act and guidelines by IRDAI/NAIC) mandate transparency and explainability. Techniques like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) are used to attribute the prediction output to individual features, ensuring the decision-making process is interpretable.

2.9 InsurTech

InsurTech encompasses technological innovations designed to squeeze out savings and efficiency from the current insurance industry model. This includes API ecosystems for embedded insurance (e.g., buying travel insurance seamlessly while booking a flight) and blockchain/smart contracts for automated, trustless claims settlements.

2.10 Digital Twins

A digital twin is a virtual representation of a physical asset (a factory, a fleet of ships) continuously updated with real-time IoT sensor data. Insurers use digital twins for predictive maintenance—intervening before a breakdown causes a massive business interruption loss—and for dynamic risk pricing based on real-time operational states.

3. Worked Examples

Example 41.1: Telematics Driving Score

Problem: An insurer tracks a driver over 1,000 miles. The telemetry records 12 hard braking events and 4 rapid accelerations. The baseline expected claim frequency is 0.05 claims per year. The telematics model modifies the base frequency \( \lambda_0 \) multiplicatively based on events per 100 miles: \( \lambda = \lambda_0 \times \exp(\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2) \), where \( x_1 \) is hard braking per 100 miles, \( x_2 \) is rapid acceleration per 100 miles, \( \beta_1 = 0.15 \), and \( \beta_2 = 0.08 \). Calculate the revised expected claim frequency.

Solution:

1. Calculate rates per 100 miles:

\( x_1 = \frac{12}{1000/100} = \frac{12}{10} = 1.2 \)

\( x_2 = \frac{4}{10} = 0.4 \)

2. Calculate the multiplier:

\( M = \exp(0.15 \times 1.2 + 0.08 \times 0.4) = \exp(0.18 + 0.032) = \exp(0.212) \approx 1.2361 \)

3. Revised frequency:

\( \lambda = 0.05 \times 1.2361 = 0.0618 \) claims per year.

Example 41.2: Geospatial Risk Scoring

Problem: A property located at coordinates A is evaluated for flood risk. The base pure premium for flood is ₹5,000. GIS analysis determines the distance \( d \) (in meters) to the nearest major water body is 150m. The elevation relative to the water level \( h \) is +2m. The pricing factor is given by \( F(d,h) = 1 + \max(0, 500 - d)/1000 \times \max(0, 5 - h)/5 \). Calculate the final premium.

Solution:

1. Calculate distance component:

\( \max(0, 500 - 150)/1000 = 350 / 1000 = 0.35 \)

2. Calculate elevation component:

\( \max(0, 5 - 2)/5 = 3 / 5 = 0.6 \)

3. Calculate Factor \( F \):

\( F = 1 + (0.35 \times 0.6) = 1 + 0.21 = 1.21 \)

4. Final Premium:

\( \pi = 5000 \times 1.21 = \text{₹}6,050 \).

Example 41.3: CLV with Survival Analysis

Problem: A policyholder generates an annual profit margin of $150 (assumed received at year-end). The survival probability at the end of year \( t \) is given by a Weibull survival function \( S(t) = \exp(-(0.1 t)^{1.5}) \). The discount rate is 5%. Calculate the CLV over a 3-year horizon.

Solution:

1. Calculate survival probabilities \( S(t) \):

Year 1: \( S(1) = \exp(-(0.1 \times 1)^{1.5}) = \exp(-0.0316) \approx 0.9689 \)

Year 2: \( S(2) = \exp(-(0.1 \times 2)^{1.5}) = \exp(-0.0894) \approx 0.9144 \)

Year 3: \( S(3) = \exp(-(0.1 \times 3)^{1.5}) = \exp(-0.1643) \approx 0.8485 \)

2. Calculate discounted cash flows:

\( \text{DCF}_1 = \frac{150 \times 0.9689}{1.05} = 138.41 \)

\( \text{DCF}_2 = \frac{150 \times 0.9144}{1.05^2} = 124.41 \)

\( \text{DCF}_3 = \frac{150 \times 0.8485}{1.05^3} = 109.95 \)

3. CLV = \( 138.41 + 124.41 + 109.95 = \$372.77 \).

Example 41.4: Fraud Network Graph Analysis

Problem: Consider a bipartite graph of 3 claims (\(C_1, C_2, C_3\)) and 4 entities (Doctor A, Lawyer B, Auto Shop C, Claimant D).

  • \(C_1\) involves Doctor A, Lawyer B, Claimant D.
  • \(C_2\) involves Doctor A, Lawyer B, Auto Shop C.
  • \(C_3\) involves Doctor A, Auto Shop C.

Construct the one-mode projection graph of entities (connecting entities if they share a claim) and calculate the degree of Doctor A and Lawyer B.

Solution:

In the one-mode projection, an edge exists between two entities if they co-occur in at least one claim.

Doctor A co-occurs with: Lawyer B (in \(C_1, C_2\)), Claimant D (in \(C_1\)), Auto Shop C (in \(C_2, C_3\)).

Degree of Doctor A = 3 (connected to B, C, D).

Lawyer B co-occurs with: Doctor A (in \(C_1, C_2\)), Claimant D (in \(C_1\)), Auto Shop C (in \(C_2\)).

Degree of Lawyer B = 3 (connected to A, C, D).

The high connectivity indicates a tight-knit cluster typical of coordinated activity.

Example 41.5: Parametric Crop Insurance

Problem: A parametric drought insurance product pays out based on total rainfall \( X \) (in mm) during the monsoon season. If \( X < 300 \) mm, the payout is \( 100 \times (300 - X) \), up to a maximum payout of ₹15,000. Historical data suggests \( X \) follows a Normal distribution \( N(\mu=350, \sigma=50) \). Estimate the pure premium using the standard normal CDF \( \Phi \).

Solution:

Payout function: \( L(X) = \min(15000, \max(0, 100(300 - X))) \).

Maximum payout of 15,000 occurs when \( 100(300 - X) \ge 15000 \implies 300 - X \ge 150 \implies X \le 150 \).

The pure premium is \( \E[L(X)] \).

\( \E[L(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^{150} 15000 f(x) dx + \int_{150}^{300} 100(300 - x) f(x) dx \)

For \( X \sim N(350, 50^2) \):

\( Z_1 = (150 - 350)/50 = -4 \). \( \Phi(-4) \approx 0 \).

\( Z_2 = (300 - 350)/50 = -1 \). \( \Phi(-1) \approx 0.1587 \).

The first term is approx 0. The second term requires integrating a truncated normal. Using the formula for partial expectations of normal distribution: \( \E[c - X | X < c] P(X < c) \).

\( \E[300 - X | 150 < X < 300] P(150 < X < 300) = \int_{150}^{300} (300 - x) f(x) dx \).

We know \( \int_{-\infty}^{c} x f(x) dx = \mu \Phi(\frac{c-\mu}{\sigma}) - \sigma \phi(\frac{c-\mu}{\sigma}) \).

Approximating the lower bound as \( -\infty \) since \( \Phi(-4) \approx 0 \):

\( \int_{-\infty}^{300} (300 - x) f(x) dx = 300 \Phi(-1) - [350 \Phi(-1) - 50 \phi(-1)] = -50 \Phi(-1) + 50 \phi(-1) \).

Where \( \phi(-1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp(-0.5) \approx 0.2420 \).

Integral \( = -50(0.1587) + 50(0.2420) = -7.935 + 12.10 = 4.165 \).

Pure premium \( = 100 \times 4.165 = \text{₹}416.50 \).

4. Python Code

The following script demonstrates how to calculate a telematics driving score based on the model in Example 41.1, utilizing a sample dataset of drivers.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# Generate sample telematics data for 5 drivers
np.random.seed(42)
data = {
    'Driver_ID': range(1, 6),
    'Miles_Driven': np.random.uniform(500, 2000, 5),
    'Hard_Brakes': np.random.randint(0, 20, 5),
    'Rapid_Accels': np.random.randint(0, 10, 5)
}
df = pd.DataFrame(data)

# Baseline frequency
lambda_0 = 0.05
beta_1 = 0.15
beta_2 = 0.08

# Calculate events per 100 miles
df['Brakes_per_100'] = df['Hard_Brakes'] / (df['Miles_Driven'] / 100)
df['Accels_per_100'] = df['Rapid_Accels'] / (df['Miles_Driven'] / 100)

# Calculate Multiplier and Revised Frequency
df['Multiplier'] = np.exp(beta_1 * df['Brakes_per_100'] + beta_2 * df['Accels_per_100'])
df['Revised_Frequency'] = lambda_0 * df['Multiplier']

# Map to a 0-100 Score (Inverse of Risk Multiplier, scaled)
max_mult = df['Multiplier'].max()
min_mult = df['Multiplier'].min()
df['Driving_Score'] = 100 - ((df['Multiplier'] - 1) * 50)  # Simplified scaling
df['Driving_Score'] = df['Driving_Score'].clip(0, 100)

print(df[['Driver_ID', 'Multiplier', 'Revised_Frequency', 'Driving_Score']].round(4))

6. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Common Modeling Pitfalls
  • Confusing Correlation with Causation in Telematics: Late-night driving correlates with higher accident rates, but penalizing night shift workers (e.g., nurses) can lead to unfair bias and disparate impact.
  • Ignoring Right-Censoring in CLV: When estimating customer retention, using simple logistic regression instead of survival analysis ignores the fact that active customers are right-censored, underestimating true lifetime value.
  • Basis Risk in Parametric Insurance: Assuming the index perfectly matches the loss. If the weather station is 50 miles away from the farm, the index might trigger no payout despite the farm suffering a localized drought (basis risk).
  • Data Leakage in Fraud Models: Including features derived after the fraud investigation concluded (e.g., "investigator_flag") as predictors in the training data, resulting in falsely optimistic model accuracy.

7. Industry Application Box

Application: AI in Claims Triage at Global Insurers

Top-tier auto insurers employ "touchless claims" workflows. When a policyholder submits a photo of auto damage via a mobile app, a computer vision model immediately segments the image to identify damaged car parts. Simultaneously, NLP models scan the First Notice of Loss (FNOL) text for injury keywords. If the damage is superficial and no injuries are detected, the system bypasses human adjusters entirely, estimating repair costs via API integration with local body shops and settling the claim in minutes. This drastically reduces Loss Adjustment Expenses (LAE) and improves customer satisfaction.

8. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Summary & Formulas

Data analytics expands the actuarial toolkit beyond tabular GLMs into unstructured data, geospatial analysis, and dynamic pricing.

  • Telematics Pricing Multiplier: \( M = \exp(\sum \beta_j X_j) \) where \( X_j \) are event frequencies per unit distance/time.
  • Spatial GLM: \( \mathbf{y} = \rho \mathbf{W} \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \)
  • TF-IDF: \( \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \log(N / \text{df}_t) \)
  • CLV (Discrete Time): \( \text{CLV} = \sum_{t=1}^{T} S(t) \cdot \text{Margin}_t / (1+d)^t \)
  • Parametric Pure Premium: \( \pi = \int L(x) f(x) dx \)

\( \text{CLV} = 173.08 + 147.93 + 124.46 = \$445.47 \).
Solution to 7:
Payout \( L(W) = \min(100000, \max(0, 5000(W - 100))) \).
Max payout is reached when \( 5000(W - 100) \ge 100000 \implies W - 100 \ge 20 \implies W \ge 120 \).
\( W \sim U(80, 130) \), so \( f(w) = 1/50 \).
\( \pi = \int_{100}^{120} 5000(w - 100) \frac{1}{50} dw + \int_{120}^{130} 100000 \frac{1}{50} dw \)
\( \pi = 100 \left[ \frac{(w-100)^2}{2} \right]_{100}^{120} + 2000(130 - 120) \)
\( \pi = 100 (400 / 2) + 20000 = 20000 + 20000 = \$40,000 \).
Solution to 9:
Let \( Z = (T - \mu)/\sigma \). The integral is \( \int_{T_0}^{\infty} k(t - T_0) \frac{1}{\sigma} \phi(\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}) dt \).
Using standard truncation formulas:
\( \E[P(T)] = k [ (\mu - T_0)(1 - \Phi(z_0)) + \sigma \phi(z_0) ] \), where \( z_0 = \frac{T_0 - \mu}{\sigma} \).
Solution to 10:
\( S(t) = e^{-\lambda t} \). Median \( = 4 \implies e^{-4\lambda} = 0.5 \implies \lambda = \ln(2)/4 \approx 0.1733 \).
Continuous CLV = \( \int_{0}^{\infty} C e^{-\lambda t} e^{-\delta t} dt = C \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-(\lambda + \delta)t} dt = \frac{C}{\lambda + \delta} \).

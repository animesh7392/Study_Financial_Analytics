3. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you run an airline in India. Fuel is your largest variable cost. If oil prices surge, your profit margins vanish. You could lock in a price today using a forward contract (as seen in Chapter 14), but if oil prices plummet, you are still bound to pay the higher locked-in price, watching your unhedged competitors slash ticket prices and steal your market share.

What you really want is insurance: the right, but not the obligation, to buy fuel at a maximum price. If oil drops, you abandon the insurance and buy at the market rate. This asymmetrical payoff—limiting downside risk while retaining upside potential—is the essence of an option. However, this insurance isn't free; the seller of the option takes on the downside risk and requires an upfront premium. The central question of quantitative finance over the last half-century has been: What is the fair value of that premium?

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Option Basics

Definition 15.1: Calls and Puts

A call option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy the underlying asset \(S\) at a specified strike price \(K\) on or before an expiration date \(T\).

A put option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to sell the underlying asset at the strike price \(K\) on or before expiration \(T\).

Options are further classified by exercise style:

  • European Options: Can only be exercised exactly at maturity \(t = T\).
  • American Options: Can be exercised at any time \(t \le T\).

The payoff of a European call at maturity is \( \max(S_T - K, 0) \), and for a put is \( \max(K - S_T, 0) \).

An option's value consists of two parts: Intrinsic Value (the payoff if exercised immediately, e.g., \(\max(S_t - K, 0)\) for a call) and Time Value (the premium paid for the probability that the option will move deeper in the money before expiry).

Moneyness describes the relationship between \(S\) and \(K\):

  • In-the-Money (ITM): Positive intrinsic value. Call: \(S > K\). Put: \(S < K\).
  • At-the-Money (ATM): \(S \approx K\).
  • Out-of-the-Money (OTM): Zero intrinsic value. Call: \(S < K\). Put: \(S > K\).

4.2 Put-Call Parity and Option Bounds

Model-free pricing relies on the principle of no-arbitrage.

Theorem 15.1: Put-Call Parity (European)

For European options on a non-dividend-paying stock, the prices of a call \(c\) and a put \(p\) with the same strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) are related by:

\[ c - p = S_0 - K e^{-rT} \]
Proof by No-Arbitrage

Consider two portfolios at \(t=0\):

  • Portfolio A: Buy one call (\(c\)) and invest \(K e^{-rT}\) in a risk-free bond.
  • Portfolio B: Buy one put (\(p\)) and one share of stock (\(S_0\)).

At maturity \(T\), the bond is worth \(K\). The payoff of A is \( \max(S_T - K, 0) + K = \max(S_T, K) \).

The payoff of B is \( \max(K - S_T, 0) + S_T = \max(K, S_T) \).

Since the payoffs are identical in all states of the world, their costs at \(t=0\) must be equal: \(c + K e^{-rT} = p + S_0\).

If the stock pays discrete dividends with present value \(D\), the parity becomes \( c - p = S_0 - D - K e^{-rT} \).

Option Bounds

For European calls (\(c\)) and puts (\(p\)) on non-dividend stocks:

  • Call Bounds: \( \max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, 0) \le c \le S_0 \)
  • Put Bounds: \( \max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0, 0) \le p \le Ke^{-rT} \)

For American options (\(C\) and \(P\)):

  • American Call: \( C \ge \max(S_0 - K, 0) \). In fact, an American call on a non-dividend paying stock should never be exercised early. Thus, \( C = c \).
  • American Put: Early exercise can be optimal (especially if \(S\) falls close to 0, capturing \(K\) now rather than later). Thus \( P \ge p \), and \( P \ge \max(K - S_0, 0) \).

4.3 The Binomial Model

The binomial model assumes the stock price can only move to two possible states over a small time interval \(\Delta t\): up to \(S_u = S_0 u\) or down to \(S_d = S_0 d\).

Theorem 15.2: One-Period Binomial Pricing

The price of a derivative \(f\) is given by the risk-neutral expectation discounted at the risk-free rate:

\[ f = e^{-r\Delta t} [ p f_u + (1-p) f_d ] \]

where the risk-neutral probability of an up-move is:

\[ p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d} \]
Derivation via Replicating Portfolio

Construct a portfolio \(\Pi\) of \(\Delta\) shares of stock and \(B\) risk-free bonds to replicate the option payoff.

At \(t=\Delta t\):

  • If Up: \(\Delta S_0 u + B e^{r\Delta t} = f_u\)
  • If Down: \(\Delta S_0 d + B e^{r\Delta t} = f_d\)

Solving this system yields:

\[ \Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_0(u - d)} \quad \text{and} \quad B = e^{-r\Delta t} \frac{u f_d - d f_u}{u - d} \]

By no-arbitrage, the option price today must be the cost of the replicating portfolio: \( f = \Delta S_0 + B \).

Substituting \(\Delta\) and \(B\) and rearranging gives \( f = e^{-r\Delta t} [ p f_u + (1-p) f_d ] \).

For multi-period models, we build a tree of stock prices. The option is priced by backward induction starting from the terminal payoffs at \(T\). For American options, at each node, the value is \(\max(\text{Intrinsic Value}, \text{Continuation Value})\).

4.4 The Black-Scholes-Merton Model

Assume the stock price follows a Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM):

\[ \dd S_t = \mu S_t \dd t + \sigma S_t \dd W_t \]

where \(W_t\) is a standard Brownian motion under the real-world measure \(\mathbb{P}\).

Derivation 1: The PDE Approach (Delta-Hedging)

Let \(V(t, S)\) be the price of an option. Construct a portfolio \(\Pi = -V + \Delta S\).

The change in the portfolio value over \(\dd t\) is \( \dd \Pi = -\dd V + \Delta \dd S \).

Applying Itô's Lemma from Chapter 8 to \(V(t, S)\):

\[ \dd V = \left( \pdv{V}{t} + \mu S \pdv{V}{S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} \right) \dd t + \sigma S \pdv{V}{S} \dd W_t \]

Substitute \(\dd V\) and \(\dd S\) into \(\dd \Pi\):

\[ \dd \Pi = \left( -\pdv{V}{t} - \mu S \pdv{V}{S} - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \Delta \mu S \right) \dd t + \left( -\sigma S \pdv{V}{S} + \Delta \sigma S \right) \dd W_t \]

To eliminate the random risk (\(\dd W_t\)), we choose \(\Delta = \pdv{V}{S}\). The portfolio is now riskless, so by no-arbitrage it must earn the risk-free rate: \( \dd \Pi = r \Pi \dd t = r(-V + \Delta S)\dd t \).

Equating the \(\dd t\) terms:

\[ -\pdv{V}{t} - \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} = r\left(-V + S\pdv{V}{S}\right) \]

Rearranging yields the famous Black-Scholes PDE:

\[ \pdv{V}{t} + r S \pdv{V}{S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0 \]

This PDE is solved subject to the terminal condition \(V(T, S) = \max(S-K, 0)\) for a call.

Derivation 2: Risk-Neutral Expectation

Under the risk-neutral measure \(\mathbb{Q}\), the stock earns the risk-free rate \(r\):

\[ \dd S_t = r S_t \dd t + \sigma S_t \dd W_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \]

By Itô's Lemma on \(\ln(S_T)\), \(S_T\) is lognormally distributed:

\[ S_T = S_0 \exp\left( \left(r - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma \sqrt{T} Z \right), \quad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \]

The call price is the discounted expected payoff: \( c = e^{-rT} \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\max(S_T - K, 0)] \).

Evaluating this integral (which requires integrating the lognormal density over the domain where \(S_T > K\)) yields the Black-Scholes formula.

Black-Scholes Pricing Formulas

The price of a European call \(c\) and put \(p\) are:

\[ c = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) \] \[ p = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S_0 N(-d_1) \]

Where \(N(\cdot)\) is the cumulative standard normal distribution function, and:

\[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \] \[ d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \]

4.5 The Greeks

The Greeks measure the sensitivity of the option price to various parameters.

  • Delta (\(\Delta\)): \(\pdv{V}{S}\). For a call, \(\Delta_c = N(d_1) \in (0,1)\). For a put, \(\Delta_p = N(d_1) - 1 \in (-1,0)\).
  • Gamma (\(\Gamma\)): \(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\). Same for calls and puts: \(\Gamma = \frac{N'(d_1)}{S_0 \sigma \sqrt{T}}\). Maximum at-the-money.
  • Theta (\(\Theta\)): \(\pdv{V}{t}\). Time decay. Usually negative, meaning options lose value as time passes.
  • Vega (\(\mathcal{V}\)): \(\pdv{V}{\sigma}\). Sensitivity to volatility. \(\mathcal{V} = S_0 \sqrt{T} N'(d_1)\). Maximum ATM.
  • Rho (\(\rho\)): \(\pdv{V}{r}\). Sensitivity to interest rates.
  • Second-order Greeks: Vanna (\(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S \partial \sigma}\)) and Volga (\(\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial \sigma^2}\)) are crucial for FX and exotic option hedging.

4.6 Implied Volatility

The market observes the option price \(C_{\text{mkt}}\). Since \(S, K, T, r\) are known, we can invert the Black-Scholes formula to find the volatility \(\sigma_{\text{imp}}\) that sets \(C_{\text{BS}}(\sigma) = C_{\text{mkt}}\). Because no closed-form inverse exists, numerical methods like Newton-Raphson are used.

In reality, \(\sigma_{\text{imp}}\) is not constant. Plotted against strike \(K\), it forms a Volatility Smile or Skew (out-of-the-money puts often trade at higher implied vols due to crash fears). Plotted against both \(K\) and \(T\), it forms the Volatility Surface.

4.7 Options on Futures, Currencies, and Dividend Stocks

  • Merton Model (Continuous Dividends): Replace \(S_0\) with \(S_0 e^{-qT}\) in the BS formula, where \(q\) is the dividend yield.
  • Garman-Kohlhagen (Currencies): Treat the foreign risk-free rate \(r_f\) as a continuous dividend yield on the currency.
  • Black-76 (Futures Options): For an option on a futures contract \(F_0\), the futures price is a martingale under \(\mathbb{Q}\), so \(r=0\) for the drift, but the payoff is discounted by the domestic rate \(r\): \[ c = e^{-rT}[F_0 N(d_1) - K N(d_2)] \] where \( d_1 = \frac{\ln(F_0/K) + (\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \). This is extensively used in interest rate caps and floors.

5. Worked Examples

Example 15.1: One-Period Binomial Model

Problem: A stock is priced at INR 1000. In 6 months (\(T=0.5\)), it will either go up by 20% or down by 20%. The risk-free rate is 5% per annum (continuous). Price a European call with strike \(K=1050\). Find the replicating portfolio (\(\Delta\) and \(B\)).

Solution:

1. Parameters: \(S_0 = 1000, u = 1.20, d = 0.80, \Delta t = 0.5, r = 0.05, K = 1050\).

2. Terminal stock prices: \(S_u = 1200, S_d = 800\).

3. Terminal option payoffs: \(c_u = \max(1200 - 1050, 0) = 150\). \(c_d = \max(800 - 1050, 0) = 0\).

4. Risk-neutral probability: \( p = \frac{e^{0.05 \times 0.5} - 0.80}{1.20 - 0.80} = \frac{1.0253 - 0.80}{0.40} = 0.5633 \).

5. Call Price: \( c = e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} [0.5633(150) + (1-0.5633)(0)] = 0.9753 \times 84.495 = \text{INR } 82.41 \).

6. Replicating Portfolio:
\(\Delta = \frac{150 - 0}{1200 - 800} = \frac{150}{400} = 0.375\) shares.
\(B = e^{-0.025} \frac{1.2(0) - 0.8(150)}{1.2 - 0.8} = 0.9753 \times \frac{-120}{0.4} = -292.59\) (borrow INR 292.59).
Check: \(\Delta S_0 + B = 0.375(1000) - 292.59 = 375 - 292.59 = 82.41 \). Matches exactly.

Example 15.2: Black-Scholes Pricing and Greeks

Problem: Calculate the price, Delta, Gamma, and Vega of a European call. \(S_0 = \$100, K = \$105, T = 0.5 \text{ years}, r = 5\%, \sigma = 20\%\).

Solution:

1. Calculate \(d_1\) and \(d_2\):
\(d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/105) + (0.05 + 0.2^2/2) \times 0.5}{0.2\sqrt{0.5}} = \frac{-0.04879 + (0.05 + 0.02) \times 0.5}{0.1414} = \frac{-0.01379}{0.1414} = -0.0975\)
\(d_2 = d_1 - 0.1414 = -0.2389\)

2. Calculate Normals:
\(N(d_1) = N(-0.0975) = 0.4612\)
\(N(d_2) = N(-0.2389) = 0.4056\)
\(N'(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-d_1^2/2} = 0.3989 \times e^{-0.00475} = 0.3970\)

3. Price \(c\): \( c = 100(0.4612) - 105 e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} (0.4056) = 46.12 - 105(0.9753)(0.4056) = 46.12 - 41.53 = \$4.59 \).

4. Greeks:
\(\Delta = N(d_1) = 0.4612\).
\(\Gamma = \frac{N'(d_1)}{S_0 \sigma \sqrt{T}} = \frac{0.3970}{100 \times 0.2 \times 0.7071} = 0.0281\).
\(\mathcal{V} = S_0 \sqrt{T} N'(d_1) = 100 \times 0.7071 \times 0.3970 = 28.07\) (usually divided by 100 to show sensitivity to 1% change in vol, so 0.28 per 1% vol).

Example 15.3: Verifying Put-Call Parity

Problem: Using the parameters from Example 15.2, price the corresponding European put using the BSM formula, and verify Put-Call Parity.

Solution:

1. Put formula: \(p = K e^{-rT} N(-d_2) - S_0 N(-d_1)\).
\(N(-d_1) = 1 - 0.4612 = 0.5388\).
\(N(-d_2) = 1 - 0.4056 = 0.5944\).

2. Put Price: \( p = 105(0.9753)(0.5944) - 100(0.5388) = 60.87 - 53.88 = \$6.99 \).

3. Parity Check:
\(c - p = 4.59 - 6.99 = -2.40\).
\(S_0 - K e^{-rT} = 100 - 105(0.9753) = 100 - 102.40 = -2.40\).
Matches perfectly.

Example 15.4: Implied Volatility via Newton-Raphson

Problem: Suppose for the option in Example 15.2, the market price is actually $5.50. Find the implied volatility. Assume an initial guess \(\sigma_0 = 20\%\).

Solution:

The Newton-Raphson update rule is: \(\sigma_{n+1} = \sigma_n - \frac{C(\sigma_n) - C_{\text{mkt}}}{\mathcal{V}(\sigma_n)}\).

Iteration 1: \(\sigma_0 = 0.20\).
\(C(0.20) = 4.59\).
\(\mathcal{V}(0.20) = 28.07\).
\(\sigma_1 = 0.20 - \frac{4.59 - 5.50}{28.07} = 0.20 - (-0.0324) = 0.2324\) (or 23.24%).

Iteration 2: Re-calculate at \(\sigma_1 = 0.2324\).
Recalculating yields \(C(0.2324) \approx 5.48\), \(\mathcal{V}(0.2324) \approx 28.1\).
\(\sigma_2 = 0.2324 - \frac{5.48 - 5.50}{28.1} = 0.2331\).
The implied volatility is approximately 23.3%.

Example 15.5: Delta-Hedging Simulation

Problem: A market maker sells 1,000 European call options (params from 15.2) at $4.59. They immediately delta-hedge. The stock moves to $102 the next day (\(\dd t = 1/252\)). Calculate the unhedged vs hedged P&L.

Solution:

1. Initial Position: Short 1000 calls. Cash received = $4,590. Initial \(\Delta = 0.4612\).

2. Delta Hedge: Buy \(\Delta \times 1000 = 461.2\) shares. Cost = $46,120. Borrow $41,530 at 5%.

3. Day 1 (Stock = $102):
Unhedged Call P&L: New call price (at S=102, T=0.496) \(\approx \$5.67\). Loss on short calls = \((4.59 - 5.67) \times 1000 = -\$1,080\).
Hedge P&L: Gain on stock = \((102 - 100) \times 461.2 = +\$922.40\).
Interest paid: \(41530 \times 0.05 \times (1/252) = -\$8.24\).
Hedged Net P&L: \(-1080 + 922.40 - 8.24 = -\$165.84\).

Why a loss? The loss is due to Gamma. As the stock rose, delta increased, meaning we were under-hedged for the move from 100 to 102. Continuous hedging is required to perfectly replicate.

6. Python Code: Black-Scholes Pricer & Greeks

Below is a robust implementation of the BSM model in Python using `scipy.stats`.


import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

class BSMOption:
    def __init__(self, S, K, T, r, sigma, q=0.0, option_type='call'):
        self.S = float(S)
        self.K = float(K)
        self.T = float(T)
        self.r = float(r)
        self.sigma = float(sigma)
        self.q = float(q) # Continuous dividend yield
        self.type = option_type.lower()
        
        # Pre-compute d1 and d2
        self.d1 = (np.log(self.S / self.K) + (self.r - self.q + 0.5 * self.sigma**2) * self.T) / (self.sigma * np.sqrt(self.T))
        self.d2 = self.d1 - self.sigma * np.sqrt(self.T)

    def price(self):
        if self.type == 'call':
            return self.S * np.exp(-self.q * self.T) * norm.cdf(self.d1) - \
                   self.K * np.exp(-self.r * self.T) * norm.cdf(self.d2)
        elif self.type == 'put':
            return self.K * np.exp(-self.r * self.T) * norm.cdf(-self.d2) - \
                   self.S * np.exp(-self.q * self.T) * norm.cdf(-self.d1)
                   
    def delta(self):
        if self.type == 'call':
            return np.exp(-self.q * self.T) * norm.cdf(self.d1)
        else:
            return np.exp(-self.q * self.T) * (norm.cdf(self.d1) - 1)

    def gamma(self):
        return np.exp(-self.q * self.T) * norm.pdf(self.d1) / (self.S * self.sigma * np.sqrt(self.T))

    def vega(self):
        return self.S * np.exp(-self.q * self.T) * norm.pdf(self.d1) * np.sqrt(self.T)

# Example usage from 15.2
opt = BSMOption(S=100, K=105, T=0.5, r=0.05, sigma=0.20)
print(f"Price: {opt.price():.4f}")
print(f"Delta: {opt.delta():.4f}")
print(f"Gamma: {opt.gamma():.4f}")
print(f"Vega:  {opt.vega():.4f}")

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Watch Out!
  • Confusing the \(\mathbb{P}\) and \(\mathbb{Q}\) measures: The actual drift of the stock \(\mu\) does not appear in the Black-Scholes formula. Options are priced based on the risk-free rate \(r\), not the expected return of the stock.
  • American Call Early Exercise: Students often assume American calls should be exercised early to capture intrinsic value. However, exercising destroys the Time Value (the insurance). It is always better to sell the call in the open market than to exercise it early, unless there is a large dividend pending.
  • Vega Scale: Vega is mathematically the derivative with respect to \(\sigma\) in decimal form. If \(\mathcal{V}=28\), a 1% absolute increase in vol (from 20% to 21%) changes the price by \(28 \times 0.01 = 0.28\), not 28.

9. Industry Application

Options in Exotics and Structuring

At an investment bank, vanilla European options are highly commoditized and traded via algos. Human traders spend their time managing the Volatility Surface and trading Exotic options.

A common product sold to high-net-worth individuals in Asia is the Autocallable Note. To hedge this, banks must model how volatility changes with stock price (the skew). Because Autocallables embed complex barriers, the trading desk accumulates massive higher-order Greek exposures—specifically Vanna and Volga. During market crashes, these non-linear exposures can explode, causing massive trading losses if not properly hedged using a portfolio of vanilla options across multiple strikes and expiries.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Put-Call Parity: \( c - p = S_0 - K e^{-rT} \)
  • Binomial Probability: \( p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d} \)
  • Black-Scholes PDE: \( \pdv{V}{t} + r S \pdv{V}{S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0 \)
  • \(d_1, d_2\): \( d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} \)
  • Call Delta: \( \Delta = N(d_1) \)
  • Gamma: \( \Gamma = \frac{N'(d_1)}{S_0 \sigma \sqrt{T}} \)

Solution 7: Portfolio Delta \(\Delta_P = 1000 \times 1 + N \times (-0.4) = 0\).
\(N = 1000 / 0.4 = 2500\). The trader must buy 2,500 put options.

Solution 10: By put-call parity \(c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\). Differentiating both sides with respect to \(\sigma\), we get \(\pdv{c}{\sigma} - \pdv{p}{\sigma} = 0\), hence \(\mathcal{V}_c = \mathcal{V}_p\).

Chapter 34: Loss Reserving

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the structure of Claims Development Triangles and distinguish between accident year, development year, and calendar year.
  • Calculate unpaid claims reserves using deterministic methods: Chain Ladder (CL), Bornhuetter-Ferguson (BF), and Cape Cod.
  • Apply stochastic reserving frameworks, specifically Mack's Model and Bootstrap Chain Ladder, to estimate reserve variability.
  • Understand Generalized Linear Models (GLM) for reserving using over-dispersed Poisson.
  • Estimate tail factors and compare paid vs. incurred data.
  • Grasp the reserving implications under Actuarial Standards like IFRS 17 and Solvency II.

2. Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter builds directly on Chapter 33: Ratemaking, where we priced policies based on expected future losses. Understanding expected loss ratios is crucial for methods like Bornhuetter-Ferguson. A strong grasp of generalized linear models (Chapter 12) and stochastic processes (Chapter 18) is required for Mack's Model and GLM reserving.

3. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you manage a large property & casualty (P&C) insurance company. You've collected $100 million in premium this year. Over the course of the year, policyholders get into accidents, and some claims are immediately reported and paid. However, by December 31, many accidents have occurred but haven't been reported yet (Incurred But Not Reported - IBNR). Additionally, claims that have been reported might be more expensive than initially estimated (Incurred But Not Enough Reported - IBNER). If you simply wait for all claims to settle before setting aside money, you might distribute dividends to shareholders prematurely and go bankrupt when the final bills arrive years later.

Loss reserving is the actuary's time machine. We use historical patterns of how claims are reported and paid over time to predict the final ultimate cost of claims that have already occurred. This "unpaid claim estimate" or "reserve" is the largest liability on a P&C insurer's balance sheet, often dwarfing shareholders' equity. Small errors in reserving can turn reported profits into massive losses, triggering regulatory intervention.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Claims Development Triangle

Data for loss reserving is typically organized in a run-off triangle or development triangle. Let \( i \in \{1, 2, \dots, I\} \) denote the origin period (e.g., accident year) and \( j \in \{1, 2, \dots, J\} \) denote the development period (e.g., development year), with \( I = J \) typically representing the current evaluation date.

  • Accident Year (\(i\)): The year the loss event occurred.
  • Development Year (\(j\)): The number of years since the accident year.
  • Calendar Year (\(i + j - 1\)): The year the payment or evaluation was made. For the current evaluation date, \( i + j - 1 = I \).

Let \( C_{i,j} \) be the cumulative claims paid (or incurred) for accident year \( i \) evaluated at the end of development year \( j \). The incremental claims are \( X_{i,j} = C_{i,j} - C_{i,j-1} \) (with \( C_{i,0} = 0 \)). The observable data is the upper triangle: \( \mathcal{D}_I = \{ C_{i,j} : i+j-1 \le I \} \). The goal is to predict the lower triangle: \( \{ C_{i,j} : i+j-1 > I \} \) and ultimately the final value \( C_{i,J} \).

4.2 Chain Ladder (CL) Method

Definition: Chain Ladder Method

The Chain Ladder method assumes that the proportional development of claims from one period to the next is consistent across origin periods. The expected value of future cumulative claims is proportional to the current cumulative claims.

We compute historical age-to-age factors (or link ratios) for \( j = 1, \dots, J-1 \): \[ f_{i,j} = \frac{C_{i,j+1}}{C_{i,j}} \] The selected development factor \( f_j \) is typically an average of historical \( f_{i,j} \). Common selections include:

  • Volume-weighted average: \( \hat{f}_j = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{I-j} C_{i,j+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{I-j} C_{i,j}} \)
  • Simple average: \( \hat{f}_j = \frac{1}{I-j} \sum_{i=1}^{I-j} f_{i,j} \)
  • Last 3 or 5 years: Averages restricted to recent calendar periods to reflect changing trends.

The ultimate claims for accident year \( i \) are projected as: \[ \hat{C}_{i,J} = C_{i, I-i+1} \prod_{k=I-i+1}^{J-1} \hat{f}_k \] The IBNR (in a broad sense, meaning total unpaid claims) is: \[ \text{IBNR}_i = \hat{C}_{i,J} - C_{i, I-i+1} \]

4.3 Bornhuetter-Ferguson (BF) Method

The Chain Ladder method is highly sensitive to the current cumulative claims \( C_{i, I-i+1} \), especially for recent accident years where development is immature. The Bornhuetter-Ferguson method introduces an a priori estimate of ultimate claims, \( \mu_i \), often derived from a prior expected loss ratio (ELR) multiplied by earned premium \( P_i \).

Theorem: BF IBNR Formula

Let \( \text{CDF}_j = \prod_{k=j}^{J-1} \hat{f}_k \) be the cumulative development factor from age \( j \) to ultimate. The proportion of ultimate claims expected to emerge after evaluation age \( j \) is \( q_j = 1 - \frac{1}{\text{CDF}_j} \). The BF reserve for accident year \( i \) evaluated at age \( j = I-i+1 \) is:

\[ \text{IBNR}_i^{\text{BF}} = \mu_i \times \left(1 - \frac{1}{\text{CDF}_{I-i+1}}\right) \]

The total projected ultimate claims under BF are: \( \hat{C}_{i,J}^{\text{BF}} = C_{i, I-i+1} + \text{IBNR}_i^{\text{BF}} \).

4.4 Cape Cod Method

The Cape Cod (or Stanard-Bühlmann) method is a variant of BF that estimates a single overall Expected Loss Ratio (ELR) from the data rather than relying on an external a priori estimate. It introduces the concept of "used-up premium" \( P_i / \text{CDF}_{I-i+1} \).

The overall ELR is estimated as: \[ \text{ELR}_{\text{CC}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{I} C_{i, I-i+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{I} P_i / \text{CDF}_{I-i+1}} \] The a priori ultimate for year \( i \) is then \( \mu_i = P_i \times \text{ELR}_{\text{CC}} \), which is then plugged into the standard BF formula.

4.5 Mack's Model (Stochastic Chain Ladder)

Thomas Mack (1993) formalized the implicit statistical assumptions of the volume-weighted Chain Ladder method, allowing for the calculation of the standard error of reserves.

Definition: Mack's Assumptions
  1. \(\E[C_{i,j+1} \mid C_{i,1}, \dots, C_{i,j}] = f_j C_{i,j} \)
  2. \(\Var(C_{i,j+1} \mid C_{i,1}, \dots, C_{i,j}) = \sigma_j^2 C_{i,j} \)
  3. Accident years \( \{C_{i,1}, \dots, C_{i,J}\} \) are independent for different \( i \).

Under these assumptions, the volume-weighted \(\hat{f}_j\) is an unbiased estimator for \( f_j \). The variance parameters \( \sigma_j^2 \) are estimated as: \[ \hat{\sigma}_j^2 = \frac{1}{I-j-1} \sum_{i=1}^{I-j} C_{i,j} \left( \frac{C_{i,j+1}}{C_{i,j}} - \hat{f}_j \right)^2 \] The Mean Squared Error of Prediction (MSEP) for a single accident year \( i \) decomposes into process variance (randomness of claims) and estimation variance (uncertainty in \(\hat{f}_j\)): \[ \text{MSEP}(\hat{C}_{i,J}) \approx \hat{C}_{i,J}^2 \sum_{k=I-i+1}^{J-1} \frac{\hat{\sigma}_k^2}{\hat{f}_k^2} \left( \frac{1}{\hat{C}_{i,k}} + \frac{1}{\sum_{m=1}^{I-k} C_{m,k}} \right) \]

4.6 Bootstrap Chain Ladder

Instead of relying on analytical variance approximations like Mack, bootstrapping simulates the full predictive distribution of reserves. The Over-Dispersed Poisson (ODP) bootstrap relies on Pearson residuals.

Let \( m_{i,j} \) be the expected incremental claims. The unscaled Pearson residuals are: \[ r_{i,j} = \frac{X_{i,j} - m_{i,j}}{\sqrt{m_{i,j}}} \] The bootstrap procedure: 1. Fit a model (like GLM or CL) to get fitted incrementals \( \hat{m}_{i,j} \) and residuals \( r_{i,j} \). 2. Resample residuals with replacement to get \( r^*_{i,j} \). 3. Create a pseudo-triangle of past claims: \( X^*_{i,j} = \hat{m}_{i,j} + r^*_{i,j} \sqrt{\hat{m}_{i,j}} \). 4. Project the pseudo-triangle to ultimate using CL to get process means. 5. Add process variance (e.g., using Gamma or Poisson random variables) to get simulated future payments.

4.7 GLM Reserving

The Chain Ladder method is mathematically equivalent to fitting a Generalized Linear Model to incremental claims \( X_{i,j} \) assuming an Over-Dispersed Poisson (ODP) distribution with a logarithmic link function. The model incorporates row (accident year) and column (development year) factors: \[ \E[X_{i,j}] = \mu_{i,j}, \quad \Var(X_{i,j}) = \phi \mu_{i,j} \] \[ \ln(\mu_{i,j}) = \eta + \alpha_i + \beta_j \] Where \( \alpha_1 = \beta_1 = 0 \) for identifiability. If we use Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) or quasi-likelihood, the fitted marginal totals equal the observed marginal totals, matching exactly the volume-weighted CL ultimates.

4.8 Paid vs Incurred Analysis

Reserving analysis typically utilizes both paid claims and incurred claims (Paid + Case Reserves).

  • Paid Data: Objective, not subject to case reserve adequacy changes, but slow to develop and volatile.
  • Incurred Data: Develops faster, less volatile, but sensitive to changes in company reserving philosophy (e.g., strengthening case reserves).

Actuaries must check for consistency between paid and incurred projections. A growing gap between the two suggests a change in settlement rates or case reserve adequacy.

4.9 Tail Factors

Often, claims are not fully settled at the end of the observed triangle (period \( J \)). We must apply a tail factor to extrapolate development beyond \( J \). Common methods for estimating tail factors include curve fitting to the observed \( \hat{f}_j \):

  • Exponential Decay: \( \hat{f}_j - 1 = a \cdot e^{-b j} \)
  • Inverse Power Curve: \( \hat{f}_j - 1 = a \cdot j^{-b} \)
The tail factor is \( f_{\text{tail}} = \prod_{j=J}^{\infty} f_j \).

4.10 Actuarial Standards: IFRS 17 and Solvency II

Modern reserving extends beyond point estimates to encompass risk margins and discounting.

  • IFRS 17: Requires discounting of future cash flows and an explicit Risk Adjustment for Non-Financial Risk. Models like Building Block Approach (BBA) or Premium Allocation Approach (PAA) are used. The Contractual Service Margin (CSM) represents unearned profit.
  • Solvency II: Technical Provisions equal Best Estimate Liabilities (discounted probability-weighted average of future cash flows) plus a Risk Margin (cost of capital approach using a 6% rate).

For AY1: \( f_{1,1} = 150/100 = 1.500 \), \( f_{1,2} = 175/150 = 1.167 \), \( f_{1,3} = 180/175 = 1.029 \), \( f_{1,4} = 185/180 = 1.028 \).

For AY2: \( f_{2,1} = 168/110 = 1.527 \), \( f_{2,2} = 192/168 = 1.143 \), \( f_{2,3} = 205/192 = 1.068 \).

For AY3: \( f_{3,1} = 176/115 = 1.530 \), \( f_{3,2} = 210/176 = 1.193 \).

For AY4: \( f_{4,1} = 190/125 = 1.520 \).

Step 2: Volume-weighted averages \( \hat{f}_j \)

\( \hat{f}_1 = \frac{150+168+176+190}{100+110+115+125} = \frac{684}{450} = 1.520 \)

\( \hat{f}_2 = \frac{175+192+210}{150+168+176} = \frac{577}{494} = 1.168 \)

\( \hat{f}_3 = \frac{180+205}{175+192} = \frac{385}{367} = 1.049 \)

\( \hat{f}_4 = \frac{185}{180} = 1.028 \)

Step 3: Cumulative Development Factors to Ultimate (CDF)

\( \text{CDF}_4 = 1.028 \)

\( \text{CDF}_3 = 1.049 \times 1.028 = 1.078 \)

\( \text{CDF}_2 = 1.168 \times 1.078 = 1.259 \)

\( \text{CDF}_1 = 1.520 \times 1.259 = 1.914 \)

Step 4: Ultimate Claims and IBNR

AY2: Ultimate = \( 205 \times 1.028 = 210.74 \). IBNR = \( 210.74 - 205 = 5.74 \).

AY3: Ultimate = \( 210 \times 1.078 = 226.38 \). IBNR = \( 226.38 - 210 = 16.38 \).

AY4: Ultimate = \( 190 \times 1.259 = 239.21 \). IBNR = \( 239.21 - 190 = 49.21 \).

AY5: Ultimate = \( 140 \times 1.914 = 267.96 \). IBNR = \( 267.96 - 140 = 127.96 \).

Total IBNR = 199.29 ('000s INR).

Example 34.2: Bornhuetter-Ferguson Method

Using the data from Example 34.1, assume earned premiums for AY3, AY4, and AY5 are 350, 380, and 420 ('000s INR) respectively. The a priori expected loss ratio is 65%. Find the BF IBNR.

Step 1: Expected Ultimate Claims (\( \mu_i \))

AY3: \( \mu_3 = 350 \times 0.65 = 227.5 \)

AY4: \( \mu_4 = 380 \times 0.65 = 247.0 \)

AY5: \( \mu_5 = 420 \times 0.65 = 273.0 \)

Step 2: Unpaid Proportion (\( 1 - 1/\text{CDF} \))

AY3 (Age 3): \( 1 - 1/1.078 = 0.0724 \)

AY4 (Age 2): \( 1 - 1/1.259 = 0.2057 \)

AY5 (Age 1): \( 1 - 1/1.914 = 0.4775 \)

Step 3: BF IBNR

AY3: \( 227.5 \times 0.0724 = 16.47 \)

AY4: \( 247.0 \times 0.2057 = 50.81 \)

AY5: \( 273.0 \times 0.4775 = 130.36 \)

Total BF IBNR for these years = 197.64. The BF method produces slightly different results than CL, relying heavily on the 65% ELR assumption.

Example 34.3: Mack's Model Standard Error

For development year 1 to 2 in Example 34.1, calculate the estimated variance parameter \( \hat{\sigma}_1^2 \).

\( \hat{f}_1 = 1.520 \).

\( \hat{\sigma}_1^2 = \frac{1}{4 - 1 - 1} \left[ 100(1.500 - 1.520)^2 + 110(1.527 - 1.520)^2 + 115(1.530 - 1.520)^2 + 125(1.520 - 1.520)^2 \right] \)

\( = \frac{1}{2} \left[ 100(-0.020)^2 + 110(0.007)^2 + 115(0.010)^2 + 0 \right] \)

\( = \frac{1}{2} \left[ 0.040 + 0.00539 + 0.0115 \right] = 0.028445 \)

This \( \hat{\sigma}_1^2 \) is then used in Mack's MSEP formula to quantify the variability in the projection for the youngest accident year (AY5).

Example 34.4: Bootstrap Predictive Distribution

An actuary runs an Over-Dispersed Poisson bootstrap on a large triangle. They generate 1,000 simulated triangles and sum the total unpaid claims for each. The resulting set of 1,000 totals has a mean of $12,500,000 and a standard deviation of $1,200,000. The 5th percentile is $10,800,000 and the 95th percentile is $14,700,000.

Conclusion: The actuary can report a point estimate of $12.5M. If management desires a risk margin to be 95% confident that reserves are adequate, they should set aside $14.7M (a risk margin of $2.2M above the central estimate).

Example 34.5: Cape Cod ELR Calculation

Using the data from Examples 34.1 and 34.2 (AY3-5). We need "Used-up Premium".

AY3: Premium = 350. \( 1/\text{CDF}_3 = 1/1.078 = 0.9276 \). Used-up Premium = \( 350 \times 0.9276 = 324.66 \).

AY4: Premium = 380. \( 1/\text{CDF}_2 = 1/1.259 = 0.7943 \). Used-up Premium = \( 380 \times 0.7943 = 301.83 \).

AY5: Premium = 420. \( 1/\text{CDF}_1 = 1/1.914 = 0.5225 \). Used-up Premium = \( 420 \times 0.5225 = 219.45 \).

Total Used-up Premium (AY3-5) = 845.94. Total known claims = \( 210 + 190 + 140 = 540 \).

Cape Cod ELR = \( 540 / 845.94 = 63.8\% \).

The Cape Cod method would then use this 63.8% ELR in place of the 65% a priori ELR used in the standard BF method.

6. Python Code


import numpy as np

# Cumulative triangle (0 for missing lower triangle)
triangle = np.array([
    [100, 150, 175, 180, 185],
    [110, 168, 192, 205,   0],
    [115, 176, 210,   0,   0],
    [125, 190,   0,   0,   0],
    [140,   0,   0,   0,   0]
], dtype=float)

I, J = triangle.shape
f_factors = []

# Calculate volume weighted age-to-age factors
for j in range(J - 1):
    sum_c_j = 0
    sum_c_j1 = 0
    for i in range(I - 1 - j):
        sum_c_j += triangle[i, j]
        sum_c_j1 += triangle[i, j + 1]
    f = sum_c_j1 / sum_c_j
    f_factors.append(f)

print(f"Age-to-age factors: {[round(f, 3) for f in f_factors]}")

# Square the triangle
sq_triangle = np.copy(triangle)
for i in range(1, I):
    for j in range(I - i, J):
        sq_triangle[i, j] = sq_triangle[i, j - 1] * f_factors[j - 1]

print("Squared Triangle:\n", np.round(sq_triangle, 1))

# Calculate IBNR
ibnr = 0
for i in range(1, I):
    latest_observed = triangle[i, I - i - 1]
    ultimate = sq_triangle[i, J - 1]
    ibnr += (ultimate - latest_observed)
    
print(f"Total CL IBNR: {round(ibnr, 2)}")

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Confusing Paid vs Incurred IBNR: IBNR derived from paid data includes both true "not reported" claims and case reserve development (IBNER). IBNR from incurred data only covers true IBNR (assuming case reserves are perfectly adequate).
  • Ignoring Calendar Year Effects: The standard Chain Ladder method assumes columns (development years) and rows (accident years) are independent. It fails to explicitly capture diagonal effects, such as a sudden spike in claims inflation affecting all accident years in a specific calendar year.
  • Over-relying on CL for young years: The Chain Ladder method projects an enormous amount of claims based on a tiny early observation. The BF method is much safer for immature accident years.
9. Industry Application: Reserving at a Global Insurer

At a large multinational insurer, loss reserving is not just about mathematical formulas; it's a critical corporate governance process. Actuarial teams perform detailed reviews every quarter. They segment data into hundreds of homogeneous reserving classes (e.g., UK Commercial Motor, US Workers' Compensation). They apply CL, BF, and Cape Cod to both Paid and Incurred data, creating multiple views of the ultimate.

The selected ultimate is rarely a pure mechanical output. Actuaries apply "expert judgment" to select development factors, often holding meetings with claims adjusters and underwriters to understand operational changes (e.g., "Did we speed up claims processing this year?"). Finally, the Chief Actuary must sign off on the reserves, and external auditors rigorously review the methodology and assumptions to prevent earnings manipulation.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Volume-weighted link ratio: \( \hat{f}_j = \frac{\sum_{i} C_{i,j+1}}{\sum_{i} C_{i,j}} \)
  • Chain Ladder Ultimate: \( \hat{C}_{i,J} = C_{i, I-i+1} \prod_{k=I-i+1}^{J-1} \hat{f}_k \)
  • Cumulative Development Factor (CDF): \( \text{CDF}_j = \prod_{k=j}^{J-1} \hat{f}_k \)
  • Proportion Unpaid: \( q_j = 1 - \frac{1}{\text{CDF}_j} \)
  • Bornhuetter-Ferguson IBNR: \( \text{IBNR}_i^{\text{BF}} = \mu_i \times q_{I-i+1} \)
  • Cape Cod ELR: \( \text{ELR}_{\text{CC}} = \frac{\sum C_{i, I-i+1}}{\sum P_i / \text{CDF}_{I-i+1}} \)

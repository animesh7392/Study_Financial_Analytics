17.3 Intuition & Motivation

Imagine a regional bank, "Springfield Savings & Loan", that issues 30-year fixed-rate mortgages to its customers at 4.5%. To fund these loans, the bank borrows short-term on the interbank market (e.g., rolling over 3-month deposits) paying a floating rate based on SOFR (Secured Overnight Financing Rate).

This creates a classic asset-liability mismatch. If short-term interest rates spike to 6%, the bank is paying 6% on its liabilities while earning only 4.5% on its assets. The bank faces catastrophic interest rate risk. How can it protect itself? It could buy an interest rate cap—a derivative that pays off if the floating rate exceeds a certain strike (e.g., 5%). Alternatively, it could use an interest rate swap to convert its floating liabilities to fixed, but perhaps it wants the flexibility to benefit if rates fall. What if it wants an option to enter a swap in the future? That's a swaption.

The valuation of these derivatives is notoriously complex because interest rates are not simple traded assets. An equity option involves one stock price. An interest rate option involves the entire yield curve evolving over time. Furthermore, unlike a stock price which can theoretically go to infinity, interest rates exhibit mean reversion. In recent years, they have even gone negative, forcing a paradigm shift from log-normal (Black) models to normal (Bachelier) or shifted log-normal models.

17.4 Formal Theory

17.4.1 Interest Rate Caps and Floors

An interest rate cap is a contract that provides a payoff if a specified reference rate (e.g., SOFR, EURIBOR, or traditionally LIBOR) exceeds a predetermined strike rate \( K \) on a series of future dates. A cap is mathematically equivalent to a portfolio of individual options on the interest rate, known as caplets.

Definition: Caplet and Floorlet

A caplet covering the period \([T_{k-1}, T_k]\) with strike \( K \), notional \( N \), and day-count fraction \(\delta_k\) pays out at time \( T_k \): \[ \text{Caplet Payoff}_{T_k} = N \delta_k \max(R(T_{k-1}, T_k) - K, 0) \] where \( R(T_{k-1}, T_k) \) is the reference floating rate fixed at \( T_{k-1} \) for the period to \( T_k \).

A floorlet pays out if the rate is below \( K \): \[ \text{Floorlet Payoff}_{T_k} = N \delta_k \max(K - R(T_{k-1}, T_k), 0) \]

Note the timing mismatch: the rate is observed (reset) at \( T_{k-1} \), but the payoff is received at the end of the accrual period \( T_k \). By shifting our numéraire to the zero-coupon bond \( P(t, T_k) \), we can price this under the \( T_k \)-forward measure.

Theorem: Black's Formula for Caplets

Assuming the forward rate \( F_k(t) \) for the period \([T_{k-1}, T_k]\) follows a geometric Brownian motion with volatility \( \sigma_k \), the value of a caplet at time \( t=0 \) is given by Black's Formula (Black 1976): \[ \text{Caplet}(0) = N \delta_k P(0, T_k) \left[ F_k(0) N(d_1) - K N(d_2) \right] \] where: \[ d_1 = \frac{\ln(F_k(0)/K) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_k^2 T_{k-1}}{\sigma_k \sqrt{T_{k-1}}} \] \[ d_2 = d_1 - \sigma_k \sqrt{T_{k-1}} \]

A floorlet is priced as: \[ \text{Floorlet}(0) = N \delta_k P(0, T_k) \left[ K N(-d_2) - F_k(0) N(-d_1) \right] \]

Cap-Floor Parity: Buying a cap and selling a floor with the same strike is equivalent to entering a payer forward rate agreement (or a swap, if it's a strip). \[ \text{Cap}(0) - \text{Floor}(0) = \text{Value of Payer Swap} \]

17.4.2 Swaptions

A swaption is an option to enter into an interest rate swap at a future date \( T \) at a specified fixed rate \( K \). A payer swaption gives the right to pay fixed and receive floating; a receiver swaption gives the right to receive fixed and pay floating.

Using the annuity factor \( A(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_i P(t, T_i) \) as the numéraire, the forward swap rate \( S(t) \) is a martingale under the corresponding swap measure. Applying Black's formula:

Theorem: Black's Formula for Swaptions

The price of a payer swaption expiring at \( T_0 \) on a swap with payment dates \( T_1, \dots, T_n \), strike \( K \), and forward swap rate \( S_0 \) is: \[ \text{Payer Swaption}(0) = N \cdot A(0) \left[ S_0 N(d_1) - K N(d_2) \right] \] where \( d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 T_0}{\sigma \sqrt{T_0}} \) and \( d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T_0} \).

Because caps and swaptions are both actively traded, there is often a tension in calibrating models to match both sets of implied volatilities simultaneously.

17.4.3 The Normal (Bachelier) Model for Negative Rates

When interest rates approach zero or turn negative (as they did in Europe and Japan), the log-normal Black model fails because \( \ln(S_0/K) \) is undefined for negative values. The market shifted to the Bachelier (Normal) Model, assuming rates follow arithmetic Brownian motion: \( dF = \sigma_N dW \).

The Bachelier pricing formula for a payer swaption is: \[ \text{Payer Swaption}_N(0) = N \cdot A(0) \sigma_N \sqrt{T_0} \left[ d N(d) + n(d) \right] \] where \( d = \frac{S_0 - K}{\sigma_N \sqrt{T_0}} \), and \( n(x) \) is the standard normal PDF.

17.4.4 LIBOR Market Model (BGM)

The Brace-Gatarek-Musiela (BGM) model models observable forward rates directly, rather than abstract instantaneous short rates (like Vasicek). The forward rate \( F_k(t) \) under its own \( T_k \)-forward measure is driftless: \( dF_k(t) = \sigma_k(t) F_k(t) dW^k(t) \).

Under the terminal measure (numéraire \( P(t, T_N) \)), all forward rates have a drift induced by their correlation with other forward rates. BGM is powerful because it can exactly match the market prices of caps (and approximate swaptions) by adjusting the volatility functions and correlation matrix of the forward rates, making it the industry standard for exotic interest rate products.

The price of a call option expiring at \( T \) on a zero-coupon bond maturing at \( S \) (\( T < S \)) with strike \( K \): \[ C(t) = P(t,S) N(h_1) - K P(t,T) N(h_2) \] where \( \sigma_P \) is the volatility of the zero-coupon bond price, and \[ h_1 = \frac{\ln(P(t,S) / (K P(t,T))) + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_P^2}{\sigma_P} \]

17.4.6 Structured Products & MBS

Structured products combine vanilla bonds and derivatives to create custom payoff profiles.

  • Callable Bond: Non-callable (bullet) bond \( - \) Receiver Swaption. The issuer holds the option to recall the debt, effectively buying a call option on the bond (which is equivalent to an option to enter a receive-fixed swap).
  • Inverse Floater: A bond that pays a coupon like \( 15\% - 2 \times \text{SOFR} \). High leverage when rates fall. Capped at a minimum of 0% (embedded floor).
  • Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS): Pools of residential mortgages. Homeowners have a "prepayment option"—they can refinance if rates drop. Therefore, an MBS is equivalent to a regular bond short a call option held by the homeowners. This creates negative convexity: as rates fall, the duration shortens exactly when investors want it to lengthen. Prepayment is modeled using CPR (Conditional Prepayment Rate) and the PSA model.

17.5 Worked Examples

Example 17.1: Price a Caplet using Black's Formula

Problem: Price a single SOFR caplet. Current time \( t=0 \). The caplet fixes at \( T_1 = 1.0 \) year and pays at \( T_2 = 1.25 \) years (quarterly, \(\delta = 0.25\)). The strike \( K = 4.00\% \). The forward rate \( F(0, T_1, T_2) = 4.50\% \). The log-normal volatility \(\sigma = 20\%\). The discount factor \( P(0, 1.25) = 0.945 \). Notional \( N = \text{USD } 10,000,000 \).

Solution:
1. Identify parameters: \( F = 0.045 \), \( K = 0.04 \), \( T_1 = 1.0 \), \( \sigma = 0.20 \), \( \delta = 0.25 \).
2. Calculate \( d_1 \) and \( d_2 \):
\( d_1 = \frac{\ln(0.045/0.04) + 0.5(0.20^2)(1.0)}{0.20 \sqrt{1.0}} = \frac{0.11778 + 0.02}{0.20} = 0.6889 \)
\( d_2 = d_1 - 0.20 = 0.4889 \)
3. Standard normal CDFs:
\( N(d_1) = N(0.6889) \approx 0.7546 \)
\( N(d_2) = N(0.4889) \approx 0.6876 \)
4. Apply Black's formula:
Caplet Price = \( 10,000,000 \times 0.25 \times 0.945 \times [0.045(0.7546) - 0.04(0.6876)] \)
= \( 2,362,500 \times [0.033957 - 0.027504] = 2,362,500 \times 0.006453 \approx \text{USD } 15,245.21 \)

Example 17.2: Payer Swaption Valuation

Problem: Price a 1y into 4y (1x4) payer swaption. Option expires in 1 year. The underlying swap lasts 4 years (payments at years 2, 3, 4, 5). Annuity factor \( A(0) = 3.450 \). Forward swap rate \( S_0 = 5.2\% \). Strike \( K = 5.5\% \). Swaption vol \( \sigma = 18\% \). Notional \( N = \text{INR } 1,000,000,000 \) (1 billion INR).

Solution:
1. \( d_1 = \frac{\ln(0.052/0.055) + 0.5(0.18^2)(1)}{0.18 \sqrt{1}} = \frac{-0.05608 + 0.0162}{0.18} = -0.2215 \)
2. \( d_2 = -0.2215 - 0.18 = -0.4015 \)
3. \( N(d_1) = 0.4124 \), \( N(d_2) = 0.3440 \)
4. Price = \( 1,000,000,000 \times 3.450 \times [0.052(0.4124) - 0.055(0.3440)] \)
= \( 3,450,000,000 \times [0.021445 - 0.018920] = 3,450,000,000 \times 0.002525 \approx \text{INR } 8,711,250 \)

Example 17.3: Callable Bond Decomposition

Problem: A corporation issues a 10-year bond paying 6% annual coupon. It is callable at par (100) after 5 years. Decompose this into vanilla instruments.

Solution:
The investor receives a 10-year 6% bullet bond, but has implicitly written an option to the issuer to call the bond back at 100 at year 5. If rates drop below 6%, the bond's value exceeds 100, the issuer will call the bond, and the investor loses out on the high coupons.
Therefore: Callable Bond = 10-year Bullet Bond - Receiver Swaption (5y into 5y).
The receiver swaption gives the issuer the right to receive fixed 6% (implicitly by canceling the 6% debt) and pay floating (funding at new lower market rates). Because the investor is short this option, the callable bond trades at a higher yield (lower price) than a non-callable equivalent.

Example 17.4: WAL and Mortgage Pass-Through

Problem: An MBS pool has a constant Conditional Prepayment Rate (CPR) of 10%. Calculate the Single Monthly Mortality (SMM).

Solution:
The SMM is the percentage of the remaining principal balance that prepays in a given month, over and above the scheduled principal payment.
\( \text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR})^{1/12} \)
\( \text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - 0.10)^{1/12} = 1 - (0.90)^{0.0833} = 1 - 0.99124 = 0.00876 \) or \( 0.876\% \) per month.

Example 17.5: Zero-Coupon Bond Option (Vasicek)

Problem: Under Vasicek, an option expires at \( T=1 \), bond matures at \( S=3 \). \( P(0,1) = 0.96 \), \( P(0,3) = 0.88 \), strike \( K=0.90 \), bond volatility \( \sigma_P = 3\% \).

Solution:
1. \( h_1 = \frac{\ln(0.88 / (0.90 \times 0.96)) + 0.5(0.03^2)}{0.03} = \frac{\ln(1.0185) + 0.00045}{0.03} = 0.626 \)
2. \( h_2 = 0.626 - 0.03 = 0.596 \)
3. \( N(h_1) = 0.734 \), \( N(h_2) = 0.724 \)
4. Call Price = \( 0.88(0.734) - 0.90(0.96)(0.724) = 0.6459 - 0.6255 = 0.0204 \) (per $1 face value).

17.6 Python Code: Cap Pricer

This script prices an interest rate cap across a simple flat term structure using Black's formula.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def black_caplet(F, K, T, sigma, delta, P):
    """Prices a single caplet using Black's formula."""
    if F <= 0 or K <= 0: return 0.0  # Log-normal requires positive rates
    d1 = (np.log(F / K) + 0.5 * sigma**2 * T) / (sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    return delta * P * (F * norm.cdf(d1) - K * norm.cdf(d2))

def price_cap(notional, strike, F_list, T_list, sigma, delta):
    """
    Prices a multi-period cap.
    T_list: reset times (T_{k-1})
    Assume discount factor P roughly exp(-F * (T + delta))
    """
    cap_value = 0.0
    print(f"Pricing Cap | Notional: {notional:,.0f} | Strike: {strike:.2%}")
    print("-" * 55)
    print(f"{'Period':<10} {'Reset Time':<15} {'Fwd Rate':<10} {'Caplet Val'}")
    
    for i, (F, T) in enumerate(zip(F_list, T_list)):
        P = np.exp(-F * (T + delta)) # Approximate discount factor
        val = notional * black_caplet(F, strike, T, sigma, delta, P)
        cap_value += val
        print(f"{i+1:<10} {T:<15.2f} {F:<10.2%} ${val:,.2f}")
        
    print("-" * 55)
    print(f"Total Cap Value: ${cap_value:,.2f}")
    return cap_value

# Term structure for 3 years, quarterly resets (12 periods)
delta = 0.25
T_resets = np.arange(0, 3, delta)
# Upward sloping forward curve
F_curve = np.linspace(0.04, 0.06, len(T_resets))

price_cap(notional=10_000_000, strike=0.045, F_list=F_curve, 
          T_list=T_resets, sigma=0.20, delta=delta)

17.8 Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Warning: Pitfalls in IR Derivatives
  • Confusing Reset and Payment Dates: In a caplet, the rate is determined at time \( T_{k-1} \) but paid at \( T_k \). Discounting from \( T_{k-1} \) without accounting for this delay is a classic error.
  • Spot vs. Forward Volatility: Black's formula uses the volatility of the forward rate, not the spot rate. Forward rates have their own term structure of volatility.
  • Flat Volatility Assumption: Quoting a cap using a single "flat" volatility across all caplets vs. "spot" caplet volatilities. A cap is a portfolio; summing caplets priced at a flat vol requires careful stripping to extract true individual caplet volatilities.
  • Misunderstanding MBS Yield (OAS vs YTM): Quoting a standard Yield-to-Maturity for an MBS is misleading because cash flows change with interest rates. One must use Option-Adjusted Spread (OAS) to measure true relative value.

17.9 Industry Application Box

Industry Perspective: Bermudan Swaptions & Cancelable Swaps

At major investment banks and life insurance companies, standard European swaptions are heavily traded. However, a massive portion of the OTC market involves Bermudan Swaptions—the right to enter a swap on several specific dates (e.g., any quarterly coupon date after 2 years).

A Bermudan swaption is inherently path-dependent because exercising early kills the option for future dates. Therefore, closed-form Black's formulas cannot price them. Quants at banks use BGM (LIBOR Market Model) calibrated to the European swaption volatility surface, paired with Longstaff-Schwartz Monte Carlo simulation (Least-Squares Monte Carlo) to price the early-exercise boundary.

These are actively used to hedge callable bonds. If a bank issues a callable bond, it implicitly buys a Bermudan receiver swaption from investors. The bank will immediately delta-hedge and vega-hedge this by selling European or Bermudan swaptions in the interbank market.

17.10 Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Key Formulas
  • Caplet (Black): \( \text{Caplet}_0 = N \delta_k P(0, T_k) [ F_k(0) N(d_1) - K N(d_2) ] \)
  • Payer Swaption (Black): \( \text{Swpn}_0 = N A(0) [ S_0 N(d_1) - K N(d_2) ] \)
  • Annuity Factor: \( A(0) = \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_i P(0, T_i) \)
  • Cap-Floor Parity: \( \text{Cap}(K) - \text{Floor}(K) = \text{Payer Swap}(K) \)
  • SMM to CPR: \( \text{SMM} = 1 - (1 - \text{CPR})^{1/12} \)
  • Bachelier Swaption: \( N \cdot A(0) \sigma_N \sqrt{T_0} [ d N(d) + n(d) ] \)

Chapter 1: Algebra, Functions, Sequences and Series

Learning Objectives

  • Master the concepts of functions, operations on functions, and key families (polynomial, exponential, logarithmic).
  • Analyze arithmetic and geometric sequences and series, applying them to compound interest and annuity valuation.

Prerequisites & Roadmap

This is the foundational chapter of the textbook. It assumes a firm grasp of standard high-school mathematics, basic arithmetic operations, and elementary algebra. Mastery of this chapter is critical for Chapter 2 (Calculus) and Chapter 3 (Probability).

1. Intuition & Motivation

In quantitative finance and actuarial science, precision in modeling is everything. Before calculating the expected shortfall of a complex derivative portfolio, one must comfortably manipulate the underlying algebraic equations. Imagine you are a quantitative analyst tasked with modeling the future value of a continuous stream of cash flows. The tools you use—exponential functions, limits of geometric series, and logarithmic scaling—are not just abstract mathematical concepts; they are the gears of financial machinery.

For instance, why is the expected logarithmic return of a stock lower than its expected simple return? The answer lies in Jensen's Inequality, a purely algebraic and analytic property of convex functions, which governs the core of asset pricing. This chapter builds these concepts from the ground up, ensuring a mathematically rigorous yet deeply practical foundation.

2. Sets and Number Systems

Definition 1.1: Standard Number Sets

We define the following standard sets of numbers:

  • Natural Numbers (\(\N\)): \(\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\)
  • Integers (\(\Z\)): \(\{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}\)
  • Rational Numbers (\(\Q\)): Numbers that can be expressed as \(\frac{p}{q}\), where \(p, q \in \Z\) and \(q \neq 0\).
  • Real Numbers (\(\R\)): The set of all rational and irrational numbers, representing all points on the continuous number line.

Intervals in \(\R\) are fundamental to defining domains. A closed interval includes its endpoints, denoted \([a, b] = \{x \in \R \mid a \le x \le b\}\). An open interval excludes endpoints, \((a, b) = \{x \in \R \mid a < x < b\}\).

3. Algebra Fundamentals

Algebra provides the rules for manipulating abstract quantities. Here, we review essential laws and techniques.

3.1 Laws of Exponents

For any \(a, b \in \R\) (with \(a, b > 0\)) and \(x, y \in \R\):

  • \(a^x a^y = a^{x+y}\)
  • \(\frac{a^x}{a^y} = a^{x-y}\)
  • \((a^x)^y = a^{xy}\)
  • \((ab)^x = a^x b^x\)
  • \(a^{-x} = \frac{1}{a^x}\)
  • \(a^0 = 1\) (for \(a \neq 0\))

3.2 Completing the Square & Quadratic Formula

A quadratic equation takes the form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\). To find its roots, we can complete the square:

\[ ax^2 + bx + c = a\left(x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x\right) + c = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + c - \frac{b^2}{4a} \]

Setting this to zero yields the celebrated quadratic formula:

\[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \]

4. Functions

Definition 1.2: Function

A function \(f: A \to B\) is a rule that assigns exactly one element \(y \in B\) (the codomain) to each element \(x \in A\) (the domain). The set of all possible outputs is the range.

Key Function Families:

  • Linear: \(f(x) = mx + c\). Used in simple asset returns.
  • Quadratic: \(f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c\). Used in mean-variance optimization (variance is quadratic in weights).
  • Rational: \(f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\) where \(P, Q\) are polynomials.
  • Absolute Value: \(f(x) = |x|\). Used in L1 regularization (Lasso regression).
  • Piecewise: Functions defined by different formulas in different parts of their domains (e.g., payoff of an option).

5. Exponentials and Logarithms

The exponential function \(f(x) = a^x\) (where \(a>0, a \neq 1\)) models constant proportional growth. The most important base in finance and nature is Euler's number, \(e \approx 2.71828\).

Definition 1.3: Logarithm

The logarithm is the inverse of the exponential function. If \(y = a^x\), then \(\log_a(y) = x\). The natural logarithm, denoted \(\ln(x)\), has base \(e\).

Log Laws:

  • \(\ln(xy) = \ln(x) + \ln(y)\)
  • \(\ln(x/y) = \ln(x) - \ln(y)\)
  • \(\ln(x^y) = y \ln(x)\)
  • Change of base: \(\log_b(x) = \frac{\ln(x)}{\ln(b)}\)
Example 1.4: Using logarithms to solve doubling-time problems (Rule of 72)

Suppose an investment grows at an annual rate of \(r = 8\%\). How long does it take to double the investment under continuous compounding?

Solution:

The formula for continuous compounding is \(V(t) = V_0 e^{rt}\). We want \(V(t) = 2 V_0\):

\[ 2 V_0 = V_0 e^{0.08t} \implies e^{0.08t} = 2 \]

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides:

\[ 0.08t = \ln(2) \approx 0.693 \] \[ t = \frac{0.693}{0.08} \approx 8.66 \text{ years} \]

This is the basis of the "Rule of 72", an approximation stating that doubling time is roughly \(72 / (100r)\).

6. Sequences

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. Formally, it is a function whose domain is \(\N\).

6.1 Arithmetic Sequences

A sequence where the difference between consecutive terms is constant, \(d\). The \(n\)-th term is \(a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d\). The sum of the first \(n\) terms is \(S_n = \frac{n}{2}(a_1 + a_n)\).

6.2 Geometric Sequences

A sequence where the ratio of consecutive terms is a constant, \(r\). The \(n\)-th term is \(a_n = a_1 r^{n-1}\).

Theorem 1.1: Sum of a Geometric Series

The sum of the first \(n\) terms of a geometric sequence is:

\[ S_n = a_1 \frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}, \quad r \neq 1 \]

If \(|r| < 1\), the infinite geometric series converges to:

\[ S_\infty = \frac{a_1}{1 - r} \]

Example 1.1: Compound interest calculation using geometric sequences (INR)

You invest INR 10,000 at an annual interest rate of 6%, compounded annually. What is the value after 5 years?

Solution:

This forms a geometric sequence where \(a_1 = 10000(1.06)\) and \(r = 1.06\). The value at year \(n\) is \(V_n = V_0 r^n\).

\[ V_5 = 10000 \times (1.06)^5 = 10000 \times 1.338225 = \text{INR } 13,382.25 \]

Example 1.2: Sum of a geometric series applied to annuity valuation (USD)

An investor receives USD 1,000 at the end of each year for 10 years. If the discount rate is 5%, what is the present value of this annuity?

Solution:

The present value of the cash flow at year \(k\) is \(1000 \times (1.05)^{-k}\). The total present value (PV) is the sum of a geometric series:

\[ PV = \sum_{k=1}^{10} 1000 (1.05)^{-k} \]

Here, the first term \(a_1 = 1000/1.05\) and the common ratio \(r = 1/1.05\).

\[ PV = \frac{1000}{1.05} \times \frac{1 - (1/1.05)^{10}}{1 - 1/1.05} = 1000 \times \frac{1 - 1.05^{-10}}{0.05} \approx USD 7,721.73 \]

7. Series and Summation Notation

The Greek letter Sigma (\(\Sigma\)) is used to denote sums concisely.

\[ \sum_{i=1}^n x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n \]

Properties of sums include linearity: \(\sum (a x_i + b y_i) = a \sum x_i + b \sum y_i\).

Product notation uses Pi (\(\Pi\)):

\[ \prod_{i=1}^n x_i = x_1 \times x_2 \times \dots \times x_n \]

8. Limits of Sequences

Definition 1.4: Formal Limit of a Sequence

A sequence \(\{a_n\}\) converges to a limit \(L\), denoted \(\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n = L\), if for every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists an integer \(N\) such that for all \(n > N\), we have \(|a_n - L| < \epsilon\).

One of the most important limits in finance defines continuous compounding:

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)^n = e \]

9. Inequalities

Inequalities allow us to establish bounds when exact calculations are impossible. They are heavily utilized in risk management and portfolio optimization.

9.1 Arithmetic Mean - Geometric Mean (AM-GM) Inequality

Theorem 1.2: AM-GM Inequality

For any set of non-negative real numbers \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\), the arithmetic mean is greater than or equal to the geometric mean:

\[ \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} \ge \sqrt[n]{x_1 x_2 \dots x_n} \]

Equality holds if and only if \(x_1 = x_2 = \dots = x_n\).

Example 1.3: Proving AM-GM for n=2 and Portfolio Bounds

Proof for n=2:

We want to show that for \(a, b \ge 0\), \(\frac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}\).

Consider the square of a real number, which must be non-negative: \((\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b})^2 \ge 0\).

Expanding the square: \(a - 2\sqrt{ab} + b \ge 0\).

Rearranging terms yields: \(a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab} \implies \frac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}\). \(\blacksquare\)

9.2 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

For any real sequences \(a_1, \dots, a_n\) and \(b_1, \dots, b_n\):

\[ \left( \sum_{i=1}^n a_i b_i \right)^2 \le \left( \sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2 \right) \left( \sum_{i=1}^n b_i^2 \right) \]

9.3 Jensen's Inequality

Theorem 1.3: Jensen's Inequality

If \(f(x)\) is a convex function on an interval, and \(x_1, \dots, x_n\) are in its domain, and \(w_1, \dots, w_n\) are non-negative weights summing to 1, then:

\[ f\left( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i \right) \le \sum_{i=1}^n w_i f(x_i) \]

For concave functions, the inequality is reversed.

Industry Application Box: Jensen's Inequality in Finance

In quantitative finance, the logarithmic function \(f(x) = \ln(x)\) is strictly concave. Therefore, by Jensen's inequality (reversed):

\[ \ln(\E[1+R]) \ge \E[\ln(1+R)] \]

This explains why the expected log return (geometric average return) of a volatile asset is always strictly less than its expected simple return (arithmetic average return). A failure to understand this distinction led to widespread mispricing of long-term options in the early days of derivatives trading.

10. Binomial Theorem

Theorem 1.4: Binomial Theorem

For any integer \(n \ge 0\):

\[ (x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^k \]

where \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\) are the binomial coefficients.

Example 1.5: Binomial expansion applied to approximate calculations

Approximate \((1.01)^5\) using the Binomial Theorem.

Solution:

Let \(x = 1\) and \(y = 0.01\).

\[ (1 + 0.01)^5 = \binom{5}{0}1^5(0.01)^0 + \binom{5}{1}1^4(0.01)^1 + \binom{5}{2}1^3(0.01)^2 + \dots \]

Calculating the first three terms:

\[ 1 + 5(0.01) + 10(0.0001) = 1 + 0.05 + 0.001 = 1.051 \]

The exact value is 1.0510100501, showing our approximation is highly accurate and computationally cheap.

12. Python Code Implementation

The following script calculates the compound interest and reproduces the geometric series annuity valuation from the examples above.


# Example 1.1: Compound Interest
initial_investment = 10000
rate = 0.06
years = 5
future_value = initial_investment * (1 + rate)**years
print(f"Future Value of Investment: INR {future_value:.2f}")

# Example 1.2: Present Value of Annuity (Geometric Series)
cash_flow = 1000
discount_rate = 0.05
periods = 10

# Using sum of geometric series formula
r = 1 / (1 + discount_rate)
a1 = cash_flow * r
pv = a1 * (1 - r**periods) / (1 - r)
print(f"Present Value of Annuity: USD {pv:.2f}")

13. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Watch Out!
  • Confusing Arithmetic and Geometric Series: Applying the geometric sum formula to an arithmetic sequence, or vice-versa. Always check if the ratio or difference is constant.
  • Logarithm Laws: A frequent error is assuming \(\ln(x+y) = \ln(x) + \ln(y)\). This is false! Logarithms turn products into sums, not sums into sums.
  • Jensen's Inequality Direction: Getting the inequality sign wrong. Remember: for convex functions (like \(x^2\)), the average of the function is greater than the function of the average. For concave (like \(\ln(x)\)), it is reversed.

Formula Cheat-Sheet

2. Evaluate \(\ln(e^5)\) and \(e^{\ln(10)}\).

8. Calculate the partial sum \(\sum_{k=1}^n \left( \frac{1}{k} - \frac{1}{k+1} \right)\) using the telescoping property.

Advanced

9. Use Jensen's inequality to prove that for \(x, y, z > 0\), \(\left( \frac{x+y+z}{3} \right)^2 \le \frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{3}\).
10. Prove the Binomial Theorem by mathematical induction.
11. A perpetuity pays USD 100 at the end of year 1, and the payments grow at 2% per year indefinitely. If the discount rate is 6%, find the present value.
12. Evaluate \(\lim_{n \to \infty} n \ln(1 + \frac{x}{n})\) and interpret its meaning in finance.
Solutions to Selected Exercises
1. Solution: Using the quadratic formula with \(a=2, b=-5, c=3\), we get \(x = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 24}}{4} = \frac{5 \pm 1}{4}\). Roots are \(x=1.5\) and \(x=1\).

6. Solution: Here \(a=5\) and \(r = -1/2\). Sum \(S = \frac{5}{1 - (-1/2)} = \frac{5}{3/2} = \frac{10}{3}\).
7. Solution: By AM-GM on \(x\) and \(1/x\), \(\frac{x + 1/x}{2} \ge \sqrt{x \cdot (1/x)} = \sqrt{1} = 1\). Multiplying by 2 yields \(x + 1/x \ge 2\).
8. Solution: \(\sum_{k=1}^n (1/k - 1/(k+1)) = (1 - 1/2) + (1/2 - 1/3) + \dots + (1/n - 1/(n+1)) = 1 - \frac{1}{n+1}\).
9. Solution: The function \(f(t) = t^2\) is strictly convex because \(f''(t) = 2 > 0\). Applying Jensen's Inequality with equal weights \(w_i = 1/3\), \(f(\frac{x+y+z}{3}) \le \frac{f(x)+f(y)+f(z)}{3}\).
12. Solution: We can rewrite as \(\ln( (1 + \frac{x}{n})^n )\). As \(n \to \infty\), \((1+x/n)^n \to e^x\). So the limit is \(\ln(e^x) = x\). In finance, this proves that continuous compounding is the limit of discrete compounding.

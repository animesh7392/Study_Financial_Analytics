Chapter 33: Ratemaking

Learning Objectives
  • Understand the fundamental principles of ratemaking: rate adequacy, equity, and regulatory compliance.
  • Apply the Pure Premium and Loss Ratio methods to determine indicated rate changes.
  • Design Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) for claim frequency and severity to establish multiplicative tariff structures.
  • Incorporate trend factors and loss development factors to adjust historical data for future rating periods.
  • Implement experience rating and schedule rating to modify manual rates for individual risks.
Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter relies heavily on the credibility concepts from Chapter 32: Credibility Theory, as well as the fundamentals of generalized linear models introduced in Chapter 14. We will also preview loss development factors (LDFs), which will be extensively covered in Chapter 34: Loss Reserving.

1. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you are the Chief Actuary for a newly established auto insurance company. Your first task is to price the policies. If you charge too much, healthy drivers will flock to competitors, leaving you with only the riskiest drivers (adverse selection). If you charge too little, you will sell many policies but eventually go bankrupt as claims exceed premium revenue. This balancing act is the essence of ratemaking (or pricing).

Ratemaking is not just about finding an average cost; it is about segmenting the risk. A 20-year-old driving a sports car in a congested city has a fundamentally different risk profile than a 50-year-old driving a sedan in a rural area. How do we quantify these differences? How do we adjust for inflation and the fact that claims take time to settle? This chapter provides the quantitative tools to answer these questions.

2. Formal Theory: Ratemaking Principles

The ultimate goal of ratemaking is to determine a premium that covers expected losses, expenses, and provides a reasonable profit margin, while adhering to regulatory and actuarial principles.

Definition 33.1: Fundamental Ratemaking Principles

Rates must be:

  • Adequate: Rates should generate enough revenue to pay all claims and expenses.
  • Not Excessive: Rates should not generate unreasonable profits.
  • Equitable (Not Unfairly Discriminatory): Risks with similar expected costs should be charged similar rates. Differences in rates must reflect demonstrable differences in expected costs.

The gross premium (or rate) consists of three components:

\[ \text{Rate} = \text{Pure Premium} + \text{Expenses} + \text{Profit Margin} \]

Or, often formulated as:

\[ \text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Pure Premium} + \text{Fixed Expenses}}{1 - \text{Variable Expense Ratio} - \text{Target Profit Ratio}} \]

3. Fundamental Methods

3.1 The Pure Premium Method

The pure premium (PP) is the expected loss per unit of exposure. It is the product of expected frequency and expected severity.

Definition 33.2: Pure Premium
\[ \text{Pure Premium} = \E[\text{Frequency}] \times \E[\text{Severity}] = \frac{\text{Total Expected Losses}}{\text{Total Exposures}} \]

Where Frequency = Claim Count / Exposure, and Severity = Total Losses / Claim Count.

Example 33.1: Pure Premium Calculation for Motor Insurance

An insurer has 50,000 car-years of exposure. Over the experience period, there were 4,000 claims totaling $12,000,000 in losses.

Step 1: Frequency
Frequency = 4,000 / 50,000 = 0.08 (or 8% per car-year).

Step 2: Severity
Severity = $12,000,000 / 4,000 = $3,000 per claim.

Step 3: Pure Premium
Pure Premium = Frequency \times Severity = 0.08 \times \$3,000 = \$240 \text{ per car-year}.

If fixed expenses are $30 per exposure, variable expenses are 20%, and the profit target is 5%, the gross rate is:
\[ \text{Rate} = \frac{240 + 30}{1 - 0.20 - 0.05} = \frac{270}{0.75} = \$360 \]

3.2 The Loss Ratio Method

The loss ratio method calculates the necessary rate change relative to the current rate level, rather than computing a rate from scratch.

Definition 33.3: Indicated Rate Change
\[ \text{Indicated Rate Change} = \frac{\text{Experienced Loss Ratio} + \text{Fixed Expense Ratio}}{\text{Target Loss Ratio}} - 1 \]

Alternatively, neglecting fixed expenses as a separate component:
\[ \text{Indicated Rate Change} = \frac{\text{Experienced Loss Ratio}}{\text{Target Loss Ratio}} - 1 \]

Where the Target Loss Ratio = 1 - Variable Expense Ratio - Target Profit Ratio.

Example 33.2: Loss Ratio Method

Current premium volume is $5,000,000. Projected ultimate losses are $3,800,000. The experienced loss ratio is:
\[ \text{ELR} = \frac{3,800,000}{5,000,000} = 0.76 \]

The insurer targets a variable expense ratio of 25% and a profit margin of 5%, giving a Target Loss Ratio of:
\[ \text{TLR} = 1 - 0.25 - 0.05 = 0.70 \]

Indicated Rate Change:
\[ \text{Change} = \frac{0.76}{0.70} - 1 = 1.0857 - 1 = +8.57\% \]

The insurer needs to increase rates by 8.57% to achieve the target profitability.

4. GLMs for Ratemaking

To ensure rate equity, insurers segment risks using rating factors (e.g., age, vehicle type). Modern ratemaking uses Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) to create a multiplicative tariff structure. The rate for an individual is a base rate multiplied by a set of relativities.

4.1 Multiplicative Tariff Structure

Theorem 33.1: GLM Multiplicative Tariff

Under a Poisson GLM with log link, the predicted claim frequency for a risk with factors \(x_1, \dots, x_k\) is multiplicative:

\[ \hat{\lambda} = e^{\beta_0} \cdot e^{\beta_1 x_1} \cdots e^{\beta_k x_k} \]

Each \(e^{\beta_j}\) is the relativity for factor \(j\). This result follows from the exponential inverse-link function of the Poisson GLM.

A multiplicative model assumes the expected pure premium for a risk with characteristics \( x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k \) is:

\[ \E[Y] = \text{Base} \times f_1(x_1) \times f_2(x_2) \times \dots \times f_k(x_k) \]

To fit this with a GLM, we use a logarithmic link function, turning the multiplicative relationship into an additive one in the linear predictor:

\[ \ln(\E[Y]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots + \beta_k x_k \]

Here, \( \exp(\beta_0) \) represents the base rate, and \( \exp(\beta_j) \) represents the relativity for factor \( j \).

4.2 Frequency and Severity GLMs

  • Frequency: Modeled using a Poisson or Negative Binomial distribution with a log link. Exposure is included as an offset term: \( \ln(\text{Exposure}) \).
  • Severity: Modeled using a Gamma distribution with a log link.
  • Pure Premium (Tweedie): Modeled directly using a Tweedie distribution (variance proportional to \( \mu^p \), where \( 1 < p < 2 \)), which handles the point mass at zero (no claims) and the continuous positive tail (claim sizes).
Example 33.3: GLM Poisson Frequency Model

An actuary runs a Poisson GLM for claim frequency with two categorical variables: Area (Urban/Rural) and Driver Age (Young/Adult). The reference levels are Rural and Adult. The estimated coefficients are:

  • Intercept (\( \beta_0 \)): -2.30
  • Area: Urban (\( \beta_1 \)): 0.40
  • Age: Young (\( \beta_2 \)): 0.65

Calculations:

Base Frequency (Rural, Adult) = \( \exp(-2.30) = 0.100 \)

Relativity for Urban = \( \exp(0.40) = 1.492 \)
Relativity for Young = \( \exp(0.65) = 1.915 \)

Expected frequency for a Young driver in an Urban area:
\( \E[F] = 0.100 \times 1.492 \times 1.915 = 0.286 \) (or 28.6%).

5. Trend and Development

Historical loss data must be adjusted to the future period when the rates will be in effect.

5.1 Loss Development

Claims take time to be reported and settled. Historical losses must be multiplied by Loss Development Factors (LDFs) to estimate their ultimate value. We compute LDFs using run-off triangles (detailed in Chapter 34). For ratemaking, we take reported losses and multiply by the appropriate Cumulative LDF (CLDF) to reach ultimate losses.

5.2 Trending

Losses also suffer from inflation (severity trend) and changes in accident rates (frequency trend). We project these from the average date of the historical experience period to the average date of the future policy period.

Example 33.5: Trending and Developing Loss Data

Experience period: Accident Year 2023. We are setting rates for policies written in 2025.

  • Reported losses for AY 2023: $5,000,000
  • CLDF to ultimate: 1.15
  • Annual severity trend: 4%
  • Annual frequency trend: 0%

Development:
Ultimate Losses = $5,000,000 \times 1.15 = \$5,750,000.

Trending:
Average date of AY 2023 is July 1, 2023. Average date of accident for policies written in 2025 is July 1, 2026 (assuming 1-year policies). The trend period is 3 years.
Trend Factor = \( (1.04)^3 = 1.12486 \).

Trended Ultimate Losses:
$5,750,000 \times 1.12486 = \$6,467,945.

6. Experience, Schedule, and Judgment Rating

6.1 Experience Rating

For larger commercial risks, the manual rate (class rate) is modified based on the insured's own historical loss experience. This uses an Experience Modification Factor (Mod), driven by credibility theory (Chapter 32).

\[ \text{Mod} = \frac{Z \times \text{Actual Losses} + (1-Z) \times \text{Expected Losses}}{\text{Expected Losses}} \]

Where \( Z \) is the credibility factor. The final rate is the manual rate multiplied by the Mod.

Example 33.4: Experience Modification Factor

A commercial fleet's manual premium is $200,000, with expected losses of $130,000. Over the past 3 years, their actual losses were $180,000. Actuarial tables assign a credibility factor of \( Z = 0.60 \).

Mod = \( \frac{0.60(180,000) + 0.40(130,000)}{130,000} = \frac{108,000 + 52,000}{130,000} = \frac{160,000}{130,000} = 1.23 \)

The modified premium is $200,000 \times 1.23 = \$246,000.

6.2 Schedule and Judgment Rating

Schedule rating allows underwriters to apply subjective percentage credits or debits based on risk characteristics not captured in the manual rate (e.g., condition of premises, safety training programs). Judgment rating is used when data is too sparse for statistical analysis (e.g., unique marine or aviation risks), relying entirely on the underwriter's expertise.

7. Regulatory Considerations

Insurance is heavily regulated to protect consumers.

  • Prior Approval: Insurers must file rates with regulators and wait for approval before using them.
  • File-and-Use: Insurers file rates and can use them immediately, subject to later regulatory review.
  • Use-and-File: Insurers can change rates immediately and file the justification within a specified window.

Regulators also enforce anti-discrimination laws. For instance, the EU Gender Directive (2012) prohibits European insurers from using gender as a rating factor for pricing, forcing actuaries to find proxy variables (like car weight or occupation) to maintain risk segmentation.

8. Diagrams Description

While we omit plotted images here, visualizing GLM output is critical. A standard plot in ratemaking is the One-Way Lift Chart. The data is sorted into deciles by predicted pure premium. For each decile, the chart plots the actual pure premium vs. the predicted pure premium. A well-fitted model will show the predicted lines tracking the actual lines closely, with a steep slope from the 1st to the 10th decile, indicating strong segmentation power.

9. Python Code: Poisson GLM

We use statsmodels to fit a Poisson frequency model, illustrating the use of an offset for exposure.


import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# Simulated dataset
np.random.seed(42)
data = pd.DataFrame({
    'exposure': np.random.uniform(0.1, 1.0, 1000),
    'driver_age': np.random.choice(['Young', 'Adult', 'Senior'], 1000),
    'vehicle_type': np.random.choice(['Sedan', 'SUV', 'Sports'], 1000)
})

# True coefficients
age_eff = {'Young': 1.5, 'Adult': 1.0, 'Senior': 1.2}
veh_eff = {'Sedan': 1.0, 'SUV': 0.9, 'Sports': 1.8}

# Simulate claims
lambda_true = 0.1 * data['exposure'] * data['driver_age'].map(age_eff) * data['vehicle_type'].map(veh_eff)
data['claim_count'] = np.random.poisson(lambda_true)

# Fit Poisson GLM using statsmodels
# We use log(exposure) as the offset. 
# C() treats variables as categorical.
formula = "claim_count ~ C(driver_age, Treatment('Adult')) + C(vehicle_type, Treatment('Sedan'))"

model = smf.glm(formula=formula, data=data, 
                family=sm.families.Poisson(), 
                offset=np.log(data['exposure']))
results = model.fit()

print(results.summary())

# Extracting relativities (exp(coefficients))
relativities = np.exp(results.params)
print("\nMultiplicative Relativities:")
print(relativities)

Output note: The relativities for Adult and Sedan (the reference classes) are inherently 1.0. The exponentiated coefficients will closely approximate the true effects defined in the simulation code.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Forgetting the Offset: When modeling frequency with a Poisson GLM, failing to include log(exposure) as an offset will cause the model to confuse exposure duration with underlying risk.
  • Double Counting Trend: If loss development factors (LDFs) already implicitly contain some inflation, applying a full economic trend factor on top may overstate future losses.
  • Confusing Rate Adequacy with Profitability: Adequate rates mean the ex-ante pricing is mathematically sound. Ex-post, natural disasters or sudden inflation can still cause massive underwriting losses.
Industry Application: Telematics and Usage-Based Insurance (UBI)

Modern auto insurers use IoT devices or smartphone apps to track driving behavior (harsh braking, cornering, nighttime driving). This high-frequency data is fed into Machine Learning models (like Gradient Boosting Machines) rather than traditional GLMs. However, regulatory constraints often require actuaries to translate these ML outputs back into transparent, tabular rating structures resembling GLM outputs so they can be filed and approved by state regulators.

Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet
  • Pure Premium (PP): \( PP = \E[\text{Freq}] \times \E[\text{Sev}] \)
  • Indicated Rate (PP Method): \( \text{Rate} = \frac{PP + \text{Fixed Exp}}{1 - \text{Var Exp} - \text{Profit}} \)
  • Indicated Change (Loss Ratio Method): \( \text{Change} = \frac{\text{ELR}}{\text{TLR}} - 1 \)
  • Experience Mod: \( \text{Mod} = \frac{Z \cdot A + (1-Z) \cdot E}{E} \)
  • Poisson GLM Linear Predictor: \( \ln(\mu) = \text{offset}(\ln(\text{Exposure})) + \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots \)

11. Solutions to Selected Exercises

Solution to 33.1: Rate = (Pure Premium + Fixed Exp) / (1 - Var Exp - Profit)
Rate = (400 + 50) / (1 - 0.15 - 0.05) = 450 / 0.80 = $562.50.
Solution to 33.2: Target Loss Ratio (TLR) = 1 - Var Exp - Fixed Exp - Profit = 1 - 0.20 - 0.05 - 0.03 = 0.72.
Indicated Change = (ELR / TLR) - 1 = (0.82 / 0.72) - 1 = 1.1389 - 1 = +13.89%.
Solution to 33.5: Linear predictor = 7.5 + 0.2 + 0.5 = 8.2.
Expected Severity = \(\exp(8.2) = \$3,640.95\).
Solution to 33.6: Mod = (Z * A + (1-Z) * E) / E = (0.45 * 800,000 + 0.55 * 500,000) / 500,000 = (360,000 + 275,000) / 500,000 = 635,000 / 500,000 = 1.27.
Modified Premium = Base Premium * Mod = $500,000 * 1.27 = $635,000.
Solution to 33.7: Ultimate Losses = Reported * LDF = 2,000,000 * 1.30 = 2,600,000.
Trend Factor = \((1.05)^{2.5} = 1.1297\).
Trended Ultimate = 2,600,000 * 1.1297 = $2,937,220.
Solution to 33.9: Let \(E\) be expected losses and \(A\) actual losses.
Credibility Estimate = \(Z \cdot A + (1-Z) \cdot E\).
By definition, Mod = \((Z \cdot A + (1-Z) \cdot E) / E\).
Applying Mod to Expected Losses: \(E \cdot \text{Mod} = E \cdot \frac{Z \cdot A + (1-Z) \cdot E}{E} = Z \cdot A + (1-Z) \cdot E\), which is exactly the credibility estimate.
Solution to 33.11: AIC for Model A = \(1200 + 2(10) = 1220\).
AIC for Model B = \(1180 + 2(15) = 1210\).
We prefer Model B because it has a lower AIC, indicating that the reduction in deviance (better fit) outweighs the penalty for adding 5 more parameters.

3. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you are the Chief Risk Officer of a large investment bank in Mumbai. Your trading desks hold billions of INR in equities, government bonds, FX derivatives, and exotic options. The CEO asks you a deceptively simple question at 5:00 PM: "How much money could we lose tomorrow?"

If you say "We could lose everything," it's factually true but practically useless. The business needs a statistical estimate to allocate capital efficiently. You need to provide a statement like: "We are 99% confident that our losses tomorrow will not exceed INR 250 Crores." This threshold is the Value at Risk (VaR). But what if that 1% disaster happens? If we breach the VaR, how bad could it be? The average loss in that tail scenario is the Expected Shortfall (ES).

Historically, during events like the 2007-2008 Global Financial Crisis or the March 2020 COVID-19 crash, models based on normal distributions severely underestimated tail risks. Furthermore, traders often found that when they rushed to sell assets to cut losses, the market vanished—they faced massive bid-ask spreads and price impacts. This introduces Liquidity Risk. This chapter formalizes these concepts, transitioning from single-asset modeling to enterprise-wide quantitative risk management.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Value at Risk (VaR)

Definition 19.1: Value at Risk (VaR)
Let \( L \) be the loss of a portfolio over a given holding period \( \Delta t \). The Value at Risk at a confidence level \( \alpha \) (where \( \alpha \in (0, 1) \), typically 0.95 or 0.99) is defined as the smallest value \( v \) such that the probability of the loss \( L \) exceeding \( v \) is at most \( 1 - \alpha \): \[ \text{VaR}_\alpha = \inf \{ v \in \mathbb{R} : \mathbb{P}(L > v) \le 1 - \alpha \} \] Equivalently, it is the \( \alpha \)-quantile of the loss distribution.

There are three primary methodologies to compute VaR:

4.1.1 Parametric (Variance-Covariance) VaR

If we assume that the portfolio returns are normally distributed with mean \( \mu_p \) and standard deviation \( \sigma_p \), the VaR can be derived analytically. For a holding period \( \Delta t \) and confidence level \( \alpha \):

\[ \text{VaR}_\alpha = V_0 \left( z_\alpha \sigma_p - \mu_p \right) \]

where \( V_0 \) is the initial portfolio value, and \( z_\alpha \) is the standard normal deviate corresponding to the \( \alpha \) probability (e.g., \( z_{0.99} \approx 2.326 \)). In short horizons (e.g., daily), \( \mu_p \) is often assumed to be zero. For a portfolio of \( n \) assets with weights \( \mathbf{w} \) and covariance matrix \( \Sigma \):

\[ \sigma_p^2 = \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma \mathbf{w} \]

4.1.2 Historical Simulation VaR

Instead of assuming a normal distribution, Historical Simulation (HS) uses actual historical returns. We apply past \( N \) daily returns of the risk factors to the current portfolio weights to simulate \( N \) hypothetical daily P&Ls. The simulated P&Ls are sorted from worst loss to largest gain. The \( (1-\alpha) \)-percentile of this empirical distribution is the HS VaR.

4.1.3 Monte Carlo VaR

Monte Carlo simulation involves specifying a stochastic process (e.g., Geometric Brownian Motion) for the underlying risk factors. We simulate tens of thousands of future scenarios, revalue the portfolio in each scenario (using full repricing, which is crucial for non-linear instruments like options), and find the \( \alpha \)-quantile of the resulting loss distribution.

4.2 Expected Shortfall (CVaR)

Definition 19.2: Expected Shortfall (ES)
Expected Shortfall (also known as Conditional VaR or CVaR) at confidence level \( \alpha \) is the expected loss given that the loss exceeds the VaR threshold. For continuous distributions: \[ \text{ES}_\alpha = \mathbb{E}[L \mid L > \text{VaR}_\alpha] = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \int_{\alpha}^{1} \text{VaR}_u \dd u \]

For a normal distribution with zero mean, the ES has a closed-form solution: \( \text{ES}_\alpha = \sigma_p \frac{\phi(z_\alpha)}{1-\alpha} \), where \( \phi \) is the standard normal PDF.

4.3 Coherent Risk Measures

A fundamental critique of VaR is that it can penalize diversification. Artzner et al. (1999) proposed axioms for a coherent risk measure \( \rho \):

  • Monotonicity: If portfolio \( X \) always yields worse returns than \( Y \), then \( \rho(X) \ge \rho(Y) \).
  • Subadditivity: \( \rho(X + Y) \le \rho(X) + \rho(Y) \). (Diversification reduces risk)
  • Positive Homogeneity: For \( \lambda > 0 \), \( \rho(\lambda X) = \lambda \rho(X) \).
  • Translation Invariance: For a risk-free cash amount \( c \), \( \rho(X + c) = \rho(X) - c \).
Theorem 19.1: Coherence of ES and Non-Coherence of VaR
Expected Shortfall (ES) satisfies all four Artzner axioms and is a coherent risk measure. Value at Risk (VaR) fails the subadditivity axiom in general (especially for highly skewed or heavy-tailed distributions), meaning the VaR of a combined portfolio can be higher than the sum of the individual VaRs.

4.4 Backtesting

Backtesting compares the daily VaR forecasts against the actual realized daily P&L. An exception or exceedance occurs when the actual loss is greater than the VaR.

Kupiec POF (Proportion of Failures) Test: Let \( N \) be the number of days, \( x \) be the number of exceptions, and \( p = 1-\alpha \) be the target failure rate. Under the null hypothesis that the model is correct, \( x \sim \text{Binomial}(N, p) \). The likelihood ratio test statistic is:

\[ LR_{\text{UC}} = -2 \ln\left( \frac{p^x (1-p)^{N-x}}{(x/N)^x (1 - x/N)^{N-x}} \right) \]

which follows a \( \chi^2_1 \) distribution.

Christoffersen Conditional Coverage Test: It extends the Kupiec test by testing for independence of exceptions (i.e., exceedances should not cluster together in time).

Traffic Light Approach (Basel): Banks are assigned zones based on the number of exceedances over 250 days at 99% VaR. Green (0-4), Yellow (5-9, leading to higher capital multipliers), and Red (10+, model rejected).

4.5 Stress Testing and Scenario Analysis

Since VaR/ES only cover "normal" market conditions up to the \( \alpha \)-level, stress testing evaluates extreme, implausible but possible events.

  • Historical Scenarios: Revaluing the portfolio using market shocks from Black Monday (1987), Asian Financial Crisis (1997), Global Financial Crisis (2008), or COVID-19 (2020).
  • Hypothetical Scenarios: E.g., a simultaneous 200 bps hike in yield curves and a 30% drop in equities.
  • Reverse Stress Testing: Identifying which specific scenarios would cause the bank to fail, and assessing their plausibility.

4.6 FRTB Overview (Fundamental Review of the Trading Book)

Basel IV introduced FRTB to overhaul market risk capital requirements:

  • ES Replaces VaR: Internal models must use 97.5% ES instead of 99% VaR to better capture tail risk.
  • Liquidity Horizons: Different risk factors have different liquidity horizons (e.g., 10 days for large cap equities, 120 days for complex credit products) reflecting how long it takes to exit a position.
  • Standardized Approach (SbM): A highly sensitive fallback model calculating capital based on sensitivities (Delta, Vega, Curvature) to prescribed risk factors.
  • P&L Attribution (PLA) Test: Strict backtesting requiring alignment between the risk management model's theoretical P&L and the front-office actual P&L.

4.7 Liquidity Risk

Liquidity risk has two distinct branches:

  1. Funding Liquidity Risk: Inability to meet cash flow obligations (e.g., margin calls, deposit withdrawals). Managed via Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR) and Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR).
  2. Market Liquidity Risk: Inability to sell assets quickly without causing a significant price drop. Measured via bid-ask spreads and market depth.

Liquidity-Adjusted VaR (LVaR): Adjusts standard VaR by adding the cost of unwinding the position. A simple approach adds half the bid-ask spread (\( s \)) times the position size: \[ \text{LVaR} = \text{VaR} + \frac{1}{2} s \cdot V_0 \] More advanced models incorporate price impact functions where the spread widens as the unwound volume increases.

5. Worked Examples

Example 19.1: Parametric VaR for a 2-Asset Portfolio
Problem: A portfolio consists of $10M in Asset A and $5M in Asset B. Daily volatilities are \( \sigma_A = 1.5\% \) and \( \sigma_B = 2.0\% \). The correlation is \( \rho = 0.4 \). Assume zero mean return. Calculate the 1-day 99% VaR.

Solution: 1. Define weights/exposures: \( w_A V_0 = 10,000,000 \), \( w_B V_0 = 5,000,000 \).
2. Calculate variance of portfolio value: \[ \sigma_P^2 = (w_A V_0 \sigma_A)^2 + (w_B V_0 \sigma_B)^2 + 2(w_A V_0 \sigma_A)(w_B V_0 \sigma_B)\rho \] \[ \sigma_P^2 = (150,000)^2 + (100,000)^2 + 2(150,000)(100,000)(0.4) \] \[ \sigma_P^2 = 22,500,000,000 + 10,000,000,000 + 12,000,000,000 = 44,500,000,000 \] 3. Portfolio standard deviation: \( \sigma_P = \sqrt{44,500,000,000} \approx \$210,950 \).
4. Calculate 99% VaR (using \( z_{0.99} = 2.326 \)): \[ \text{VaR}_{0.99} = 2.326 \times 210,950 \approx \$490,670 \] The 1-day 99% VaR is approximately $490,670.
Example 19.2: Historical Simulation VaR and ES
Problem: You have 250 daily simulated P&Ls for a portfolio. Sorting them from worst loss to highest gain, the 10 worst daily returns are (in INR Crores): -50, -42, -38, -35, -31, -28, -25, -20, -18, -15. Calculate the 97.5% VaR and 97.5% ES.

Solution: 1. Confidence level \( \alpha = 0.97.5 \). The tail probability is \( 1 - 0.975 = 0.025 \).
2. The number of observations in the tail is \( 250 \times 0.025 = 6.25 \). We interpolate between the 6th and 7th worst losses, or simply take the 6th worst loss depending on convention. Using the 6th worst loss: index 6 is -28 Crores.
\[ \text{VaR}_{97.5\%} = \text{INR 28 Crores} \text{ (quoted as a positive loss number)} \]
3. The ES is the average of the losses exceeding the VaR (the worst 6 losses, though strictly we should average the tail distribution). \[ \text{ES}_{97.5\%} = \frac{50 + 42 + 38 + 35 + 31 + 28}{6} = \frac{224}{6} \approx \text{INR 37.33 Crores} \]
Example 19.3: Monte Carlo VaR for Non-Linear Portfolio
Problem: A portfolio holds a short position in an ATM call option on a stock (S=$100). If the stock jumps significantly, losses increase non-linearly. We simulate 10,000 paths for S tomorrow. After repricing the option using Black-Scholes for all 10,000 paths, we sort the P&Ls. The 100th worst P&L (1% tail) is a loss of $14,500. Calculate 99% VaR.

Solution: Since we simulated 10,000 paths, the 99% confidence level corresponds to the \( 10,000 \times (1 - 0.99) = 100 \)-th worst outcome.
Therefore, the Monte Carlo \( \text{VaR}_{99\%} = \$14,500 \).
Note: Unlike Parametric VaR, this method perfectly captures the convexity (gamma) of the option.
Example 19.4: Kupiec Backtest Traffic Light
Problem: Over the last 250 trading days, a bank's 99% VaR model recorded 8 exceedances. Under Basel's traffic light approach, what zone is the bank in? Calculate the Kupiec test statistic.

Solution: 1. Traffic Light: 8 exceedances over 250 days falls in the Yellow Zone (5-9 exceedances). The bank will face a capital surcharge.
2. Kupiec Test: \( N = 250, x = 8, p = 0.01 \). \[ LR = -2 \ln\left( \frac{0.01^8 (0.99)^{242}}{(8/250)^8 (1 - 8/250)^{242}} \right) \] Numerator: \( 10^{-16} \times 0.0877 \approx 8.77 \times 10^{-18} \)
Denominator: \( (0.032)^8 \times (0.968)^{242} \approx 1.09 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.000378 \approx 4.14 \times 10^{-16} \)
Ratio = \( \frac{8.77 \times 10^{-18}}{4.14 \times 10^{-16}} \approx 0.0211 \)
\( LR = -2 \ln(0.0211) \approx 7.72 \)
The critical value for \( \chi^2_1 \) at 95% confidence is 3.84. Since 7.72 > 3.84, we reject the null hypothesis. The VaR model is statistically inaccurate (underestimating risk).
Example 19.5: Liquidity-Adjusted VaR
Problem: A trader holds 100,000 shares of an illiquid stock. Current price is INR 500. The daily volatility is 2%. The bid-ask spread is typically 3% of the price. Calculate the 99% standard VaR and the LVaR.

Solution: 1. Position Value \( V_0 = 100,000 \times 500 = \text{INR 50,000,000} \).
2. Standard 99% VaR (assuming normal, \(\mu=0\)): \[ \text{VaR} = 2.326 \times 0.02 \times 50,000,000 = \text{INR 2,326,000} \]
3. Liquidity cost: Half the spread applied to the position size. Spread \( s = 0.03 \). \[ \text{Cost} = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.03 \times 50,000,000 = \text{INR 750,000} \]
4. LVaR: \[ \text{LVaR} = 2,326,000 + 750,000 = \text{INR 3,076,000} \] Ignoring liquidity risk would underestimate the tail risk upon unwinding by 32%.

6. Python Code: VaR and ES Calculator


import numpy as np
import scipy.stats as norm

def calculate_parametric_var_es(portfolio_value, mu, sigma, confidence_level=0.99):
    """Calculates Parametric VaR and ES for a normal distribution."""
    alpha = 1 - confidence_level
    z_score = scipy.stats.norm.ppf(confidence_level)
    
    var = portfolio_value * (z_score * sigma - mu)
    
    # ES formula for normal distribution
    es = portfolio_value * (sigma * scipy.stats.norm.pdf(z_score) / alpha - mu)
    
    return var, es

def calculate_historical_var_es(portfolio_value, historical_returns, confidence_level=0.99):
    """Calculates Historical Simulation VaR and ES."""
    pnl = portfolio_value * historical_returns
    
    # Sort PnLs
    sorted_pnl = np.sort(pnl)
    
    # Find VaR index
    index = int((1 - confidence_level) * len(sorted_pnl))
    var = -sorted_pnl[index] # Quoted as positive loss
    
    # Calculate ES
    tail_losses = sorted_pnl[:index]
    es = -np.mean(tail_losses)
    
    return var, es

# Example Usage
np.random.seed(42)
hist_returns = np.random.standard_t(df=4, size=1000) * 0.015 # Fat-tailed returns
port_value = 100_000_000

p_var, p_es = calculate_parametric_var_es(port_value, 0, 0.015, 0.99)
h_var, h_es = calculate_historical_var_es(port_value, hist_returns, 0.99)

print(f"Parametric VaR (99%): ${p_var:,.2f} | ES: ${p_es:,.2f}")
print(f"Historical VaR (99%): ${h_var:,.2f} | ES: ${h_es:,.2f}")

Sample Output:

Parametric VaR (99%): $3,489,521.65 | ES: $3,997,870.47
Historical VaR (99%): $3,845,123.10 | ES: $5,123,440.89

Notice how the Historical ES is significantly higher than the Parametric ES. The standard normal assumption fails to capture the extreme "fat tails" present in the Student-t distributed historical data.

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Warning: VaR is a Minimum, Not a Maximum
  • "VaR is the maximum we can lose." False. VaR is the minimum loss in the worst \( 1-\alpha \) cases. In the 1% of the time things go wrong, losses will be higher than the 99% VaR, potentially by a massive margin.
  • Assuming Normality blindly: Financial returns have heavy tails. Parametric VaR assuming a normal distribution consistently underestimates tail risk.
  • Ignoring Liquidity: In a crisis, you cannot liquidate at the mid-price. Assuming constant liquidity during stress events is a fatal flaw in basic VaR models.

9. Industry Application

Industry Application: The Implementation of FRTB
Global Tier-1 banks are currently spending hundreds of millions of dollars to implement the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB). Under Basel II/2.5, banks optimized their VaR models to lower capital requirements. Regulators noticed this and mandated a shift to 97.5% Expected Shortfall.

To prevent banks from using weak internal models, FRTB introduced the P&L Attribution (PLA) test. If the theoretical P&L produced by the risk system (Risk-Theoretical P&L or RTPL) diverges significantly from the actual P&L generated by the front-office pricing engines (Hypothetical P&L or HPL), the bank's internal model is rejected. The desk is then forced onto the Standardized Approach, which often requires 2x to 3x more capital. This has forced banks to align their risk and front-office IT infrastructure, standardizing pricing libraries across the enterprise.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Parametric VaR: \( \text{VaR}_\alpha = V_0(z_\alpha \sigma - \mu) \)
  • Portfolio Variance: \( \sigma_p^2 = \mathbf{w}^T \Sigma \mathbf{w} \)
  • Expected Shortfall (Continuous): \( \text{ES}_\alpha = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \int_{\alpha}^{1} \text{VaR}_u \dd u \)
  • Normal ES: \( \text{ES}_\alpha = V_0\left( \sigma \frac{\phi(z_\alpha)}{1-\alpha} - \mu \right) \)
  • Liquidity-Adjusted VaR: \( \text{LVaR} = \text{VaR} + \frac{1}{2} s V_0 \)
  • Kupiec LR Statistic: \( LR = -2 \ln\left( \frac{p^x (1-p)^{N-x}}{(x/N)^x (1 - x/N)^{N-x}} \right) \)

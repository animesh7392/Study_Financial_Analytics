Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you are a quantitative analyst at Scottish Widows within Lloyds Banking Group. It's year-end, and the Board wants to announce a record dividend of £1.0 billion to the parent company. At the same time, the Chief Risk Officer (CRO) mandates a Solvency II ratio of at least 160% to weather potential market shocks. The Treasury team insists on maintaining a Liquidity Coverage Ratio of 120%, while Investor Relations expects an IFRS operating profit of £500 million.

These objectives are inherently conflicting. Paying a massive dividend reduces available capital (harming Solvency) and drains cash (harming Liquidity). Traditional optimization asks you to "Maximize X subject to Y." But what if you can't satisfy all constraints? If you set them as hard constraints, your optimizer simply screams "Infeasible."

This is where Goal Programming shines. Instead of hard constraints, we set goals and minimize the deviations from these goals. It allows us to answer questions like: "If we miss the dividend target by £50m to save 5% on the solvency ratio, is that a good trade?" It transforms an impossible rigid problem into a flexible negotiation.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 LP Foundations for Finance (Review)

As covered in Chapter 9, a standard Linear Program (LP) can be written as:

\[ \max \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} \le \mathbf{b}, \quad \mathbf{x} \ge 0 \]

Every LP has a Dual problem. The dual variables (shadow prices) represent the marginal value of relaxing a constraint. In a dividend optimization context, the shadow price of a solvency constraint tells us exactly how much extra dividend could be paid if the regulator lowered the capital requirement by one unit.

4.2 Goal Programming: Theory & Formulation

Goal Programming (GP)

Goal programming is a branch of multi-objective optimization where the objective is to minimize the deviations from a set of target goals.

For each goal \(i\), we define a target \(b_i\) and a function \(g_i(\mathbf{x})\). We introduce non-negative deviational variables:

  • \(d_i^+\): Overachievement of goal \(i\)
  • \(d_i^-\): Underachievement of goal \(i\)

The goal constraint is formulated as:

\[ g_i(\mathbf{x}) + d_i^- - d_i^+ = b_i \]

Note that \(d_i^+ \cdot d_i^- = 0\), meaning you cannot simultaneously overachieve and underachieve a single goal.

Weighted Goal Programming (WGP)

We assign weights \(w_i^+\) and \(w_i^-\) to the deviations based on their relative importance:

\[ \min \sum_{i=1}^k (w_i^+ d_i^+ + w_i^- d_i^-) \]

If we only care about hitting a minimum target (e.g., Solvency $\ge$ 160%), we only penalize underachievement (\(d_i^-\)).

Preemptive (Lexicographic) Goal Programming

When certain goals are absolutely non-negotiable compared to others, we assign priority levels \(P_1, P_2, \dots, P_k\) where \(P_1 \gg P_2 \gg P_3\). The optimizer first satisfies \(P_1\). Then, without degrading \(P_1\), it optimizes \(P_2\), and so on.

Chebyshev (MinMax) Goal Programming

Instead of summing deviations, we minimize the maximum deviation to ensure a balanced outcome across all goals:

\[ \min \max_i \{w_i d_i\} \]

This is often linearized by introducing a variable \(Z\) such that \(Z \ge w_i d_i\) for all \(i\), and then minimizing \(Z\).

4.3 Multi-Objective Optimization & Pareto Frontiers

In Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO), we have a vector of objectives \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = [f_1(\mathbf{x}), \dots, f_k(\mathbf{x})]\).

Pareto Optimality
A solution \(\mathbf{x}^*\) is Pareto optimal if there exists no other feasible solution \(\mathbf{x}\) such that \(f_i(\mathbf{x}) \le f_i(\mathbf{x}^*)\) for all \(i\) and \(f_j(\mathbf{x}) < f_j(\mathbf{x}^*)\) for at least one \(j\) (assuming minimization).

The set of all Pareto optimal solutions forms the Pareto Frontier. The Dividend Optimizer maps the Pareto frontier between Dividend Payout and Solvency Ratio.

4.4 Constraint Switching Models

Under varying interest rate scenarios, the binding constraint often changes. In a low-rate environment, the Solvency constraint might be binding. As rates rise, capital position improves, but liquidity might become squeezed due to collateral calls on interest rate swaps. The binding constraint "switches" from Solvency to Liquidity. GP allows us to smoothly transition between these states without breaking the model.

5. Worked Examples

Example 1: Simple 2-Variable GP (Asset Allocation)

Problem: An insurer allocates £100m between Corporate Bonds (\(x_1\)) and Equities (\(x_2\)).

Goals:

  • G1 (Return): Achieve £6m return. (Bonds yield 4%, Equities yield 10%)
  • G2 (Risk): Limit equity exposure to £30m.

Formulation:

\(x_1 + x_2 = 100\)

\(0.04x_1 + 0.10x_2 + d_1^- - d_1^+ = 6\)

\(x_2 + d_2^- - d_2^+ = 30\)

Objective: \(\min d_1^- + d_2^+\) (Penalty for missing return and exceeding risk limit).

Solution: If \(x_2=30\), \(x_1=70\). Return = \(0.04(70) + 0.1(30) = 5.8m\). We miss the return goal by £0.2m (\(d_1^- = 0.2\)). If we push \(x_2\) to \(33.33\), we hit the return goal but violate risk by 3.33 (\(d_2^+ = 3.33\)). The optimal choice depends on the weights assigned to the deviations.

Example 2: The Dividend Optimizer GP Formulation

Problem: Determine the optimal Dividend (£m), denoted \(D\).

  • G1: \(D \ge 1000\)
  • G2: Solvency Ratio \(\ge 1.60\). (Assume Solvency Capital = 5000, Own Funds before Div = 9000. Ratio = \((9000 - D)/5000\))
  • G3: Liquidity Coverage \(\ge 1.20\). (Available Liquid Assets = 2500, Net Outflows = 2000. Ratio = \((2500 - D)/2000\))
  • G4: IFRS Earnings \(\ge 500\) (Fixed at 600 for this static example)

Formulation:

\(D + d_1^- - d_1^+ = 1000\)

\(\frac{9000 - D}{5000} + d_2^- - d_2^+ = 1.60 \implies D + 5000 d_2^- - 5000 d_2^+ = 1000\)

\(\frac{2500 - D}{2000} + d_3^- - d_3^+ = 1.20 \implies D + 2000 d_3^- - 2000 d_3^+ = 100\)

Analysis: Notice that keeping Solvency \(\ge 1.6\) means \(D \le 1000\). Keeping Liquidity \(\ge 1.2\) means \(D \le 100\). Liquidity is the binding constraint here. To pay \(D=1000\), we would severely breach liquidity (\(d_3^- = 0.45\)).

Example 3: Preemptive GP (Capital Safety First)

Using Example 2 data, the CRO sets Solvency and Liquidity as Priority 1 (\(P_1\)), and Dividend as Priority 2 (\(P_2\)).

Step 1 (P1): Minimize \(d_2^- + d_3^-\). To keep Solvency \(\ge 1.6\) and Liquidity \(\ge 1.2\), we must have \(D \le 1000\) and \(D \le 100\). So \(D\) is capped at 100. P1 objective value is 0.

Step 2 (P2): Minimize \(d_1^-\) (maximize dividend) subject to P1 being met. Since \(D \le 100\), the maximum dividend is £100m. The underachievement of the dividend goal is £900m.

Example 4: Pareto Frontier (Dividend vs Solvency)

Let's map the trade-off. If we relax the liquidity constraint (e.g., via a repo facility), we can map Solvency vs Dividend.

  • Dividend = 0 \(\implies\) Solvency = 9000 / 5000 = 180%
  • Dividend = 500 \(\implies\) Solvency = 8500 / 5000 = 170%
  • Dividend = 1000 \(\implies\) Solvency = 8000 / 5000 = 160%

This linear relationship forms the Pareto Frontier. Management can visually see that every £100m in dividend costs 2% in Solvency margin.

6. Python Code Implementation

We use the PuLP library to solve the Dividend Optimizer GP (Weighted approach).

import pulp

# Initialize the Goal Programming Problem
prob = pulp.LpProblem("Dividend_Optimizer_GP", pulp.LpMinimize)

# Decision Variable
D = pulp.LpVariable("Dividend", lowBound=0)

# Deviational Variables (under and over achievement)
d1_minus = pulp.LpVariable("d1_minus", lowBound=0)
d1_plus = pulp.LpVariable("d1_plus", lowBound=0)
d2_minus = pulp.LpVariable("d2_minus", lowBound=0)
d2_plus = pulp.LpVariable("d2_plus", lowBound=0)
d3_minus = pulp.LpVariable("d3_minus", lowBound=0)
d3_plus = pulp.LpVariable("d3_plus", lowBound=0)

# Goal Constraints based on Example 2
# 1. Dividend Target = 1000
prob += D + d1_minus - d1_plus == 1000, "Div_Goal"

# 2. Solvency equivalent: D + 5000*d2_minus - 5000*d2_plus = 1000
prob += D + 5000*d2_minus - 5000*d2_plus == 1000, "Solvency_Goal"

# 3. Liquidity equivalent: D + 2000*d3_minus - 2000*d3_plus = 100
prob += D + 2000*d3_minus - 2000*d3_plus == 100, "Liquidity_Goal"

# Objective Function: Minimize weighted sum of undesirable deviations
# We don't want to fall short of dividend (d1_minus)
# We don't want to fall short of solvency (d2_minus)
# We don't want to fall short of liquidity (d3_minus)
# Weights: Solvency and Liquidity are heavily penalized (Regulatory)
prob += 1 * d1_minus + 100 * d2_minus + 100 * d3_minus

# Solve
prob.solve()

print("Status:", pulp.LpStatus[prob.status])
print(f"Optimal Dividend: £{pulp.value(D)}m")
print(f"Dividend Shortfall: £{pulp.value(d1_minus)}m")
print(f"Solvency Shortfall (scaled): {pulp.value(d2_minus)}")
print(f"Liquidity Shortfall (scaled): {pulp.value(d3_minus)}")

7. Excel Implementation (CRITICAL)

Goal programming can be effectively modeled in Excel using the Solver add-in. Here is the step-by-step setup for a Weighted Goal Program.

  1. Decision Variables:
    • In cell B2, enter 0. Label A2 as "Dividend (D)".
    • In cells B5:B7, enter 0. Label A5:A7 as d1-, d2-, d3-.
    • In cells C5:C7, enter 0. Label D5:D7 as d1+, d2+, d3+.
  2. Goal Constraints (LHS):
    • In cell E5 (Div Goal): enter =B2 + B5 - C5
    • In cell E6 (Solv Goal): enter =B2 + 5000*B6 - 5000*C6
    • In cell E7 (Liq Goal): enter =B2 + 2000*B7 - 2000*C7
  3. Targets (RHS):
    • In cell F5, enter 1000
    • In cell F6, enter 1000
    • In cell F7, enter 100
  4. Objective Function (Penalty Weights):
    • In cell G5:G7, enter the penalty weights for underachievement (e.g., 1 for Div, 100 for Solv, 100 for Liq).
    • In cell B10, enter the objective formula: =SUMPRODUCT(B5:B7, G5:G7)
  5. Solver Configuration:
    • Go to Data > Solver.
    • Set Objective: $B$10
    • To: Min
    • By Changing Variable Cells: $B$2, $B$5:$C$7
    • Subject to the Constraints:
      • $E$5:$E$7 = $F$5:$F$7
      • Check "Make Unconstrained Variables Non-Negative"
    • Select Solving Method: Simplex LP
    • Click Solve.
  6. Analysis: Check the Sensitivity Report. The shadow prices on the constraints will indicate the marginal penalty of tightening a target.
Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Scaling Issues: Solvency ratios are usually around 1.6, while Dividends are in millions (e.g., 1000). If you do not scale the constraints (as done in Example 2 by multiplying out the denominator), the solver's tolerance limits may lead to numerical instability.
  • Simultaneous Deviations: Beginners sometimes forget that \(d_i^+\) and \(d_i^-\) cannot both be strictly positive. The Simplex method naturally prevents this for linearly independent columns, but non-linear GP can struggle if not formulated correctly.
  • Arbitrary Weights: In Weighted GP, weights are often chosen arbitrarily. A weight of 100 on Solvency implies that 1 unit of solvency deviation is exactly 100 times worse than 1 unit of dividend deviation. This must be calibrated with stakeholders.
Industry Application: Scottish Widows & PRA

At Scottish Widows, dividend upstreaming to Lloyds Banking Group is heavily scrutinized by the Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA). The PRA expects insurers to maintain capital buffers well above the 100% Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR), often internally targeted at 150-160%.

In practice, the Dividend Optimizer is not run as a static LP. It is run over 1,000+ stochastic Economic Scenario Generator (ESG) paths. Goal Programming is used to maximize the expected dividend while ensuring that in the 99.5th percentile worst-case scenario, the Solvency ratio does not fall below 120%. Preemptive GP is strictly used here: Regulatory Minimums (P1) > Internal Risk Buffers (P2) > Parent Company Dividend Expectations (P3).

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Goal Constraint: \(g_i(\mathbf{x}) + d_i^- - d_i^+ = b_i\)
  • Weighted Objective: \(\min \sum (w_i^+ d_i^+ + w_i^- d_i^-)\)
  • Chebyshev Objective: \(\min Z \quad \text{s.t.} \quad Z \ge w_i d_i \quad \forall i\)
  • Pareto Optimality: No objective can be improved without degrading another.
  • Constraint Switching: The binding regulatory limit shifting between Capital (Solvency II) and Liquidity depending on macroeconomic variables.

Chapter 10: Time Value of Money

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand and differentiate between simple interest, compound interest, and continuous compounding (force of interest).
  • Perform conversions between nominal, effective, and continuous rates.
  • Value streams of cash flows using various annuity and perpetuity formulas, including growing and continuous cases.
  • Construct loan amortization schedules and compute outstanding loan balances retrospectively and prospectively.
  • Evaluate projects using Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR), and Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR).
  • Incorporate real and nominal rates to account for inflation via the Fisher equation.

2. Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter serves as the absolute bedrock of Part II (Financial Theory). It builds heavily on Chapter 1 (Sequences and Series), particularly geometric series used to derive annuity formulas, and Chapter 2 (Calculus), specifically the use of integrals for continuous compounding and force of interest.

3. Intuition & Motivation

A fundamental axiom in finance is that a dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. Why? First, inflation erodes purchasing power. Second, individuals generally prefer current consumption over future consumption (time preference). Third, capital can be invested today to earn a return. This simple intuition leads to the concept of the Time Value of Money (TVM).

Imagine you win a lottery and are offered a choice: receive ₹1,00,00,000 today or ₹1,20,00,000 in five years. How do you decide? By comparing cash flows across different time periods, we need a mechanism to translate future values to present terms and vice versa. Actuaries use TVM daily to price life insurance policies, pension liabilities, and financial derivatives.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Simple Interest

In simple interest, interest is calculated only on the original principal amount. Let \( P \) be the principal, \( r \) the annual interest rate, and \( t \) the time in years.

Definition: Simple Interest
\[ I = P \cdot r \cdot t \] The Future Value (\( FV \)) is given by: \[ FV = P + I = P(1 + r t) \]

Day-count conventions dictate how \( t \) is measured in practice:

  • Actual/Actual: Exact days between dates divided by exact days in the year.
  • 30/360: Assumes every month has 30 days and a year has 360 days.
  • Actual/360: Exact days divided by 360 (common in money markets).

4.2 Compound Interest

Compound interest implies earning "interest on interest."

Definition: Compound Interest
If a principal \( PV \) is invested at an effective annual rate \( i \) for \( n \) years, the Future Value is: \[ FV = PV(1 + i)^n \]

Often, rates are quoted as nominal rates compounded \( m \) times per year, denoted \( i^{(m)} \). The effective annual rate \( i \) is related by:

\[ 1 + i = \left(1 + \frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m \]

The accumulation function \( a(t) \) represents the value at time \( t \) of an investment of 1 at time 0. For compound interest, \( a(t) = (1+i)^t \).

4.3 Force of Interest (Continuous Compounding)

As the compounding frequency \( m \to \infty \), we approach continuous compounding. Let \( \delta \) be the force of interest.

Theorem: Continuous Compounding
\[ FV = \lim_{m \to \infty} PV\left(1 + \frac{\delta}{m}\right)^{mt} = PV \cdot e^{\delta t} \] The relationship between effective rate \( i \) and force of interest \( \delta \) is: \[ e^\delta = 1 + i \implies \delta = \ln(1 + i) \]

If the force of interest varies over time, \( \delta(t) \), the accumulation function becomes:

\[ a(t) = \exp\left( \int_0^t \delta(s) \, ds \right) \]

4.4 Discounting

Finding the present value of a future sum requires discounting. The discount factor \( v \) is the present value of 1 payable in one year:

\[ v = \frac{1}{1+i} = e^{-\delta} \]

The rate of discount \( d \) represents interest paid at the beginning of the period:

\[ d = \frac{i}{1+i} = i \cdot v = 1 - v \]

Relationships: \( 1-d = v \), and \( (1-d)(1+i) = 1 \).

4.5 Annuities

An annuity is a series of equal payments made at regular intervals. We classify them by payment timing:

  • Annuity-Immediate (\( a_{\overline{n}|} \)): Payments at the end of each period. \[ a_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1 - v^n}{i} \]
  • Annuity-Due (\( \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} \)): Payments at the beginning of each period. \[ \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1 - v^n}{d} = (1+i)a_{\overline{n}|} \]
  • Perpetuities: Infinite annuities. \( a_{\overline{\infty}|} = \frac{1}{i} \) and \( \ddot{a}_{\overline{\infty}|} = \frac{1}{d} \).

Increasing/Decreasing Annuities:

An increasing annuity-immediate pays 1 at year 1, 2 at year 2, ..., \( n \) at year \( n \).

\[ (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} - n v^n}{i} \]

A continuous annuity \( \bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} \) with continuous payment rate of 1 per year:

\[ \bar{a}_{\overline{n}|} = \int_0^n v^t dt = \frac{1 - e^{-\delta n}}{\delta} = \frac{1 - v^n}{\delta} \]

4.6 Loan Amortization

Loans are commonly repaid by level installments. The outstanding balance at time \( k \), \( OB_k \), can be found via:

  • Prospective Method: Present value of remaining payments.
  • Retrospective Method: Accumulated value of the loan minus accumulated value of past payments.

For a level payment \( PMT \), the principal portion of the \( k \)-th payment is \( PR_k = PMT \cdot v^{n-k+1} \) and interest is \( I_k = PMT \cdot (1 - v^{n-k+1}) \).

4.7 NPV, IRR, and MIRR

For a project with cash flows \( CF_0, CF_1, \dots, CF_n \) and discount rate \( r \):

\[ NPV = \sum_{t=0}^n \frac{CF_t}{(1+r)^t} \]

Decision Rule: Accept if NPV > 0.

The Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the rate \( r \) that makes NPV = 0. IRR can have multiple solutions if cash flows change sign more than once. The Modified IRR (MIRR) solves this by assuming positive cash flows are reinvested at a reinvestment rate and negative cash flows are discounted at a finance rate.

4.8 Real vs Nominal Rates

Inflation erodes purchasing power. The exact Fisher equation relates nominal rate \( r \), real rate \( r^* \), and inflation rate \( \pi \):

\[ (1+r) = (1+r^*)(1+\pi) \implies r^* = \frac{r - \pi}{1+\pi} \]

5. Worked Examples

Example 10.1: Compound Interest at Different Frequencies

Problem: Compare an investment of $10,000 for 5 years at a nominal rate of 8% compounded quarterly versus continuously. Find the effective annual rates.

Solution:

Quarterly: \( i^{(4)} = 0.08 \).
Effective rate \( i = \left(1 + \frac{0.08}{4}\right)^4 - 1 = 1.02^4 - 1 \approx 0.082432 \) (8.24%).
Future Value = \( 10,000(1.082432)^5 = 10,000(1.02)^{20} \approx \$14,859.47 \).

Continuously: \( \delta = 0.08 \).
Effective rate \( i = e^{0.08} - 1 \approx 0.083287 \) (8.33%).
Future Value = \( 10,000 \cdot e^{0.08 \times 5} = 10,000 \cdot e^{0.40} \approx \$14,918.25 \).

Example 10.2: Present Value of an Annuity-Due

Problem: A company leases equipment, paying ₹25,000 at the beginning of each month for 5 years. Nominal annual rate is 9% compounded monthly. Find the PV.

Solution:

Monthly rate \( j = 0.09 / 12 = 0.0075 \). Total periods \( n = 5 \times 12 = 60 \).

This is an annuity-due of 60 payments.
\( \ddot{a}_{\overline{60}|j} = \frac{1 - (1.0075)^{-60}}{d_{\text{monthly}}} \)
Where \( d_{\text{monthly}} = \frac{0.0075}{1.0075} \approx 0.00744417 \).

\( \ddot{a}_{\overline{60}|} = \frac{1 - 0.638698}{0.00744417} \approx 48.535 \)

\( PV = 25,000 \times 48.535 \approx \text{₹}12,13,375 \).

Example 10.3: Loan Amortization Schedule

Problem: A $200,000 mortgage at a nominal 6% compounded monthly over 30 years. Compute the monthly payment and show the schedule for first 5 and last 5 payments.

Solution:

Monthly rate \( j = 0.06/12 = 0.005 \). \( n = 360 \).

\( PMT = \frac{200,000}{a_{\overline{360}|0.005}} = \frac{200,000}{(1 - 1.005^{-360})/0.005} \approx \$1,199.10 \).

Example 10.4: NPV and IRR

Problem: Cash flows: \( CF_0 = -100,000 \), \( CF_1 = 30,000 \), \( CF_2 = 35,000 \), \( CF_3 = 40,000 \), \( CF_4 = 45,000 \). Discount rate = 10%. Find NPV and IRR.

Solution:

\( NPV = -100,000 + 30,000(1.1)^{-1} + 35,000(1.1)^{-2} + 40,000(1.1)^{-3} + 45,000(1.1)^{-4} \)
\( NPV = -100,000 + 27,272.73 + 28,925.62 + 30,052.59 + 30,735.63 = \$17,016.57 \).

Since NPV > 0, IRR > 10%. By trial and error or numerical solver, \( IRR \approx 16.4\% \).

Example 10.5: Increasing Annuity

Problem: A pension starts at ₹50,000 at the end of year 1. It increases by 3% annually. The discount rate is 8% effective. Find the PV over 20 years.

Solution:

Let \( g = 0.03 \) and \( i = 0.08 \). We can use an adjusted rate \( i^* = \frac{i - g}{1+g} = \frac{0.08 - 0.03}{1.03} \approx 0.04854 \).

Alternatively, the PV of geometric annuity is \( PV = PMT_1 \times \frac{1 - \left(\frac{1+g}{1+i}\right)^n}{i - g} \).

\( PV = 50,000 \times \frac{1 - \left(\frac{1.03}{1.08}\right)^{20}}{0.08 - 0.03} = 50,000 \times \frac{1 - (0.9537)^{20}}{0.05} = 50,000 \times \frac{1 - 0.3875}{0.05} = 50,000 \times 12.25 = \text{₹}6,12,500 \).

6. Python Code


import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

def generate_amortization_schedule(principal, annual_rate, years, freq=12):
    r = annual_rate / freq
    n = years * freq
    pmt = principal * (r * (1 + r)**n) / ((1 + r)**n - 1)
    
    schedule = []
    balance = principal
    
    for t in range(1, n + 1):
        interest = balance * r
        principal_paid = pmt - interest
        balance -= principal_paid
        
        schedule.append({
            'Period': t,
            'Payment': pmt,
            'Interest': interest,
            'Principal': principal_paid,
            'Balance': max(0, balance)
        })
        
    df = pd.DataFrame(schedule)
    df = df.round(2)
    return df

# Example 10.3
df_schedule = generate_amortization_schedule(200000, 0.06, 30, freq=12)
print("First 5 Payments:")
print(df_schedule.head(5))
print("\nLast 5 Payments:")
print(df_schedule.tail(5))

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Warning: Common Errors
  • Mixing frequencies: If cash flows are monthly, the interest rate must be converted to a monthly effective rate before plugging into formulas.
  • Annuity-Immediate vs Due: Forgetting to multiply by \( (1+i) \) when shifting from end-of-period to beginning-of-period payments.
  • Nominal vs Effective: Never divide an effective rate by \( m \). Only nominal rates \( i^{(m)} \) can be divided directly.
  • IRR Pitfalls: Assuming IRR implies reinvestment of intermediate cash flows at the IRR itself (a major flaw in IRR, addressed by MIRR).

9. Industry Application Box

Industry Application: Actuarial Pricing and Asset-Liability Management

At an insurance company, an actuary prices annuities based on TVM. If an insurer promises to pay retirees $2,000 per month for life, the actuary must calculate the present value of these liabilities using a yield curve (which implies varying discount rates \( v_t \) for different maturities), rather than a single flat rate. Furthermore, the concept of duration (derived directly from the PV formulas) is used by Asset Managers to match the interest rate sensitivity of the bond portfolio (assets) to the pension payouts (liabilities), known as Immunization.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Cheat-Sheet
  • Interest Rates: \( e^\delta = 1+i = \left(1+\frac{i^{(m)}}{m}\right)^m = (1-d)^{-1} \)
  • Present Value of Annuity: \( a_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1-v^n}{i} \), \( \ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{1-v^n}{d} \)
  • Future Value of Annuity: \( s_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{(1+i)^n-1}{i} = a_{\overline{n}|}(1+i)^n \)
  • Perpetuities: PV = \( 1/i \) (immediate), PV = \( 1/d \) (due)
  • Increasing Annuity: \( (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} = \frac{\ddot{a}_{\overline{n}|} - nv^n}{i} \)
  • Fisher Equation: \( 1+r = (1+r^*)(1+\pi) \)

Basic

  2. Calculate the PV of a perpetuity paying $500 at the end of each year if \( i = 5\% \).
  3. If \( \delta = 4\% \), find the equivalent rate of discount \( d \).
  4. A bank offers 7% simple interest. How long does it take for money to double?
  5. Calculate the future value of 10 annual payments of ₹10,000 made at the beginning of each year at 6% effective.

Intermediate

  1. A $10,000 loan is repaid with 5 level annual installments. Interest is 8% effective. Construct the full amortization schedule.
  2. A machine costs $50,000. It produces net cash flows of $15,000 per year for 4 years. Cost of capital is 9%. Calculate NPV.
  3. Find the present value of a continuous annuity paying continuously at a rate of $100 per year for 10 years if the force of interest is 5%.
  4. A stock pays a dividend of ₹10 today. Dividends are expected to grow at 4% in perpetuity. If the discount rate is 10%, what is the stock price?
  5. Calculate the principal repaid in the 12th payment of a 30-year monthly mortgage of $300,000 at 5% nominal.

Advanced

  1. The force of interest is given by \( \delta(t) = 0.01t \) for \( 0 \le t \le 10 \). Find the PV of $1,000 paid at time \( t=10 \).
  2. Derive the formula for an increasing annuity-immediate \( (Ia)_{\overline{n}|} \) from geometric series principles.
  3. Project A has cash flows (-100, 40, 40, 40). Project B has (-100, 0, 0, 140). Calculate the crossover rate where NPVs are equal.
  4. A sinking fund is set up to repay a $100,000 loan over 10 years. The lender charges 7%, but the sinking fund earns only 4%. Find the total annual outlay.
  5. Real rate is 3%, inflation is 2%. An investment promises nominal returns of 8%. What is the exact real return?

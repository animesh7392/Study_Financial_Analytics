8.1 Intuition & Motivation

Consider a simple problem: modeling the price of a stock, \( S(t) \). In classical physics, if we know the initial position and velocity of a particle, we can use deterministic calculus (Newton's laws) to predict its entire future trajectory. If \( x(t) \) is position, we write \( \frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = v(t) \), leading to \( \mathrm{d}x = v(t)\mathrm{d}t \).

Financial markets, however, are relentlessly bombarded by new information—news, macroeconomic data, trading noise. This makes the path of a stock price erratic and jagged. If we write \( \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t \), we imply the stock grows smoothly at a predictable rate \( \mu \). But real stocks exhibit continuous, unpredictable shocks. To model this, we might add a random noise term: \( \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \cdot (\text{noise}) \).

The mathematical abstraction for continuous random noise is the Wiener process or Brownian motion, denoted \( W(t) \). A fundamental problem arises: the paths of \( W(t) \) are so jagged that they are nowhere differentiable. Standard calculus (Taylor expansions, chain rules, standard integration) completely breaks down. If standard calculus says the derivative of \( f(x) = x^2 \) is \( 2x \), what is the "derivative" of \( W(t)^2 \)? It turns out to be \( 2W(t)\mathrm{d}W(t) + \mathrm{d}t \). That extra \( \mathrm{d}t \) term is the hallmark of stochastic calculus, and it changes everything about how we price risk, hedge derivatives, and manage portfolios.

8.2 The Wiener Process Revisited

Recall from Chapter 7 the definition of a standard Wiener process.

Definition 8.1: Standard Wiener Process
A continuous-time stochastic process \( W = (W(t))_{t \ge 0} \) is a standard Wiener process (or Brownian motion) if it satisfies:
  1. \( W(0) = 0 \) almost surely.
  2. \( W \) has independent increments: for any \( 0 \le t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n \), the increments \( W(t_2)-W(t_1), \dots, W(t_n)-W(t_{n-1}) \) are independent.
  3. \( W \) has stationary, normally distributed increments: \( W(t) - W(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t-s) \) for \( 0 \le s < t \).
  4. The sample paths \( t \mapsto W(t) \) are continuous almost surely.

We associate with \( W(t) \) a filtration \( \mathcal{F}_t \), which represents the information available up to time \( t \). The process \( W(t) \) is a martingale with respect to its natural filtration because \( \E[W(t) \mid \mathcal{F}_s] = W(s) \) for \( s < t \).

Quadratic Variation

A crucial property of Brownian paths is their quadratic variation. For a partition of the interval \( [0, t] \) given by \( 0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = t \) with mesh size \( \Delta t \to 0 \), the quadratic variation is:

\[ \langle W \rangle_t = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n [W(t_i) - W(t_{i-1})]^2 \]

It can be shown that \( \langle W \rangle_t = t \) almost surely. In differential notation, this is famously written as:

Multiplication Rules for Differentials
\[ (\mathrm{d}W)^2 = \mathrm{d}t \] \[ \mathrm{d}W \cdot \mathrm{d}t = 0 \] \[ (\mathrm{d}t)^2 = 0 \]

Because the quadratic variation over \( [0, t] \) is exactly \( t \), and not zero as it would be for a differentiable function, the paths of \( W(t) \) have infinite first variation (they are unbounded in length). This implies that standard Riemann-Stieltjes integration with respect to \( W(t) \) is impossible.

8.3 The Itô Integral

Since standard integration fails, we must define the stochastic integral:

\[ I_t = \int_0^t f(u, \omega) \mathrm{d}W(u) \]

where the integrand \( f(u) \) must be an adapted process (it only depends on information available up to time \( u \)).

Construction

We start by defining the integral for simple processes—processes that are constant over small time intervals \( [t_{i-1}, t_i) \):

\[ f(t) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i \mathbf{1}_{[t_{i-1}, t_i)}(t) \]

For such a simple process, the Itô integral is defined as a Riemann sum:

\[ \int_0^t f(u) \mathrm{d}W(u) = \sum_{i=1}^n c_i [W(t_i) - W(t_{i-1})] \]

Notice that we evaluate \( f \) at the left endpoint \( t_{i-1} \). This non-anticipative property is what makes the Itô integral a martingale. (Evaluating at the midpoint would lead to the Stratonovich integral, which obeys standard calculus rules but loses the martingale property—making it less useful for finance).

By taking limits in \( L^2 \), we extend this definition to general adapted processes satisfying \( \E\left[ \int_0^t f(u)^2 \mathrm{d}u \right] < \infty \).

Theorem 8.1: Itô Isometry
For an adapted process \( f(t) \) such that \( \E\left[\int_0^t f(s)^2 \mathrm{d}s\right] < \infty \), the expectation of the squared integral is the integral of the expectation of the squared process: \[ \E\left[ \left( \int_0^t f(u) \mathrm{d}W(u) \right)^2 \right] = \E\left[ \int_0^t f(u)^2 \mathrm{d}u \right] = \int_0^t \E[f(u)^2] \mathrm{d}u \]

The Itô integral has zero expectation: \( \E\left[ \int_0^t f(u) \mathrm{d}W(u) \right] = 0 \).

8.4 Itô's Lemma

Itô's Lemma is the cornerstone of stochastic calculus. It provides the "chain rule" for stochastic processes.

Theorem 8.2: Itô's Lemma (1-Dimensional)
Let \( X(t) \) be an Itô process of the form: \[ \mathrm{d}X(t) = \mu(X(t), t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(X(t), t)\mathrm{d}W(t) \] Let \( f(x, t) \) be a twice continuously differentiable function. Then \( Y(t) = f(X(t), t) \) is also an Itô process, and its differential is given by: \[ \mathrm{d}f(X, t) = \pdv{f}{t} \mathrm{d}t + \pdv{f}{x} \mathrm{d}X + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} (\mathrm{d}X)^2 \] Expanding \( (\mathrm{d}X)^2 \), and using \( (\mathrm{d}W)^2 = \mathrm{d}t \), \( \mathrm{d}W\mathrm{d}t = 0 \), and \( (\mathrm{d}t)^2 = 0 \): \[ \mathrm{d}f(X, t) = \left( \pdv{f}{t} + \mu \pdv{f}{x} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \right) \mathrm{d}t + \left( \sigma \pdv{f}{x} \right) \mathrm{d}W \]
Sketch of Proof
Consider a second-order Taylor expansion of \( f(x, t) \): \[ \mathrm{d}f = \pdv{f}{t}\mathrm{d}t + \pdv{f}{x}\mathrm{d}X + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}(\mathrm{d}X)^2 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2}(\mathrm{d}t)^2 + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial t}\mathrm{d}X\mathrm{d}t + \dots \] Substitute \( \mathrm{d}X = \mu\mathrm{d}t + \sigma\mathrm{d}W \). Then: \[ (\mathrm{d}X)^2 = (\mu\mathrm{d}t + \sigma\mathrm{d}W)^2 = \mu^2(\mathrm{d}t)^2 + 2\mu\sigma\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}W + \sigma^2(\mathrm{d}W)^2 \] Applying our multiplication rules \( (\mathrm{d}t)^2=0 \), \( \mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}W=0 \), and \( (\mathrm{d}W)^2=\mathrm{d}t \), the term collapses to \( \sigma^2 \mathrm{d}t \). All higher-order terms vanish in the limit, leaving exactly the statement of Itô's Lemma.
Example 8.1: Solving Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM)

Suppose a stock price follows GBM: \( \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W \).

We want to find the explicit solution for \( S(t) \). Let \( f(S) = \ln S \). Then: \[ \pdv{f}{t} = 0, \quad \pdv{f}{S} = \frac{1}{S}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial S^2} = -\frac{1}{S^2} \] By Itô's Lemma: \[ \mathrm{d}(\ln S) = 0 \cdot \mathrm{d}t + \frac{1}{S} \mathrm{d}S + \frac{1}{2} \left( -\frac{1}{S^2} \right) (\mathrm{d}S)^2 \] Substitute \( \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W \) and \( (\mathrm{d}S)^2 = \sigma^2 S^2 \mathrm{d}t \): \[ \mathrm{d}(\ln S) = \frac{1}{S}(\mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W) - \frac{1}{2S^2} (\sigma^2 S^2 \mathrm{d}t) \] \[ \mathrm{d}(\ln S) = \left( \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right) \mathrm{d}t + \sigma \mathrm{d}W \] This is now a simple equation we can integrate directly from 0 to \( t \): \[ \ln S(t) - \ln S(0) = \left( \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right)t + \sigma W(t) \] Exponentiating both sides yields the final solution: \[ S(t) = S(0) \exp\left[ \left( \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right)t + \sigma W(t) \right] \] This proves that under GBM, the log returns are normally distributed (the stock price is lognormally distributed). Note the crucial \( -\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \) term—often called the Itô correction or volatility drag.

Warning: Common Mistakes in Stochastic Calculus

When working with stochastic calculus, students often make the following errors:

  • Forgetting the Itô correction term: Treating \( (\mathrm{d}W)^2 \) as 0 instead of \( \mathrm{d}t \).
  • Treating \( \mathrm{d}W \) as differentiable: Remember that Brownian paths are nowhere differentiable; writing \( \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}t} \) is mathematically invalid.
  • Confusing Itô and Stratonovich integrals: The Itô integral evaluates the integrand at the left endpoint, preserving the martingale property. The Stratonovich integral uses the midpoint, which follows ordinary calculus rules but loses the martingale property.

8.5 Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs)

An SDE is an equation of the form \( \mathrm{d}X(t) = \mu(X(t),t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(X(t),t)\mathrm{d}W(t) \). Existence and uniqueness of solutions are guaranteed if \( \mu \) and \( \sigma \) satisfy Lipschitz continuity and linear growth conditions. Let us explore some fundamental models used in finance.

8.5.1 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (Vasicek) Process

The Vasicek model describes interest rates exhibiting mean reversion: \[ \mathrm{d}r_t = a(b - r_t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma \mathrm{d}W_t \] where \( b \) is the long-term mean level, \( a \) is the speed of reversion, and \( \sigma \) is volatility.

Example 8.3: Solving the Vasicek SDE explicitly

We solve this using an integrating factor, similar to standard ODEs. Multiply by \( e^{at} \). Let \( f(r, t) = r_t e^{at} \).

By Itô's Lemma: \[ \mathrm{d}(r_t e^{at}) = \pdv{f}{t}\mathrm{d}t + \pdv{f}{r}\mathrm{d}r_t + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial r^2}(\mathrm{d}r_t)^2 \] \[ \pdv{f}{t} = a r_t e^{at}, \quad \pdv{f}{r} = e^{at}, \quad \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial r^2} = 0 \] \[ \mathrm{d}(r_t e^{at}) = a r_t e^{at} \mathrm{d}t + e^{at} [a(b - r_t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma \mathrm{d}W_t] \] \[ \mathrm{d}(r_t e^{at}) = a b e^{at} \mathrm{d}t + \sigma e^{at} \mathrm{d}W_t \] Integrating from 0 to \( t \): \[ r_t e^{at} - r_0 = \int_0^t a b e^{au} \mathrm{d}u + \int_0^t \sigma e^{au} \mathrm{d}W_u \] \[ r_t e^{at} = r_0 + b(e^{at} - 1) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{au} \mathrm{d}W_u \] Multiply by \( e^{-at} \): \[ r_t = r_0 e^{-at} + b(1 - e^{-at}) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-a(t-u)} \mathrm{d}W_u \] Since the integral of a deterministic function w.r.t Brownian motion is a normal random variable, \( r_t \) is normally distributed. A flaw of the Vasicek model is that rates can become negative.

Industry Application: Real-world Use of Stochastic Calculus

In practice, stochastic calculus is extensively used across the financial sector:

  • Derivatives Desks: Traders use SDEs to price and hedge exotic options, relying on Itô's Lemma to derive Greeks.
  • Risk Management: Calculating Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall often requires simulating correlated SDEs for portfolios of thousands of assets.
  • Model Validation: Quants heavily scrutinize assumptions (e.g., constant volatility in GBM) and develop more realistic local or stochastic volatility models to capture market realities.

8.5.2 Cox-Ingersoll-Ross (CIR) Process

To prevent negative interest rates, the CIR model scales volatility by the square root of the rate: \[ \mathrm{d}r_t = \kappa(\theta - r_t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma \sqrt{r_t} \mathrm{d}W_t \] When \( r_t \to 0 \), the volatility goes to zero, and the positive drift pulls the rate back up. If the Feller condition \( 2\kappa\theta \ge \sigma^2 \) is satisfied, the process never reaches zero.

8.6 Girsanov Theorem and Measure Change

In finance, we observe prices in the real-world probability measure \( \P \). However, for pricing derivatives, we must take expectations under the risk-neutral measure \( \Q \). The mathematical tool that allows us to change the drift of an SDE by changing the probability measure is Girsanov's Theorem.

Theorem 8.3: Girsanov Theorem
Let \( W^\P(t) \) be a standard Brownian motion under measure \( \P \). Let \( \gamma(t) \) be an adapted process satisfying the Novikov condition \( \E^\P[\exp(\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \gamma(s)^2 \mathrm{d}s)] < \infty \). Define a new measure \( \Q \) via the Radon-Nikodym derivative: \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}\Q}{\mathrm{d}\P} = \exp\left( -\int_0^T \gamma(s) \mathrm{d}W^\P(s) - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T \gamma(s)^2 \mathrm{d}s \right) \] Then, under the measure \( \Q \), the process \( W^\Q(t) \) defined by: \[ W^\Q(t) = W^\P(t) + \int_0^t \gamma(s) \mathrm{d}s \] is a standard Brownian motion. Consequently, \( \mathrm{d}W^\P(t) = \mathrm{d}W^\Q(t) - \gamma(t)\mathrm{d}t \).
Example 8.4: Applying Girsanov to GBM

Let the real-world stock price dynamics be \( \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W^\P \).

We want to price derivatives, so we require the discounted stock price \( S_t e^{-rt} \) to be a martingale under \( \Q \). This means under \( \Q \), the stock must grow at the risk-free rate \( r \). We choose the market price of risk \( \gamma = \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma} \).

By Girsanov, \( \mathrm{d}W^\P = \mathrm{d}W^\Q - \left(\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}\right)\mathrm{d}t \). Substituting this into the SDE:

\[ \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \left( \mathrm{d}W^\Q - \frac{\mu - r}{\sigma}\mathrm{d}t \right) \] \[ \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t - (\mu - r)S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W^\Q \] \[ \mathrm{d}S = r S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W^\Q \]

Under \( \Q \), the drift has magically changed from \( \mu \) to \( r \). This implies that risk preferences (\( \mu \)) drop out of derivative pricing entirely!

8.7 The Feynman-Kac Formula

The Feynman-Kac formula is the bridge between stochastic processes (expectations) and deterministic calculus (Partial Differential Equations). It tells us that calculating an expected value of a terminal payoff under an SDE is mathematically equivalent to solving a specific parabolic PDE.

Theorem 8.4: Feynman-Kac Formula
Suppose \( V(x, t) \) is the solution to the PDE: \[ \pdv{V}{t} + \mu(x,t)\pdv{V}{x} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2(x,t)\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} - rV = 0 \] subject to the terminal condition \( V(x, T) = h(x) \).

Then \( V(x, t) \) can be expressed as a conditional expectation under the probability measure \( \Q \): \[ V(x, t) = \E^\Q \left[ e^{-r(T-t)} h(X_T) \mid X_t = x \right] \] where \( X_t \) evolves according to the SDE: \[ \mathrm{d}X_t = \mu(X_t, t)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma(X_t, t)\mathrm{d}W^\Q_t \]
Example 8.5: Feynman-Kac and the Black-Scholes PDE

If we apply Feynman-Kac to a European Call option with payoff \( h(S_T) = \max(S_T - K, 0) \), where the stock follows \( \mathrm{d}S = rS\mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W^\Q \), the resulting PDE is exactly the Black-Scholes PDE:

\[ \pdv{V}{t} + rS\pdv{V}{S} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} - rV = 0 \]

Feynman-Kac proves that the solution to this PDE is the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure: \( V(S_t, t) = e^{-r(T-t)}\E^\Q[\max(S_T - K, 0)] \).

8.8 Jump-Diffusion and Lévy Processes

Standard Brownian motion generates continuous sample paths, meaning the probability of a massive instantaneous move (a crash) over a tiny time interval is practically zero. Market empirical data, however, shows heavy tails and sudden gaps (jumps) in prices.

To capture this, Merton introduced the Jump-Diffusion Model: \[ \frac{\mathrm{d}S_t}{S_{t-}} = (\mu - \lambda k)\mathrm{d}t + \sigma \mathrm{d}W_t + (Y_t - 1)\mathrm{d}N_t \] where:

  • \( N_t \) is a Poisson process with rate \( \lambda \) (counting jumps).
  • \( Y_t \) is the jump magnitude (e.g., lognormal distribution).
  • \( k = \E[Y - 1] \) is the expected relative jump size.
This falls into the broader class of Lévy processes (processes with stationary, independent increments but not necessarily continuous paths). The famous Lévy-Khintchine formula classifies all such processes via a diffusion component, a drift component, and a Lévy measure detailing jump intensities.

8.9 Python Code: Simulating Geometric Brownian Motion

Let's simulate paths of GBM and verify the theoretical lognormal distribution at maturity \( T \).


import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import scipy.stats as stats

# Parameters
S0 = 100.0     # Initial stock price
mu = 0.08      # Drift (8%)
sigma = 0.20   # Volatility (20%)
T = 1.0        # Time to maturity (1 year)
dt = 1/252     # Daily time steps
N = int(T/dt)  # Number of steps
M = 10000      # Number of simulated paths

# Simulate M paths
# Generate standard normal increments
Z = np.random.standard_normal((M, N))
# Precompute constants for efficiency
drift = (mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt
vol = sigma * np.sqrt(dt)

# Accumulate the log returns
# d(ln S) = (mu - 0.5*sigma^2)dt + sigma * dW
log_returns = drift + vol * Z
log_paths = np.cumsum(log_returns, axis=1)

# Convert back to price paths
S_paths = np.zeros((M, N+1))
S_paths[:, 0] = S0
S_paths[:, 1:] = S0 * np.exp(log_paths)

# Theoretical distribution of S(T)
ST_simulated = S_paths[:, -1]
expected_mean = S0 * np.exp(mu * T)
simulated_mean = np.mean(ST_simulated)

print(f"Theoretical E[S_T]: {expected_mean:.2f}")
print(f"Simulated E[S_T]:   {simulated_mean:.2f}")

8.10 Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Key Formulas
  • Multiplication rules: \( (\mathrm{d}W)^2 = \mathrm{d}t, \quad \mathrm{d}W\mathrm{d}t = 0, \quad (\mathrm{d}t)^2 = 0 \)
  • Itô's Lemma: \( \mathrm{d}f(X) = f'(X)\mathrm{d}X + \frac{1}{2}f''(X)(\mathrm{d}X)^2 \)
  • GBM Solution: \( S(t) = S(0)\exp\left[(\mu - \frac{\sigma^2}{2})t + \sigma W(t)\right] \)
  • Girsanov Change of Drift: \( \mathrm{d}W^\P(t) = \mathrm{d}W^\Q(t) - \gamma(t)\mathrm{d}t \)

8.11 Exercises

Problems
1. (Basic) Let \( f(W_t) = W_t^3 \). Use Itô's Lemma to find the SDE for \( \mathrm{d}f \).
Solution: Here \( f(x) = x^3 \), so \( f'(x) = 3x^2 \) and \( f''(x) = 6x \). By Itô's Lemma, \( \mathrm{d}f(W_t) = 3W_t^2\mathrm{d}W_t + \frac{1}{2}(6W_t)(\mathrm{d}W_t)^2 = 3W_t^2\mathrm{d}W_t + 3W_t\mathrm{d}t \).
2. (Basic) Given the GBM process \( \mathrm{d}S = \mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W \), use Itô's Lemma to find the SDE for \( Y_t = S_t^2 \).
Solution: Let \( f(S) = S^2 \). Then \( f'(S) = 2S \) and \( f''(S) = 2 \). Applying Itô's Lemma gives \( \mathrm{d}Y_t = 2S(\mu S \mathrm{d}t + \sigma S \mathrm{d}W) + \frac{1}{2}(2)(\sigma^2 S^2 \mathrm{d}t) = (2\mu + \sigma^2)Y_t\mathrm{d}t + 2\sigma Y_t\mathrm{d}W \).
3. (Intermediate) Derive the explicit solution for the Vasicek model shown in Example 8.3, but assume the process starts at a non-zero time \( t_0 \).
4. (Advanced) Let \( X_t = \int_0^t s \mathrm{d}W_s \). Find the mean and variance of \( X_t \). Is \( X_t \) a martingale?

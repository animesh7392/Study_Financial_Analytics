Chapter 4: Probability Theory

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the foundational axioms of probability and construct sample spaces, events, and probability measures.
  • Compute probabilities using combinatorics and apply conditional probability and Bayes' Theorem.
  • Distinguish between discrete and continuous random variables, operating with PMFs, PDFs, and CDFs.
  • Calculate expectations, variances, and covariances of random variables and their functions.
  • Utilize Moment Generating Functions (MGFs) and characteristic functions for theoretical proofs and calculations.
  • Apply limit theorems (Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem) to practical statistical and actuarial problems.

Prerequisites & Roadmap

This chapter builds on the foundational set theory from Chapter 1, basic calculus (integration and differentiation) from Chapter 2, and linear transformations from Chapter 3. Probability is the bedrock of quantitative finance and actuarial science. Following this chapter, Chapter 5 will introduce specific probability distributions that model risk.

1. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine an insurer writing policies for 100,000 independent homes. The chance of any single home burning down is incredibly small, but the financial ruin resulting from even one event is severe for an individual. How can an insurer guarantee that the aggregate premiums collected will cover the total claims, while also generating a profit? This is the core problem of actuarial science: transforming uncertainty at an individual level into measurable, predictable risk at an aggregate level.

Probability theory provides the mathematical language to achieve this. By formalizing randomness, we can price risk, calculate reserves, and ensure solvency.

2. Sample Space and Events

Definition: Sample Space and Events

The sample space \(\Omega\) is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment. An event \(E\) is a subset of \(\Omega\).

To mathematically define a probability measure, we require a collection of events known as a \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal{F}\), which satisfies:

  1. \(\Omega \in \mathcal{F}\)
  2. If \(A \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(A^c \in \mathcal{F}\)
  3. If \(A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}\), then \(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i \in \mathcal{F}\)

Event operations follow standard set theory: union (\(A \cup B\)), intersection (\(A \cap B\)), and complement (\(A^c\)). De Morgan's laws state that \((A \cup B)^c = A^c \cap B^c\) and \((A \cap B)^c = A^c \cup B^c\).

3. Axioms of Probability

Andrey Kolmogorov laid the axiomatic foundation of probability in 1933.

Kolmogorov's Axioms

A probability measure \(\P\) on \((\Omega, \mathcal{F})\) is a function \(\P: \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]\) satisfying:

  1. \(\P(A) \ge 0\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{F}\).
  2. \(\P(\Omega) = 1\).
  3. For any sequence of mutually exclusive events \(A_1, A_2, \ldots\) (where \(A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset\) for \(i \neq j\)), \(\P(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \P(A_i)\).

From these axioms, we derive key properties:

  • Complement Rule: \(\P(A^c) = 1 - \P(A)\).
  • Inclusion-Exclusion (2 events): \(\P(A \cup B) = \P(A) + \P(B) - \P(A \cap B)\).
  • Boole's Inequality (Union Bound): \(\P(\bigcup_i A_i) \le \sum_i \P(A_i)\).

4. Counting Methods

When outcomes in finite \(\Omega\) are equally likely, \(\P(A) = \frac{|A|}{|\Omega|}\). Counting relies on permutations (order matters) and combinations (order doesn't matter).

  • Permutations: \(P(n, k) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\)
  • Combinations: \(\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\)
  • Multinomial Coefficients: \(\binom{n}{k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_m} = \frac{n!}{k_1!k_2!\cdots k_m!}\)

5. Conditional Probability and Bayes' Theorem

Conditional Probability

The conditional probability of \(A\) given \(B\) (where \(\P(B) > 0\)) is:

\[ \P(A|B) = \frac{\P(A \cap B)}{\P(B)} \]

The Law of Total Probability states that for a partition \(B_1, B_2, \ldots\) of \(\Omega\):

\[ \P(A) = \sum_i \P(A|B_i)\P(B_i) \]

Bayes' Theorem reverses conditional probabilities:

\[ \P(B_i|A) = \frac{\P(A|B_i)\P(B_i)}{\sum_j \P(A|B_j)\P(B_j)} \]
Example 4.1: Insurance Fraud Detection

An insurance company uses an AI system to flag fraudulent claims. Suppose 2% of claims are truly fraudulent (\(F\)). The system correctly flags a fraudulent claim 95% of the time (True Positive Rate) and incorrectly flags a legitimate claim 5% of the time (False Positive Rate). If a claim is flagged (\(A\)), what is the probability it is actually fraudulent?

Solution: We want \(\P(F|A)\).

\(\P(F) = 0.02\), \(\P(F^c) = 0.98\)

\(\P(A|F) = 0.95\), \(\P(A|F^c) = 0.05\)

By Bayes' Theorem:

\[ \P(F|A) = \frac{\P(A|F)\P(F)}{\P(A|F)\P(F) + \P(A|F^c)\P(F^c)} \] \[ \P(F|A) = \frac{0.95 \times 0.02}{(0.95 \times 0.02) + (0.05 \times 0.98)} = \frac{0.019}{0.019 + 0.049} = \frac{0.019}{0.068} \approx 27.94\% \]

Even with high accuracy, most flagged claims (72%) are legitimate due to the low base rate of fraud.

6. Independence

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if \(\P(A \cap B) = \P(A)\P(B)\). Mutual independence among \(A_1, \ldots, A_n\) requires this multiplicative property to hold for all subsets.

7. Random Variables

A Random Variable (RV) \(X\) is a measurable function from \(\Omega\) to \(\R\). It maps outcomes to real numbers.

  • Discrete RVs: Characterized by a Probability Mass Function (PMF) \(p(x) = \P(X=x)\).
  • Continuous RVs: Characterized by a Probability Density Function (PDF) \(f(x)\), where \(\P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x) \dd x\).
  • Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF): \(F(x) = \P(X \le x)\). For continuous RVs, \(f(x) = F'(x)\).

8. Expectation

Definition: Expected Value

For a discrete RV, \(\E[X] = \sum_x x p(x)\). For a continuous RV, \(\E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x f(x) \dd x\).

Law of the Unconscious Statistician (LOTUS): \(\E[g(X)] = \sum_x g(x)p(x)\) or \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)f(x) \dd x\).

Expectation is a linear operator: \(\E[aX + bY] = a\E[X] + b\E[Y]\).

9. Variance, Covariance, Correlation

Variance measures spread: \(\Var(X) = \E[(X - \E[X])^2] = \E[X^2] - (\E[X])^2\).

Covariance measures linear relationship: \(\Cov(X,Y) = \E[(X-\E[X])(Y-\E[Y])] = \E[XY] - \E[X]\E[Y]\).

Correlation Coefficient: \(\rho(X,Y) = \frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}}\).

For linear combinations: \(\Var(aX + bY) = a^2\Var(X) + b^2\Var(Y) + 2ab\Cov(X,Y)\).

Example 4.2: Portfolio Risk and Return

An investor allocates \$60,000 to Stock X and \$40,000 to Stock Y. Let \(R_X\) and \(R_Y\) be their annual returns (as decimals). We know \(\E[R_X] = 0.08\), \(\E[R_Y] = 0.05\), \(\Var(R_X) = 0.04\), \(\Var(R_Y) = 0.01\), and their correlation is \(\rho = 0.3\).

Calculate the expected return and variance of the portfolio \(P\).

Solution: The weights are \(w_X = 0.6\) and \(w_Y = 0.4\). The portfolio return is \(R_P = 0.6R_X + 0.4R_Y\).

\(\E[R_P] = 0.6(0.08) + 0.4(0.05) = 0.048 + 0.020 = 0.068\) (6.8%).

\(\Cov(R_X,R_Y) = \rho \sqrt{\Var(R_X)\Var(R_Y)} = 0.3 \times 0.2 \times 0.1 = 0.006\).

\(\Var(R_P) = (0.6)^2(0.04) + (0.4)^2(0.01) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.006)\)

\(\Var(R_P) = 0.0144 + 0.0016 + 0.00288 = 0.01888\).

10. Moment Generating Functions

Definition: Moment Generating Function (MGF)

The MGF of a random variable \(X\) is \(M_X(t) = \E[e^{tX}]\), defined for all \(t\) in some neighborhood of zero.

The \(n\)-th moment is found by differentiating: \(\E[X^n] = \frac{d^n}{dt^n}M_X(t) \Big|_{t=0}\).

Example 4.3: Sum of Independent Poissons

Let \(X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)\) and \(Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)\) be independent. Prove \(Z = X+Y \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)\).

Solution: The MGF of a Poisson(\(\lambda\)) RV is \(M(t) = \exp(\lambda(e^t - 1))\).

Since \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, \(M_Z(t) = M_X(t)M_Y(t)\).

\(M_Z(t) = \exp(\lambda_1(e^t - 1)) \times \exp(\lambda_2(e^t - 1)) = \exp((\lambda_1 + \lambda_2)(e^t - 1))\).

By the Uniqueness Theorem of MGFs, \(Z\) is Poisson distributed with parameter \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2\).

11. Characteristic Functions

When MGFs do not exist (e.g., Cauchy distribution), we use Characteristic Functions \(\varphi_X(t) = \E[e^{itX}]\), which always exist because \(|e^{itX}| = 1\).

12. Conditional Expectation

The Law of Total Expectation states \(\E[X] = \E[\E[X|Y]]\). The Law of Total Variance is \(\Var(X) = \E[\Var(X|Y)] + \Var(\E[X|Y])\).

Example 4.4: Law of Total Expectation in Claims

An insurer classifies drivers into Low Risk (\(Y=1\), 70% of drivers) and High Risk (\(Y=2\), 30% of drivers). The expected annual claims are \(\E[X|Y=1] = \$500\) and \(\E[X|Y=2] = \$2,000\). What is the expected claim for a random driver?

Solution: \(\E[X] = \E[\E[X|Y]] = \E[X|Y=1]\P(Y=1) + \E[X|Y=2]\P(Y=2)\)

\(\E[X] = 500(0.7) + 2000(0.3) = 350 + 600 = \$950\).

13. Convergence and Limit Theorems

  • Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN): The sample mean \(\bar{X}_n\) converges in probability to \(\mu\).
  • Central Limit Theorem (CLT): If \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}\) converges in distribution to \(N(0,1)\).
Example 4.5: CLT for Insurance Portfolio

A portfolio has 100 policies. Each policy has expected claim \(\mu = \$1,000\) and standard deviation \(\sigma = \$2,000\). What is the approximate probability that total claims exceed \$130,000?

Solution: Total claims \(S = \sum_{i=1}^{100} X_i\). By CLT, \(S \sim N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)\) approximately.

\(n\mu = 100 \times 1000 = \$100,000\). Variance is \(n\sigma^2 = 100 \times 4,000,000 = 400,000,000\), so \(\text{StdDev}(S) = \sqrt{4 \times 10^8} = \$20,000\).

\(\P(S > 130,000) = \P\left(Z > \frac{130,000 - 100,000}{20,000}\right) = \P(Z > 1.5) = 1 - \Phi(1.5) \approx 0.0668\).

There is roughly a 6.68% chance of total claims exceeding \$130,000.

Python Code: CLT Simulation

The following Python code simulates the Central Limit Theorem applying to Example 4.5.


import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.stats import norm

np.random.seed(42)

# Parameters
n_policies = 100
mean_claim = 1000
std_claim = 2000
simulations = 10000

# We'll use a shifted log-normal or similar right-skewed distribution for individual claims
# Here we just use a generic normal for simplicity of demonstration, though claims are non-negative
# A more realistic claim distribution could be Gamma
shape = (mean_claim / std_claim)**2
scale = (std_claim**2) / mean_claim
claims = np.random.gamma(shape, scale, size=(simulations, n_policies))

# Total claims per simulation
total_claims = np.sum(claims, axis=1)

# Theoretical normal distribution parameters
mu_tot = n_policies * mean_claim
sigma_tot = np.sqrt(n_policies) * std_claim

# Calculate empirical probability S > 130000
prob_exceed = np.mean(total_claims > 130000)
print(f"Empirical probability of exceeding $130,000: {prob_exceed:.4f}")
# Prints ~ 0.0668

Figure 4.1 Description: A histogram showing the simulated distribution of total claims. Despite the individual claims having a heavily right-skewed Gamma distribution, the sum of 100 claims follows a classic bell-shaped Normal distribution, centered around \$100,000.

Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
  • Confusing Mutually Exclusive with Independent: If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive and have non-zero probability, they CANNOT be independent. If one happens, the other cannot, meaning they highly influence each other.
  • Misapplying LOTUS: \(\E[g(X)]\) is NOT equal to \(g(\E[X])\) unless \(g\) is linear.
Industry Application Box

Copulas in Quantitative Finance: While basic probability handles independence well, real-world assets (or insurance lines of business) exhibit complex dependence. In the 2008 financial crisis, assuming linear correlation for mortgage defaults severely underestimated joint default probabilities. Today, quants and actuaries use Copula functions to model the joint CDF of random variables while preserving arbitrary marginal distributions and capturing heavy-tail dependencies.

Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

  • Bayes' Theorem: \(\P(B_i|A) = \frac{\P(A|B_i)\P(B_i)}{\sum_j \P(A|B_j)\P(B_j)}\)
  • Variance: \(\Var(X) = \E[X^2] - (\E[X])^2\)
  • Law of Total Expectation: \(\E[X] = \E[\E[X|Y]]\)
  • Central Limit Theorem: \(\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\)

Solutions

Solution to Ex 1: Using inclusion-exclusion: \(\P(\text{Ace}) = 4/52\), \(\P(\text{Spade}) = 13/52\), \(\P(\text{Ace of Spades}) = 1/52\). \(\P(A \cup S) = (4+13-1)/52 = 16/52 = 4/13\).

Solution to Ex 3: \(\E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^6 x \cdot \frac{1}{6} = 3.5\). \(\E[X^2] = \sum_{x=1}^6 x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{91}{6}\). \(\Var(X) = \frac{91}{6} - (3.5)^2 = 15.167 - 12.25 = \frac{35}{12} \approx 2.917\).
Solution to Ex 4: Yes, because \(\P(A)\P(B) = 0.5 \times 0.4 = 0.2 = \P(A \cap B)\).
Solution to Ex 6: \(f_X(x) = \int_0^1 (x+y) \dd y = [xy + \frac{1}{2}y^2]_0^1 = x + 0.5\).

Solution to Ex 8: \(\Var(S) = \E[\Var(S|N)] + \Var(\E[S|N])\). \(\E[S|N] = N\E[X]\), \(\Var(S|N) = N\Var(X)\). So \(\Var(S) = \E[N\Var(X)] + \Var(N\E[X]) = \lambda\Var(X) + \lambda(\E[X])^2 = \lambda \E[X^2]\).

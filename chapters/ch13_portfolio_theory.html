3. Intuition & Motivation

Imagine you have ₹10,000,000 (1 Crore INR) to invest. You could put it all into a high-growth tech stock, hoping for a 30% return, but risking a 50% loss if the sector crashes. Alternatively, you could leave it in a bank fixed deposit earning a safe 6%, but failing to outpace inflation significantly over the long run. The core dilemma of finance is the trade-off between risk and reward.

In 1952, Harry Markowitz formalized this trade-off, showing that an investor shouldn't just look at individual stocks, but how they move together. By holding a portfolio of assets that are not perfectly correlated, one can eliminate idiosyncratic risk. This insight—diversification—is famously described as "the only free lunch in finance." From Markowitz's foundational work sprang the CAPM, factor models, and modern asset allocation strategies used by trillions of dollars in global pension funds and sovereign wealth funds today.

4. Formal Theory

4.1 Returns

Definition 13.1: Asset Returns

Let \( P_t \) be the price of an asset at time \( t \). The simple (arithmetic) return over the period \( [t-1, t] \) is: \[ R_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1 \]

The log (continuously compounded) return is: \[ r_t = \ln\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) = \ln(1 + R_t) \]

Consequently, the relationship is \( R_t = e^{r_t} - 1 \).

Simple returns are additive across assets in a portfolio, while log returns are additive across time. For a multi-period return over \( k \) periods, the cumulative log return is simply \( r_{0,k} = \sum_{i=1}^k r_i \).

Annualization: If \( r \) is the average monthly return, the annualized return is \( 12 \times r \). For simple returns, the annualized return is \( (1+R)^{12} - 1 \).

4.2 Risk Measures

Risk is commonly quantified as the dispersion of returns.

  • Variance & Standard Deviation: \( \sigma^2 = \Var(R) \), \( \sigma = \sqrt{\Var(R)} \). Standard deviation is the most ubiquitous risk metric.
  • Semi-Variance & Downside Deviation: Considers only returns below a threshold \( B \) (often the mean or risk-free rate). \( \text{Semi-Variance} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \min(R_t - B, 0)^2 \).
  • Value at Risk (VaR): The maximum expected loss over a target horizon within a given confidence interval (e.g., 95% VaR). We will preview it here and expand upon it in risk management chapters.
  • Drawdown: The peak-to-trough decline during a specific record period, assessing the pain an investor endures before a new high is reached.

4.3 Markowitz Mean-Variance Optimization

Consider an economy with \( n \) risky assets. Let \( \mu \in \R^n \) be the vector of expected returns, and \( \Sigma \in \R^{n \times n} \) be the positive-definite covariance matrix of returns. A portfolio is defined by a weight vector \( w \in \R^n \), where \( \sum_{i=1}^n w_i = w'\mathbf{1} = 1 \).

The portfolio expected return is \( \mu_p = w'\mu \).

The portfolio variance is \( \sigma_p^2 = w'\Sigma w \).

Theorem 13.1: Minimum Variance Portfolio

The global minimum variance (GMV) portfolio minimizes \( \frac{1}{2} w'\Sigma w \) subject to \( w'\mathbf{1} = 1 \). The optimal weights are given by: \[ w_{GMV} = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \]

Derivation using Lagrange Multipliers

Define the Lagrangian: \( \mathcal{L}(w, \lambda) = \frac{1}{2} w'\Sigma w - \lambda (w'\mathbf{1} - 1) \).

First order conditions with respect to \( w \): \[ \pdv{\mathcal{L}}{w} = \Sigma w - \lambda \mathbf{1} = 0 \implies w = \lambda \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} \]

Substituting into the budget constraint \( w'\mathbf{1} = 1 \): \[ \lambda \mathbf{1}'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} = 1 \implies \lambda = \frac{1}{\mathbf{1}'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \]

Therefore, \( w = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \). \(\blacksquare\)

The Efficient Frontier: Minimizing variance for a target return \( \mu_p \) traces out the efficient frontier. The two-fund separation theorem states that any efficient portfolio can be constructed as a linear combination of two distinct efficient portfolios.

4.4 Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

Introducing a risk-free asset with return \( R_f \) transforms the efficient frontier into a straight line, the Capital Market Line (CML):

\[ \E[R_p] = R_f + \frac{\E[R_m] - R_f}{\sigma_m} \sigma_p \]

The Security Market Line (SML) expresses the expected return of an individual asset \( i \) in equilibrium:

\[ \E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i (\E[R_m] - R_f) \]

Where \( \beta_i = \frac{\Cov(R_i, R_m)}{\Var(R_m)} \) represents systematic risk. Total variance of asset \( i \) can be decomposed into systematic and idiosyncratic risk: \( \sigma_i^2 = \beta_i^2 \sigma_m^2 + \sigma_{\epsilon_i}^2 \). In a well-diversified portfolio, idiosyncratic risk is eliminated. Jensen's alpha (\( \alpha \)) is the excess return above the CAPM prediction: \( \alpha_i = R_i - [R_f + \beta_i(R_m - R_f)] \).

4.5 Arbitrage Pricing Theory (APT)

Stephen Ross (1976) proposed the APT, which assumes returns are generated by a multi-factor model:

\[ R_i = \alpha_i + \sum_{j=1}^k \beta_{ij}F_j + \epsilon_i \]

Under the no-arbitrage condition, the expected return is linearly related to the factor premiums \( \lambda_j \):

\[ \E[R_i] = R_f + \sum_{j=1}^k \beta_{ij}\lambda_j \]

Unlike CAPM, APT doesn't require a single unobservable "market portfolio" and allows for multiple sources of systematic risk.

4.6 Fama-French and Multi-Factor Models

Empirical failures of the CAPM led to the development of multi-factor models. The Fama-French 3-factor model is:

\[ R_{it} - R_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_{i,M}(R_{Mt} - R_{ft}) + \beta_{i,SMB}SMB_t + \beta_{i,HML}HML_t + \epsilon_{it} \]

  • SMB (Small Minus Big): Return spread of small cap vs large cap stocks (size premium).
  • HML (High Minus Low): Return spread of high book-to-market vs low book-to-market stocks (value premium).

Extensions include the Carhart 4-factor model (adds Momentum, WML) and the Fama-French 5-factor model (adds Profitability, RMW, and Investment, CMA).

4.7 Performance Ratios

Several metrics assess risk-adjusted performance:

  • Sharpe Ratio: \( S = \frac{\E[R_p - R_f]}{\sigma_p} \). Reward per unit of total risk.
  • Treynor Ratio: \( T = \frac{\E[R_p - R_f]}{\beta_p} \). Reward per unit of systematic risk.
  • Sortino Ratio: Uses downside deviation instead of standard deviation, penalizing only negative volatility.
  • Information Ratio (IR): \( IR = \frac{\alpha}{\sigma_{\text{tracking error}}} \). Active return per unit of active risk.
  • M² Measure: Risk-adjusted performance expressed in return percentage terms.
  • Calmar Ratio: Annualized return divided by maximum drawdown.

4.8 Black-Litterman Model

The standard mean-variance optimizer is notoriously sensitive to input parameters, often resulting in extreme weights. The Black-Litterman model solves this by combining market equilibrium implied returns (the prior) with subjective investor views to create a stable posterior estimate of returns.

Posterior return formula:

\[ \E[R] = [(\tau\Sigma)^{-1} + P'\Omega^{-1}P]^{-1}[(\tau\Sigma)^{-1}\Pi + P'\Omega^{-1}Q] \]

Where \( \Pi \) are implied equilibrium returns, \( P \) is the link matrix identifying assets in the views, \( Q \) are the view returns, \( \Omega \) is the uncertainty of the views, and \( \tau \) is a scaling scalar. This is fundamentally a Bayesian update.

4.9 Risk Parity

Rather than allocating capital, Risk Parity allocates risk. An Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) portfolio ensures each asset contributes equally to the total portfolio volatility. Based on Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions, the risk contribution of asset \( i \) is \( w_i \frac{\partial \sigma_p}{\partial w_i} \).

4.10 Kelly Criterion

For maximizing long-term geometric growth, the Kelly criterion determines the optimal bet size. For a simple binary bet, \( f^* = \frac{bp - q}{b} \), where \( b \) is the net odds received, \( p \) is probability of winning, and \( q=1-p \).

For continuous returns, the Kelly fraction is \( f^* = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\sigma^2} \). Because the full Kelly bet is extremely volatile, practitioners often use "fractional Kelly" (e.g., half-Kelly).

4.11 Utility Theory and Risk Aversion

Investors maximize Expected Utility \( \E[U(W)] \). The curvature of \( U \) defines risk preference.

  • Absolute Risk Aversion (ARA): \( A(W) = -\frac{U''(W)}{U'(W)} \)
  • Relative Risk Aversion (RRA): \( R(W) = -W \frac{U''(W)}{U'(W)} \)

Common utility functions include CRRA (Constant Relative Risk Aversion) and CARA (Constant Absolute Risk Aversion). The certainty equivalent is the guaranteed amount that yields the same utility as the risky gamble, and the risk premium is the expected return minus the certainty equivalent.

5. Worked Examples

Example 13.1: 3-Asset Markowitz Optimization

Consider three Indian stocks: Reliance (RELIANCE.NS), TCS (TCS.NS), and HDFC Bank (HDFCBANK.NS). Assume annualized expected returns \( \mu \) and covariance matrix \( \Sigma \):

\[ \mu = \begin{bmatrix} 0.15 \\ 0.12 \\ 0.18 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 0.04 & 0.015 & 0.02 \\ 0.015 & 0.03 & 0.01 \\ 0.02 & 0.01 & 0.05 \end{bmatrix} \]

Find the Global Minimum Variance (GMV) portfolio weights.

Solution:
We need \( w_{GMV} = \frac{\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1}} \).

First, invert \( \Sigma \). (Skipping manual matrix inversion steps, using computational result):

\[ \Sigma^{-1} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 35.7 & -13.6 & -11.6 \\ -13.6 & 42.5 & -2.7 \\ -11.6 & -2.7 & 24.3 \end{bmatrix} \]

Multiply by \( \mathbf{1} \):

\[ \Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} = \begin{bmatrix} 35.7 - 13.6 - 11.6 \\ -13.6 + 42.5 - 2.7 \\ -11.6 - 2.7 + 24.3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 10.5 \\ 26.2 \\ 10.0 \end{bmatrix} \]

Sum of elements \( \mathbf{1}'\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{1} = 10.5 + 26.2 + 10.0 = 46.7 \).

Weights \( w_{GMV} = \begin{bmatrix} 10.5/46.7 \\ 26.2/46.7 \\ 10.0/46.7 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.225 \\ 0.561 \\ 0.214 \end{bmatrix} \).

So, 22.5% in Reliance, 56.1% in TCS, and 21.4% in HDFC Bank.

Example 13.2: CAPM, Beta, and Alpha

An analyst observes the following for TechNova Inc.: Expected return is 14%, market expected return is 10%, risk-free rate is 4%. The stock's standard deviation is 30%, the market's standard deviation is 15%, and the correlation between the stock and the market is 0.6.

Calculate TechNova's Beta, the CAPM required return, and Jensen's Alpha.

Solution:
1. Beta: \( \beta = \frac{\Cov(R_i, R_m)}{\Var(R_m)} = \frac{\rho \sigma_i \sigma_m}{\sigma_m^2} = \rho \frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m} = 0.6 \times \frac{0.30}{0.15} = 0.6 \times 2 = 1.2 \).
2. CAPM Return: \( \E[R_i] = R_f + \beta(R_m - R_f) = 0.04 + 1.2(0.10 - 0.04) = 0.04 + 0.072 = 0.112 \) or 11.2%.
3. Jensen's Alpha: \( \alpha = \text{Actual Expected Return} - \text{CAPM Return} = 14\% - 11.2\% = 2.8\% \).

Example 13.3: Fama-French Regression

A mutual fund regression on the Fama-French 3-factor model yields:

\[ R_{fund} - R_f = 0.002 + 1.10(R_m - R_f) - 0.30(SMB) + 0.45(HML) + \epsilon \]

Interpret the coefficients.

Solution:
- \( \alpha = 0.002 \) (or 0.2% monthly if data is monthly): The fund generates a positive excess return of 0.2% not explained by the factors.
- Market Beta = 1.10: The fund is slightly more volatile than the market.
- SMB = -0.30: The fund has a large-cap bias (since the coefficient is negative on Small-Minus-Big).
- HML = 0.45: The fund has a value bias (positive loading on High-Minus-Low book-to-market).

Example 13.4: Black-Litterman Setup

Consider two assets, A and B. You have an absolute view that Asset A will return 5%, and a relative view that Asset A will outperform Asset B by 2%.

Construct the View matrix \( P \) and View Return vector \( Q \).

Solution:
Let weights be \( [w_A, w_B] \).
View 1 (Absolute): \( 1 \cdot R_A + 0 \cdot R_B = 0.05 \)
View 2 (Relative): \( 1 \cdot R_A - 1 \cdot R_B = 0.02 \)
Thus,
\[ P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0.05 \\ 0.02 \end{bmatrix} \]

Example 13.5: Kelly Criterion

An algorithmic trading strategy has an expected annualized return \( \mu = 20\% \) and annualized standard deviation \( \sigma = 25\% \). The risk-free rate is \( r_f = 2\% \). What is the optimal Kelly fraction?

Solution:
\[ f^* = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\sigma^2} = \frac{0.20 - 0.02}{0.25^2} = \frac{0.18}{0.0625} = 2.88 \]

The pure Kelly criterion suggests applying 2.88x leverage. In practice, due to parameter uncertainty and non-normal tails, a trader might use a "half-Kelly" (1.44x) or "quarter-Kelly" (0.72x) to mitigate bankruptcy risk.

6. Python Code

Here we implement the Markowitz mean-variance optimization to plot the Efficient Frontier and find the maximum Sharpe ratio portfolio.


import numpy as np
import scipy.optimize as sco

# Mock data for 4 assets
np.random.seed(42)
returns = np.random.normal(0.10, 0.20, 4)  # Expected returns
# Create a valid covariance matrix
A = np.random.randn(4, 4)
cov_matrix = np.dot(A, A.transpose()) * 0.01

risk_free_rate = 0.02

def portfolio_performance(weights, returns, cov_matrix):
    port_return = np.sum(returns * weights)
    port_volatility = np.sqrt(np.dot(weights.T, np.dot(cov_matrix, weights)))
    return port_return, port_volatility

def neg_sharpe_ratio(weights, returns, cov_matrix, risk_free_rate):
    p_ret, p_vol = portfolio_performance(weights, returns, cov_matrix)
    return -(p_ret - risk_free_rate) / p_vol

# Constraints and bounds
num_assets = len(returns)
args = (returns, cov_matrix, risk_free_rate)
constraints = ({'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda x: np.sum(x) - 1})
bounds = tuple((0.0, 1.0) for asset in range(num_assets)) # No short-selling

# Optimize
initial_guess = num_assets * [1. / num_assets]
opt_results = sco.minimize(neg_sharpe_ratio, initial_guess, args=args,
                           method='SLSQP', bounds=bounds, constraints=constraints)

print("Optimal Weights (Max Sharpe):", np.round(opt_results.x, 4))
p_ret, p_vol = portfolio_performance(opt_results.x, returns, cov_matrix)
print(f"Expected Return: {p_ret:.4f}")
print(f"Volatility: {p_vol:.4f}")
print(f"Sharpe Ratio: {(p_ret - risk_free_rate) / p_vol:.4f}")

Expected Output:

Optimal Weights (Max Sharpe): [0.0805 0.5841 0.3354 0.    ]
Expected Return: 0.1706
Volatility: 0.1098
Sharpe Ratio: 1.3719

8. Common Mistakes & Misconceptions

Warning: Pitfalls in Portfolio Optimization
  • Garbage In, Garbage Out: Mean-variance optimization is hypersensitive to expected return estimates. Small errors in \( \mu \) lead to wild, unintuitive portfolio allocations. Covariance estimates are generally more robust.
  • Assuming Normal Distributions: CAPM and standard VaR assume returns are normally distributed. Real financial returns exhibit "fat tails" (kurtosis) and skewness, leading to an underestimation of extreme downside risk.
  • Confusing the CML and SML: The CML uses total risk (standard deviation) and applies only to efficient portfolios. The SML uses systematic risk (Beta) and applies to individual assets and inefficient portfolios as well.

9. Industry Application Box

How Quant Funds use Factor Models

At major quantitative hedge funds and asset managers (like AQR or BlackRock), the Fama-French model is heavily generalized. Instead of just 3 or 5 factors, they might construct a StatArb (Statistical Arbitrage) risk model with 50+ factors, including momentum, liquidity, and alternative data factors.

They run regressions to estimate the covariance matrix \( \Sigma \). Because a full \( N \times N \) matrix for 5000 stocks has over 12 million unique entries (and is mostly noise), they use the factor model to structure the covariance matrix: \( \Sigma = B \Sigma_F B' + \Delta \), where \( B \) is the matrix of factor loadings, \( \Sigma_F \) is the factor covariance matrix, and \( \Delta \) is diagonal idiosyncratic risk. This dramatically reduces the dimensionality and noise, making optimization computationally stable.

10. Summary & Formula Cheat-Sheet

Cheat-Sheet

  • Returns: \( R_t = P_t/P_{t-1} - 1 \), \( r_t = \ln(P_t/P_{t-1}) \)
  • Portfolio Mean: \( \mu_p = w'\mu \)
  • Portfolio Variance: \( \sigma_p^2 = w'\Sigma w \)
  • CAPM SML: \( \E[R_i] = R_f + \beta_i (\E[R_m] - R_f) \)
  • Beta: \( \beta_i = \Cov(R_i, R_m)/\Var(R_m) \)
  • Sharpe Ratio: \( S = (\E[R_p] - R_f)/\sigma_p \)
  • Kelly Criterion (Continuous): \( f^* = (\mu - r_f)/\sigma^2 \)
